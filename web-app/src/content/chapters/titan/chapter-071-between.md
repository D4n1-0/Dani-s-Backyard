# Chapter 07.1 – Between

## Summary

Nothing dramatic happens. No second rendezvous. No Titan attack. No diplomatic breakthrough. The helicopters land and take off on schedule. The crates are inventoried and distributed. The treaty is being implemented clause by clause, and every clause is a negotiation. This chapter takes place in the gap between the big moments — the months where the relationship either hardens into something real or rots from the weight of a thousand small failures. [[Yelena]] places an order. [[Armin Arlert|Armin]] can't find a word. [[Hange Zoë|Hange]] signs forty documents before noon. [[Floch Forster|Floch]] speaks in a voice too low to carry. Nothing is wrong yet. Everything is just — between.

## Scenes

### Scene 1: Yelena — The Wine Audit

The restaurant was called the Grey Gull — a converted storefront near the Trost market square that had become, over the past two months, the most popular eating establishment in the city. Its owner was a Marleyan prisoner of war named [[Niccolo]], who had been a naval chef before his capture and had discovered, to his own bewilderment, that Paradisian military officers would pay good money for food that tasted like the outside world. The Grey Gull served seafood steamed in kelp broth, flatbreads baked with herbs from the Volunteers' garden, and a wine list that had grown from a single case of captured Marleyan vintage to a regular supply chain.

[[Yelena]] arrived at the Grey Gull fifteen minutes before the lunch service. She was not there to eat.

The kitchen door was unlocked. She stepped through it without knocking, her boots silent on the stone floor, her pale eyes adjusting to the dim light of the gas lanterns. [[Niccolo]] was at the prep table, filleting a fish with the precise, absent attention of a man who had done this ten thousand times. He did not look up.

"Miss [[Yelena]]. You are early. The lunch special is not ready."

"I am not here for lunch."

She set a folded paper on the prep table beside the cutting board. The paper was a manifest — a list of crate numbers, port arrival dates, and delivery addresses. The addresses were not restaurants.

"The shipment that arrived at the port on Tuesday," she said. "Twenty-two crates, marked as food supplies for the Volunteer compound. They were redirected to the Grey Gull's receiving dock. I need to confirm that the contents of crates nine through fourteen reached the correct tables."

[[Niccolo]] stopped filleting. He looked at the manifest. He looked at [[Yelena]]. In the months since the second rendezvous, his status on Paradis had shifted — from captured Marleyan sailor to tolerated curiosity to, improbably, something approaching a respected civilian. He had learned to read the moods of the people who ran the island. [[Yelena]]'s mood was a mood he had learned to recognize: the mood of a person who was executing a plan and would not tolerate questions.

"The wine," he said.

"Yes."

"The wine from the Volunteer compound. The cases with the blue seal."

"Yes."

"It goes to the tables you specified. The officers' tables. The table where Premier Zachary sat when he visited. The table where the Garrison commanders hold their weekly meeting. And — " he paused, " — the table where Commander [[Hange Zoë|Hange]] sits when she comes here to escape her office."

"And how often does Commander Hange escape her office?"

"Less often than she should. Once every ten days, maybe. She sits alone. She eats the fish soup. She doesn't talk to anyone. She pays with a note I'm not supposed to read."

[[Yelena]] absorbed this. Her expression did not change. "The wine at her table — does she drink it?"

"No. She orders water. She says coffee keeps her awake and wine makes her tired and she needs to be neither."

"Then the wine at her table is being consumed by someone else."

"The officers who accompany her. Captain [[Levi Ackerman|Levi]], when he comes. Corporal [[Armin Arlert|Armin]], when she brings him. Private [[Connie Springer|Springer]], when he's in Trost." [[Niccolo]] resumed filleting the fish, his knife steady. "The wine is being consumed. As ordered."

"Good." [[Yelena]] picked up the manifest, folded it, returned it to her coat. "Continue the arrangement. The same crates, the same tables, the same frequency. If anything changes — if an officer stops drinking wine, if a bottle is returned unopened, if anyone asks about the blue seal — send word to the Volunteer compound. You will know who to speak to."

"I understand."

[[Yelena]] turned to leave. At the kitchen door, she paused.

"Private [[Connie Springer|Springer]] — he drinks the wine?"

"Sometimes. Not always. He prefers beer. He says wine tastes like dirt someone set on fire."

A small smile — not warm, not cold, just precise. "Tell him it is an acquired taste."

She left. The kitchen door swung shut behind her. [[Niccolo]] continued filleting the fish, his knife moving in the same rhythm, his hands steady. He was a chef, not a conspirator. He did not know what the wine was for. He did not want to know. He had learned, during his years as a naval cook in the Marleyan fleet, that some cargoes were best not inspected. The crates with the blue seal were delivered to the tables he was told to deliver them to. The wine was consumed. The glasses were washed. The arrangement continued.

On the cutting board, the fish lay filleted and ready.

### Scene 2: Park — The Railway Dispute

The problem was sixty centimetres.

The railway spur was a twelve-kilometre extension from the eastern port to the agricultural district of Wall Rose — the same project that the CATF bulldozer had accelerated three months ago. The grading was nearly complete. The ties were being laid at a rate of two hundred metres per day. The track-laying gang — a mixed crew of forty Paradisian labourers and six CATF engineering technicians — had been working for three weeks without incident.

The incident, when it came, was about a retaining wall.

The Paradisian foreman was named Aldo — the same Aldo who had watched the Komatsu bulldozer push twelve tonnes of earth in a single pass and asked the Korean engineer to teach him to operate it. He had learned. He had gotten good. He was now the most experienced dirt-mover on the island, and he had a disagreement with a CATF surveyor named Sergeant Kwon about the alignment of a retaining wall at kilometre 7.3.

The disagreement had escalated. Escalated was a strong word — neither man had shouted, neither man had drawn a weapon — but the work had stopped, and forty workers were standing idle in the cold morning air, watching two men argue about sixty centimetres of vertical grade.

[[Park Sung-ho]] arrived at kilometre 7.3 by horse, because the liaison office's jeep had a fuel leak and the bicycle was being used by the translator. He dismounted, walked to the impasse, and assessed the situation the way he had assessed a hundred impasses in his career: by listening to everyone say their piece before he said anything.

Sergeant Kwon spoke first. He was twenty-eight, a Korean Army engineering technician with a crew-cut and the precise, methodical speech of a man who had been trained to build things correctly and had never been trained to compromise.

"Captain. The retaining wall at this grade requires a minimum of sixty centimetres of vertical clearance from the track bed for proper drainage. The foreman — " he gestured at Aldo, " — wants to reduce the clearance to twenty centimetres to save labour on the cut-and-fill. Twenty centimetres is insufficient. Rain will wash out the ballast within two seasons. The track will settle. The line will need to be relaid."

Aldo's Eldian was accented but clear. He had been a stonemason for thirty years before the railway existed, and he had built walls that had stood through Titan attacks and ground frost and everything else Paradis had thrown at them.

"Sergeant Kwon's calculation is correct for Earth," Aldo said. "Paradisan soil is different. Our clay does not drain the same way. Twenty centimetres is standard for our roads and has been for generations. We do not need sixty. We need twenty. Sergeant Kwon is applying a standard that was developed for a different world."

[[Park Sung-ho]] looked at the track bed. At the cut-and-fill slope. At the forty workers standing idle in the cold. He had been a liaison officer for three months. He had learned that most of his job was not about strategy or intelligence or diplomacy. It was about this: two competent men who were both right and both wrong, standing on a patch of dirt that belonged to neither of their worlds, waiting for a third person to tell them which way to go.

"We test it," he said. "We build a ten-metre section of retaining wall at each grade. Twenty centimetres and sixty centimetres. We fill the track bed above both sections. We bring in a water truck. We simulate two seasons of rain in a single afternoon. The section that drains better — that is the standard we use going forward."

Sergeant Kwon considered this. He had test protocols. He had engineering standards. He did not have a protocol for building two hundred metres of wall to test a frictional disagreement.

"How long will the test take?"

"Two days. One to build both sections. One to dry. The work continues on the rest of the line while we wait."

"And if the Paradisian standard fails the test?"

"Then Aldo will accept the Earth standard."

Aldo looked at the track bed. At the retaining wall. At the forty workers who had been standing idle for thirty minutes. "And if the Earth standard proves unnecessary?"

"Then Sergeant Kwon will accept the Paradisian standard."

Neither man was happy. But both men nodded. The work resumed — forty Paradisian labourers and six CATF technicians returning to the grade, the argument deferred to a test that would be conducted in the morning. [[Park Sung-ho]] walked back to his horse, pulled out his notebook, and wrote:

*Kilometre 7.3. Retaining wall grade dispute. Paradisian soil drainage differs from Earth standards. No existing protocol for cross- world engineering standards. Created ad hoc test framework. Recommend forming a joint engineering standards committee to avoid case-by-case resolution.*

He mounted the horse. As he rode back toward Trost, he passed the place where the Komatsu bulldozer had taught a Paradisian stonemason to drive heavy equipment — and thought about how far they had come, and how far they still had to go, and how the hardest thing about building a relationship was not the grand strategy but the sixty-centimetre arguments that no one had planned for.

### Scene 3: Armin — The Translation Wall

The book was open to page 174.

[[Armin Arlert]] had been reading for three hours. The sun had moved across the window of the liaison office's reading room — the room that [[Park Sung-ho|Captain Park]] had set aside for the books from the second rendezvous, the room where [[Armin Arlert|Armin]] spent every free hour that he was not on patrol, not in meetings, not sleeping. He was reading the history of science. He had been reading it for two months. He was still on page 174.

The problem was not the writing. The writing was clear — [[Elias Vogler|Vogler]]'s translation team had done exceptional work, converting the English original into an Eldian that was both accurate and readable. The problem was not the concepts. [[Armin Arlert|Armin]] understood the concepts — understood them with the intuitive grasp that had made him the Survey Corps' best strategist at sixteen years old. The problem was the words.

The word *hypothesis* did not exist in Eldian.

He had discovered this on page 23, when the text introduced the scientific method. The translator had rendered the concept as *a proposed explanation made with limited evidence as a starting point for further investigation* — a string of seventeen Eldian words for a concept that the English language expressed in three syllables. [[Armin Arlert|Armin]] had read the sentence four times before understanding it. He had spent the next two hours trying to invent a single word for the concept: *önsmoot*. It meant nothing yet. It was a placeholder. A jar he was holding open, waiting for meaning to fill it.

The word *falsification* did not exist. The word *peer review* did not exist. The word *evidence* existed — Paradisian law had a term for it — but it meant *testimony* rather than *physical data*, and the difference was not semantic. A courtroom accepted a witness's account as evidence. A laboratory accepted a measurement. The two concepts shared a word in Eldian, and the translator had not troubled to distinguish them.

[[Armin Arlert|Armin]] stared at page 174. The text was describing the development of germ theory — Pasteur's experiments, Koch's postulates, the slow, painstaking process of proving that invisible organisms caused disease. The translator had rendered the concept *controlled experiment* as *a test where the conditions are kept the same except for the thing being tested*. Fourteen Eldian words. A single jar that he had to hold open with both hands.

He wrote in his notebook:

*The problem is not that Paradis does not have science. The problem is that Paradis does not have the words for science. The words are the tools. Without the tools, you cannot build the knowledge. The books are not enough — we need a dictionary. A new dictionary. A dictionary that invents the words that Eldian has never needed because Eldian has never had science.*

He closed the book. He opened the atlas — the Earth atlas, the one with the maps of continents that were identical to his own world's geography and utterly different in their history. He traced the Korean Peninsula with his finger. The country that had invented the scientific instruments. The country that had invented the words.

"We cannot even name what we are missing," he said aloud. The room was empty. No one answered. "We do not have the word for a gap in our knowledge. How do we begin to fill a gap we cannot name?"

He opened the notebook. He wrote the word *önsmoot* again. And then, beneath it, a new word, for the gap itself: *fuhlun*. Meaning: *the space between what you know and what you need to know, which you cannot name because the language for it has not been invented yet.*

He had invented two words today. He would need thousands.

### Scene 4: Hange — Drowning in Administration

The clock on the wall said 1:47 AM. The clock was not broken. It was just accurate.

[[Hange Zoë]] was sitting at her desk in the Survey Corps headquarters in Trost — the same desk that had belonged to Erwin Smith, the same chair, the same view of the training yard through the same window. The yard was empty at this hour. The gas lamps flickered against the cobblestones. The Wall of Rose loomed in the darkness beyond the compound, fifty metres of sleeping Titans that she had not thought about in three days.

She had not thought about the Titans in three days. That was the measurement of her decline.

The paperwork was in three stacks. The left stack: treaty implementation documents requiring Commander-level approval — supply manifests, personnel transfers, requests for CATF equipment, schedules for joint training exercises, translated amendments that she had to initial in three places each. The centre stack: intelligence summaries from [[Tanaka Kenji|Tanaka]]'s section at Grande Terre — drone coverage of Marleyan fleet movements, acoustic analysis of Mid-East shipping lanes, a week-old report on the status of the captured Marleyan cruiser. The right stack: the personal correspondence that she had not answered in two weeks — a letter from the Eichenhof village doctor requesting more aspirin, a note from [[Frieda]] asking if she could visit the Titan containment pen for a study, a formal invitation to the Grey Gull's weekly officers' dinner that she had already declined three times.

She picked up the invitation. She read it. She put it down. She had not eaten dinner. She had not eaten lunch. She had eaten a piece of bread at breakfast while reading a translated treaty clause about iceburst stone extraction royalties, and she could not remember what the bread tasted like.

[[Levi Ackerman|Captain Levi]] appeared in the doorway. She had not heard him approach — he never made sound when he did not want to — but she felt his presence the way a fire felt a draft.

"You are still awake."

"I am still working."

"It is two in the morning."

"I am aware of the time. I have a clock."

"You have three stacks of paper that were two stacks of paper last night. You are making progress in the wrong direction."

[[Hange Zoë|Hange]] looked at the stacks. She looked at [[Levi Ackerman|Levi]]. He was standing in the doorway with his arms crossed, his expression unreadable, wearing the same jacket he had worn for three days. He had not slept either. But he was not the one who was drowning.

"I have not examined a Titan in six weeks," she said. Her voice was quiet. "Six weeks. I have a live Titan in a cage on Grande Terre. I have the most important biological specimen in the history of either world, and I have not looked at it in six weeks because I have been signing documents. The Commander has consumed the scientist. The Commander is eating the scientist from the inside, and I cannot stop it because the work needs doing, and no one else can do it, and the alternative is to let the treaty collapse, and if the treaty collapses — "

She stopped. The silence in the room was the silence of a woman who had just heard herself speak an insecurity she had not allowed herself to name.

[[Levi Ackerman|Levi]] did not say anything for a long moment. Then he walked into the room, picked up the right stack of papers — the personal correspondence, the invitation, the doctors' letters — and carried them to the fireplace.

"What are you doing?"

"Making one of your stacks smaller."

"The fireplace is not lit."

"I noticed." He set the papers on the mantelpiece. "You are going to answer those tomorrow. When you have slept. The letters from the village doctor — they can wait twelve hours. The invitation from the chef — he can wait forever. And the Titan in the cage — " he paused, " — it will still be there in the morning. You are no use to anyone if you burn out before the war starts."

She looked at him. He looked at her. They had known each other for too many years to need words for this.

"Who sent you?"

"No one. I noticed the light was on. The Commander is sleeping in her office again, I thought. I was right."

"Thank you, Levi."

"Don't thank me. Sleep. That is the thanks I want. A report in the morning that you slept in a bed."

She stood. The chair scraped against the stone floor. She walked past him toward the door, paused, looked back at the three stacks — now two stacks and a pile on the mantelpiece.

"Levi."

"What?"

"The scientist is not dead yet. She is just — resting."

"He can rest. He has been dead for two years. You are alive. Act like it."

She had no answer to that. She walked out into the dark corridor, toward her quarters, toward the bed she had not used in four nights. Behind her, [[Levi Ackerman|Levi]] stood in the doorway of the Commander's office, watching the empty yard through the window, not moving until the footsteps faded.

### Scene 5: Floch — Quiet Recruitment

The stable was behind the barracks — a long, low building of stone and timber, warm with the breath of horses and the smell of hay and leather. Three young soldiers sat on overturned feed buckets, their Survey Corps cloaks folded beside them, their faces lit by a single oil lamp that [[Floch Forster]] had placed on a wooden crate.

They had come because he had asked them to. They had not told anyone they were coming. That was the first test, and they had passed it.

[[Floch Forster|Floch]] stood across from them, his arms uncrossed for once, his voice low and even. He was not shouting. He was not rallying. He was speaking the way a man spoke when he wanted to be heard by the people in front of him and no one else.

"We have been fighting for six years," he said. "The Training Corps. The Battle of Trost. The 57th Expedition. Shiganshina. The rendezvous with the people from another world. Six years of fighting, and every time we ask what we are fighting for, we get a different answer."

The youngest of the three — a girl with brown hair and the posture of someone who had not yet learned to relax — spoke. "We are fighting to survive. That is the answer Commander Hange gives."

"That used to be the answer," [[Floch Forster|Floch]] said. "When the Titans were the only enemy. When the world was just the Walls. When the question was: do we live or do we die? That was a question that had a simple answer. Live. Fight to live. But the world is not simple anymore. The world is full of nations that want us dead. The world has a fleet. The world has artillery. The world has a warrior unit. And the world has — " he paused, " — friends. The people from Earth. The people with the flying machines. The people who bring us chocolate and books and medicine for pain."

The girl's expression flickered. "The medicine is good. My cousin was wounded in the patrol last month. The morphine saved his leg."

"The morphine is good," [[Floch Forster|Floch]] agreed. "The chocolate is good. The books are good. The question is: what happens when the chocolate runs out? What happens when the people from Earth decide the trade is not worth it? What happens when Marley offers them a better deal? What happens when the gifts become a leash?"

The second soldier — a young man with a fading bruise on his cheek from a sparring session — leaned forward. "You think they are going to betray us."

"I think they are going to do what every outside power has done to Eldians for two thousand years. They are going to use us and then discard us. Not because they are evil. Because we are useful and we are weak. They are not the first people to arrive on Paradis with gifts and promises. The Eldian Empire arrived with gifts and promises. Marley arrived with gifts and promises. The Volunteers arrived with gifts and promises. And every time, the gifts became chains."

The third soldier — a boy, sixteen, who had joined the Survey Corps after Shiganshina because he wanted to be a hero — said: "What do we do about it?"

[[Floch Forster|Floch]] looked at him. The boy's eyes were earnest. He had not learned yet. He would learn.

"We become strong enough that the gifts do not matter," [[Floch Forster|Floch]] said. "We become strong enough that the people from Earth need us more than we need them. We become strong enough to choose our own future — not the future that Hange chooses, not the future that the Queen chooses, not the future that the CATF chooses. Our own future. The future that we earn with our own hands and pay for with our own blood."

The stable was silent. The horses breathed. The lamp flickered.

"Who will give us that future?" the girl asked. "Who is strong enough to lead us there?"

[[Floch Forster|Floch]] did not answer immediately. He looked at the three young faces in the lamplight — the girl who had seen her cousin saved by alien medicine, the young man who had been bruised in training, the boy who still believed in heroes. He thought about [[Eren Yeager|Eren]]. About the look in Eren's eyes on the dock, when the crates were being unloaded and he had watched his people's salvation arrive in packaging from another world. He thought about the fact that he had not heard [[Eren Yeager|Eren]] speak a full sentence in three weeks.

No, he thought. That is not true. The truth is that he has not spoken a full sentence to anyone. And the truth is that when a weapon does not speak, it is because it is aiming.

"Someone who has been silent for too long," he said. "Someone who understands that peace is a trap and patience is a form of surrender. Someone who has seen enough to know that the only thing the world respects is strength. We will find that person. Or we will become that person."

He blew out the lamp. The stable went dark.

"Go back to your barracks. Do not speak of this conversation. If anyone asks where you were, you were checking the horses. Do not meet again until I tell you. When the time comes, you will know."

The three soldiers stood in the dark. They filed out of the stable. [[Floch Forster|Floch]] followed last, closing the door behind him, standing alone in the cold night air with the smell of hay and horses and the future.

Nothing had happened tonight. No crime had been committed. No conspiracy had been formed. Three soldiers had listened to a man speak in a stable. That was all. That was enough.

He walked toward the barracks, his silhouette small against the Wall, and behind him the stable door stood closed, and the moon was covered by cloud, and the night was silent in the way that nights were silent before a storm.
