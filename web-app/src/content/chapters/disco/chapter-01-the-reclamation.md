# Chapter 01 – The Reclamation

## Summary

Lukas Delacroix arrives in Martinaise with [[Joyce Messier]] on the morning of the Coalition reclamation. The dock terminal is retaken by [[INSURCOM]] forces under [[Henrik Varga|Commander Varga]], the strike is broken, and the [[Hardie Boys]] — already dead from the [[The Tribunal|Tribunal]] four days prior — can offer no resistance. Lukas walks the aftermath: the empty terminal, the hollowed [[The Whirling-in-Rags|Whirling-in-Rags]], the gate where [[Measurehead]] clashes with Coalition soldiers. He meets [[Evrart Claire]], still untouchable. He meets [[Garte]], frayed and bitter. He meets [[Mañana]], impossible to disturb. At the fishing village, [[Tommy Le Homme]] watches him with quiet suspicion. By evening, Lukas stands alone in the conquered terminal, schematics in hand — a twenty-five-year-old with a dead man's leverage over him, about to build a cage he cannot name.

## Scenes

### Scene 1: The Boat — Martinaise Fishing Village, Dawn

The fog over the bay was the colour of old dishwater, and it clung to the water like a guilty man to a confession.

Lukas Delacroix stood at the bow of the company launch, one hand gripping the rail, the other shoved deep into the pocket of his overcoat — a coat too thin for the hour, too light for a man who was about to be seen. The engine thrummed through the deck plates, a vibration he felt in his teeth. Behind him, under the canopy, [[Joyce Messier]] sat with her ankles crossed and a cigarette burning down toward her knuckles, reading a memorandum. She had not spoken since they left the dry dock in Grand Couron. The silence was deliberate. Every silence with Joyce was.

Across the water, Martinaise resolved itself out of the grey: dock cranes, corrugated roofs, the smudged geometry of tenement blocks. Scattered lights. A Coalition patrol boat cut across the bay at an angle, its wake a white scar. Lukas watched it pass and thought about the word *reclamation*. A clinical word. The word you use when you want people to forget you are taking something back by force.

"The commander's name is Varga," Joyce said from behind him, not looking up from her papers. Her accent carried the polish of the Moralintern's executive tier — Revacholian French overlaid with the cold precision of Sur-la-Clef. "INSURCOM. Career infantry, two tours in the Samaran Isolas. He finds labour disputes tedious. Do not waste his time with your opinions, Lukas."

"I don't have any," Lukas said.

"Good." The cigarette tilted between her fingers. "That's why I brought you."

The boat bumped the jetty. A young soldier caught the line. Lukas stepped off first — automatically, habitually, the same way he opened doors for her, pulled out chairs, held her coat. Small performances. He had learned them at university, and she had drilled them into him in the years since — three years of small obediences layered until they felt like choice. Every gesture a calibration.

Commander Henrik Varga was waiting at the top of the concrete ramp, flanked by two lieutenants and a portable radio set crackling with static. He was mid-forties, thick through the shoulders but soft around the jaw, with the tired eyes of a man who had stopped distinguishing between policing and soldiering two occupations ago. His uniform was correct but not crisp — worn collar, scuffed boots, the rank insignia slightly faded. A career officer. No ideology. No fire.

"Ms. Messier." He extended a hand. Joyce took it with the precise degree of warmth that communicated *I pay your government's bills* without having to say it.

"Commander. I trust the operation went smoothly."

"Quickly," Varga said. "Smoothly is another matter. The dockworkers had been preparing for a fight, but without the Hardie Boys — well. You heard what happened."

"We heard." Joyce's voice betrayed nothing. "The Tribunal was unfortunate."

Varga's expression did not change, but something in his posture slackened — a man relieved to discover his client was not going to pretend to care about dead dockworkers. "The terminal is secured. Six arrests. The rest dispersed. No Coalition casualties. One striker wounded in the shoulder — he fired first."

"And the mercenaries?" Joyce asked.

"Bodies were removed yesterday. Krenel's people. The RCM filed their reports."

Lukas looked past the commander, toward the terminal visible through the thinning fog — a long dark building with a broken window line, its loading doors sealed, its perimeter patrolled by soldiers in Coalition grey. Four days ago men had been killed here. Four days ago the Hardie boys had faced Krenel's mercenaries outside the Whirling, and none of them had walked away. Now the terminal was quiet. A quiet that cost money.

Varga glanced at Lukas. A flicker of assessment — boy, corporate, nervous — and dismissed him. "You'll want to inspect the site. I'll assign an escort."

"Mr. Delacroix will handle the inspection," Joyce said. "He is leading our security installation."

Varga looked at Lukas again. Longer this time. Recalculating. "The kid?"

"The *engineer*," Joyce corrected, with the faintest emphasis. "From Koenigstein. He designed the system."

Varga shrugged — the shrug of a man who had seen stranger things in uniform. "Your dime. The terminal's yours as of oh-seven-hundred. Union presence is restricted to the office building across the square. My men hold the perimeter until sixteen-hundred, then it's your problem."

"What about the RCM?" Lukas asked.

The commander turned. He seemed almost surprised Lukas could speak.

"Back in Jamrock," Varga said. "The two from the murder investigation — Du Bois and Kitsuragi — they filed their final report yesterday. Case closed. Officially, the RCM has no further interest in Martinaise. Unofficially…" He let the sentence hang. "They're the ones who found the real killer. The lorry driver, which would have been the most embarrassing coincidence for the Moralintern. But the mercenaries were still here, the strike was still on, and the Tribunal still happened. The law is a process. Processes have their own momentum. You understand."

He understood. They all understood.

Joyce crushed her cigarette under a heel. "Shall we?"

### Scene 2: The Terminal — Martinaise Dock Terminal

The terminal was a cathedral of industry gone quiet. Loading bays gaped open, their doors retracted like eyelids frozen mid-blink. Pallets stood stacked in towers, freight still nested in strapping — nothing had moved in weeks. The air inside was cold and still and tasted of rust and dead diesel. Somewhere a pigeon was cooing in the rafters, a sound so incongruous it felt like mockery.

Lukas walked the length of the main floor, his footsteps echoing up into the darkness above. Behind him, two Coalition soldiers trailed at a respectful distance — whether protecting him or containing him was unclear. Maybe both. Maybe there was no difference anymore.

Graffiti covered the walls. Most of it was strike slogans — ALL WORKERS ARE BOARD, EVERY TERMINAL IS A FORTRESS, CLAIRE BROTHERHOOD — but there were older layers beneath it, faded paint and scored concrete, messages from previous disputes, previous occupations, previous resets of the long grinding cycle. Lukas stopped in front of a spray-painted fist clutching a gear, the icon of the [[Dockworkers' Union]], and felt something inside him turn cold and still.

He took out his notebook. He marked locations: *camera position A, east loading bay. Camera position B, main entrance. Control room, old supervisor's office.* The system he had designed was elegant, in its way — full camera coverage, automated door control, failsafe locks that could seal any section of the terminal in under eight seconds. Koenigstein had taught him the engineering. Joyce had taught him what to do with it.

*Prevent another strike.* That was the brief. The specification was more precise: make occupation permanent without looking like occupation. Cameras, not guns. Doors, not soldiers. A cage made of images and access codes, cleaner than occupation, cheaper than war.

Lukas stood in the centre of the floor and turned in a slow circle, mapping coverage zones in his head. A man could build something beautiful from the inside. A man could also look at his own design and see the prison it was.

He folded the notebook shut.

### Scene 3: The Whirling-in-Rags — Martinaise Commercial District

The Whirling-in-Rags had seen better days, which was saying something, because its better days had not been good. The inn stood three storeys of peeling plaster and salt-eaten brick, its sign hanging at an angle that suggested the building itself was drunk. The windows on the ground floor had been boarded up — Tribunal damage, still not repaired. Bullet holes stitched the plaster above the doorframe. A smear of something dark on the step had been scrubbed but not entirely removed.

Inside, the air was thick with the ghosts of old cigarette smoke and industrial solvent. The cafeteria was empty. The pawnshop kiosk was shuttered. Behind the counter, [[Garte]] — full name Garte Theodorus Olivier, which he had once been proud of — was wiping the same glass he had been wiping for twenty years, his knuckles white, his mouth a thin line of permanent grievance.

"We're open," he said, without looking up. "Technically."

Lukas approached the counter. "Lukas Delacroix. Wild Pines."

Garte finally looked at him. His expression cycled through several emotions — anger, exhaustion, the reflexive hospitality of a man whose livelihood depended on people liking him — and settled on a kind of weary contempt. "Of course you are. The occupation sends its salesmen. We had detectives before you. Two of them. Sat in that room for a week." He jerked his head toward the hallway. "Room one. The amnesiac and the binoclard. They solved a murder. Did you know that? A murder, solved, here. And look at the place now."

"I know," Lukas said.

"Do you? Because I had to scrub blood off the floorboards. Union men. Mercenaries. My establishment." He set the glass down with a small, precise *click*. "What does Wild Pines want now?"

"Nothing from you specifically," Lukas said. "I'm surveying."

"Surveying." Garte tasted the word like a bad oyster. "That's what they called it in the Thirties, too. Before the Commune. Surveying."

At the corner table, a man in a threadbare coat had not moved since Lukas entered. He was staring at the far wall with the serene blankness of a man who had made peace with the void. An empty glass sat in front of him. A fishing magazine, unopened. Time had no purchase on this man.

"[[Mañana]]," Garte said, following Lukas's gaze. "Dockworker. Or was. You'd have to ask him. He hasn't said more than three words in a row since the shootings. Come to think of it, he didn't say more than three words before them either. So."

Lukas looked at Mañana. Mañana did not look back. The man existed in a state of profound and enviable quietude — a human pause button. Lukas wondered what it would take to disturb him. Likely nothing. Likely that was the point.

### Scene 4: The Gates — Martinaise Entrance

The gates leading into Martinaise proper were a relic of the Revolution — iron scrollwork, rust-eaten, flanked by crumbling pillars that had once been painted red. A Coalition truck was parked across the entrance, its engine idling, a checkpoint established. Three soldiers stood at ease beside it. The gate was secured. The standoff, if there had been one, was over.

[[Measurehead]] sat on the ground with his back against the gate pillar, his wrists cuffed behind him, a Coalition soldier standing over him with the bored expression of a man waiting for a transport vehicle. He was massive — not fat, not muscled, simply *large*, as if someone had taken a normal man and enlarged him by thirty percent in every dimension, including his opinions. And those opinions were still flowing.

"—shaped by the intermingling of the proto-Semine sub-stratum and the paleographically documented nomadic incursions of the pre-Insulindic era, which any serious student of *racial* —"

"Sir," the soldier above him said, not for the first time, "I am asking you to stop talking."

"—science would immediately recognise as the *foundational* —"

"Sir. You are under arrest. You have the right to remain silent."

"—*corpus* of advanced racial theory, as set forth in the *Codex* —"

"Evidently not the right to exercise it," the soldier muttered.

Lukas watched from across the square. The soldiers had done what soldiers did: secured the position, detained the obstruction, and were now suffering the consequences of an ideological monologue that would outlast their shift and possibly their careers. Measurehead was not a threat. He was a nuisance with a bibliography, and the Coalition was processing him the way it processed everything — slowly, by the book, with mounting paperwork.

The revolution had failed to resolve him. The Occupation had failed to resolve him. The strike had risen and fallen without resolving him. And now he sat cuffed against a pillar, lecturing armed men about advanced racial theory, and the world continued to refuse to end on his terms.

Lukas walked on. The monologue continued behind him. The gate was open.

### Scene 5: The Union Office — Martinaise

The [[Dockworkers' Union Office]] occupied the second floor of a building that had been a shipping company's headquarters before the Commune, a hospital during the Commune, and an administrative annex under the early Occupation. Now it was the seat of power in Martinaise, or what passed for it — a fact that said less about the office and more about Martinaise.

[[Evrart Claire]] was seated behind a desk that had been sized for a man half his mass, which made him appear to be wearing it rather than sitting at it. He was formidable in all directions, a monument to the thermodynamic principle that power accumulates. His brother Edgar was not present — off in some other district, tending to some other piece of the machine. Evrart did not need him. Evrart did not need anyone.

"Mr. Delacroix!" He beamed. The beam was genuine, which was the disturbing thing. Evrart Claire was never pretending. Sincerity was his weapon, and he wielded it with the precision of a scalpel. "Joyce's new man. I heard you were coming. Please, sit. Are you hungry? We have ham sandwiches. The ham is very good. My brother procures it."

Lukas did not sit. "I'm inspecting the terminal."

"Naturally, naturally. Inspect away. It's your terminal now, after all. The Coalition saw to that." The smile did not waver, but something behind it shifted — a door closing in a room you didn't know was there. "The Hardie Boys are dead, you know. Titus. Theo. Glen. All of them. Did you hear about that?"

"Yes."

"Shot by Wild Pines mercenaries. The same Wild Pines you work for. I'm not blaming you, of course. You weren't there. But the men who did it were wearing your company's colours. And now the terminal is yours again, and the strike demands have been dismissed, and the Coalition is patrolling our streets, and Titus Hardie is in a box. It's quite a sequence of events. Almost mathematical, the way it resolves."

Lukas said nothing. Evrart was not looking for a response. He was building one.

"You seem like a smart young man, Mr. Delacroix. Koenigstein, I'm told. Impressive. Joyce doesn't hire stupid people. So here is my advice, and I give it freely, as a gesture of goodwill between neighbours: whatever you're about to install in my terminal — your cameras, your automatic doors — understand that we have been here longer than you. Longer than Wild Pines. Longer than the Coalition, for that matter. The institutions change. The bay does not." He leaned back. The chair groaned. "We'll be having lunch again, I'm sure. Do think about the ham."

### Scene 6: The Fishing Village — Afternoon

By afternoon the fog had burned off, and the bay lay flat and silver under a sky the colour of old pewter. Lukas walked down to the fishing village — a clutter of shacks and drying racks and boats pulled up on the shingle like sleeping animals. The tide was out. The air smelled of brine and diesel and the faint sourness of fish too long in the sun.

[[Tommy Le Homme]] was mending a net on the steps of the village chapel, a squat stone building with a wooden cross that listed slightly to the left, as if even God had trouble keeping straight in Martinaise. Tommy was a lean man in his fifties, with hands that moved through the netting with the muscle-memory of decades, and eyes that tracked Lukas from the moment he appeared on the shore road.

"You're the new one," Tommy said. Not a question.

"Lukas Delacroix. Wild Pines."

"I know what company you work for, Mr. Delacroix. The boat this morning was flying your colours." The net mesmered through his fingers. "Joyce Messier's boat. The woman who told us our demands were unreasonable while her mercenaries were loading ammunition. A woman of many talents."

Lukas did not answer. He had learned, in the minutes since arriving in Martinaise, that answering was usually the mistake.

"You're here for the cameras," Tommy continued. "The wires. The machines that watch. I heard the soldiers talking. Is it true you can lock every door in the terminal from a single switch?"

"It's a security system," Lukas said.

"It's a leash." Tommy looked up from the net. His eyes were grey, the grey of the bay before sunrise, and they held Lukas's gaze without effort. "I was a dockworker for twenty-two years, Mr. Delacroix. Then I fished. You know why I switched? Because the docks killed men. Not the work — the ownership. The demands. The strikes and the counter-strikes, the mercenaries and the soldiers, the lockouts and the layoffs and the bodies that turned up in the bay every spring when the ice melted. I switched to fishing so I could die of something ordinary." He returned to the net. "And now you're bringing the docks back. With locks."

Lukas stood there for a long moment. The chapel door was open behind Tommy. Inside, a handful of candles burned — votives, maybe, or just the village's way of keeping warm. The light flickered against the stone.

"The strike is over," Lukas said. "I'm not here to fight anyone."

"No," Tommy said, without looking up. "You're not. You're here to win."

### Scene 7: The Terminal — Night

The soldiers were gone by nightfall. The perimeter was unmanned. The terminal sat in the darkness like a ship abandoned at anchor, its windows dark, its doors sealed, its silence total.

Lukas stood alone in the main bay, a portable arc lamp casting a cone of white light across the floor. He had his schematics spread across a crate — wiring diagrams, camera placement, door actuator wiring, the control room layout. It was good work. Good engineering. Koenigstein had taught him to see systems: inputs and outputs, loops and fail-safes, the elegant mathematics of control.

*Prevent another strike.* The words had been Joyce's, but the design was his. He had drawn it on a chalkboard in her Grand Couron apartment, late at night — one of a hundred nights like it across three years — while she watched from the bed with a glass of wine and the expression of a woman watching an investment mature. He remembered the way she had looked at the diagram: not at him, at the *work*. The same way she had looked at him the first time he presented his internship report. The same way she had looked at him on the nights that followed. In the moment it had felt like pride. Later he understood it was something more like appraisal.

The Polaroids were never spoken of. They did not need to be. After every session — her apartment in Grand Couron, a hotel room in Jamrock, once in the back of a company car — she would reach for the camera on the nightstand before he had caught his breath. The click of the shutter, the whir of the print ejecting, the slow development of his image from grey to colour. She would set it aside with the others. He had never seen the full collection. He knew it existed. That was enough.

She had never threatened him with them directly. She did not need to. The ritual itself was the threat — a Polaroid taken every time, a record that could only ever grow. He worked for her because he was brilliant. He worked for her *at twenty-five* because she had made sure of it. The two facts were not incompatible. They were the same fact, viewed from different angles.

Lukas looked at the schematic and saw a cage. The same cage he lived in, scaled up to industrial dimensions. Cameras. Locks. The architecture of suspicion made concrete and steel and code. He was building, for the dockworkers of Martinaise, the same structure Joyce had built around him.

He could refuse. He could walk. He could take the consequences — the folder, the photos, the ruin of a career that was barely his. He could do what no one in Martinaise seemed capable of doing: simply stop.

He didn't.

He picked up his pencil and continued marking positions. The arc lamp hummed. Outside, the bay was black and silent. Somewhere in Jamrock, a detective with no memory was sleeping off the case that had brought him here. Somewhere in Grand Couron, Joyce Messier was reviewing the day's reports. Somewhere in the fishing village, Tommy Le Homme was mending his nets in the dark.

And in the empty terminal, a twenty-five year-old boy with a brilliant mind and a woman's collar around his throat was drawing a floor plan for a prison he was being paid to build, and which he would one day have to decide whether to tear down.
