# Chapter 04 – The Unsettling Question

## Summary

Prime Minister [[Stephen Davenport]] convenes an emergency COBR meeting deep beneath Whitehall as intelligence reports from the Faithful delegation accumulate and the theological implications of [[Rebecca Osei|Osei]]'s crossing begin to crystallise. [[William Ashford|Foreign Secretary Ashford]] argues for cautious diplomacy — treat the [[Crown of England|Faithful Crown]] as a foreign power and nothing more. [[Michael Grantham|Defence Secretary Grantham]] seizes on the [[Heretic Legion]] briefing to demand military contingency planning. [[Alex Merrick|General Sir Alex Merrick]] cuts through ideology with blunt operational realities, insisting on a clear chain of command for whatever follows. [[Hugh Renwick|Sir Hugh Renwick]] warns of the domestic dimension — Faithful theology spreading through a spiritually unvaccinated public like a pathogen. [[Stephen Davenport|Davenport]], constrained by the unresolved domestic crisis that installed his National Unity Government, sets a course of cautious secular engagement. But the real reckoning comes after the meeting disperses — in a private exchange with [[Peter Chisholm|Archbishop Chisholm]], where the most unsettling question of all is planted: what if the Faithful are not wrong? What if they are merely mistaken about what it means?

## Scenes

### Scene 1: [[Cabinet Office Briefing Rooms]] — Assembly

**[TIME]: Day 4 — 2026-06-21, 08:00 Zulu**
**[POV]: Third Person — [[Stephen Davenport|Davenport]]*

The briefing room under Whitehall had been designed for nuclear war.

Below the pavement of the Cabinet Office, past the unmarked doors and the silent security detail, down a flight of stairs that had been reinforced against blast and then reinforced again against the smaller, quieter terrors of the modern age — cyber, chemical, biological — there was a room that had existed since the Cold War and had outlived it. The walls were soundproofed. The air was filtered. A single long table of dark wood dominated the centre, ringed by leather chairs that had held the weight of Prime Ministers, Chiefs of Staff, and intelligence heads through crises that were now footnotes in classified files. A row of encrypted screens lined one wall. A portrait of the King hung opposite — the same Windsor face, [[Stephen Davenport|Davenport]] reflected, that [[Alistair Pembroke|Pembroke]] and [[Caius Blackwood|Blackwood]] would recognise from their own throne room, separated by a century and a universe.

The Cabinet Office Briefing Rooms — COBR, or COBRA as the press called it — had never been convened for this.

At half past seven, the room was still filling. The early arrivals clustered near the coffee urn — a cheap aluminium thing that had seen every prime minister since Thatcher — and spoke in the low, careful tones of people who had read the overnight briefings and were still processing what they meant.

[[Stephen Davenport|Davenport]] stood at the head of the table, a thin man in his late fifties with the face of a permanent acting-headmaster. His hair was grey and receding. His glasses were the kind that fogged in the Tube. He had been Prime Minister for fourteen months — fourteen months of a National Unity Government that had been stitched together after the constitutional crisis of the previous year, a coalition of old enemies who had agreed to share power only because the alternative was worse. The domestic wounds that had forced the coalition still bled. The economy was stabilising but brittle. The opposition watched from the wings, waiting. And now this.

He watched his ministers file in. [[William Ashford|William Ashford]], Foreign Secretary, settled into his place on [[Stephen Davenport|Davenport]]'s right with the practised ease of a man who had spent thirty years in the diplomatic corps before politics claimed him — composed, immaculate, the sort of man who believed that every problem could be solved with the right communiqué and the correct channel of communication. Across the table, [[Michael Grantham]] — Defence Secretary, former Army colonel, a man built from scar tissue and short sentences — was already annotating the threat assessment with a red pen, his jaw set in the particular angle that meant he had found something he didn't like. [[Alex Merrick|General Sir Alex Merrick]], Chief of the Defence Staff, sat stiff-backed at the Defence Secretary's shoulder, a career soldier who had commanded in Afghanistan and Iraq and had the thousand-yard stare of a man who had learned, through long and bitter experience, that the first casualty of every brief was clarity. [[Hugh Renwick|Sir Hugh Renwick]] — Director General of MI5, but representing the Joint Intelligence Committee today — placed a slim black folder on the table and did not open it. His face betrayed nothing. His face always betrayed nothing.

The Home Secretary and the Chancellor sat further down the table, flanked by aides and officials and the quiet machinery of government — note-takers, analysts, a representative from the Cabinet Office secretariat who would later argue about the minutes. The chairs filled. The door sealed with the soft click of a magnetic lock.

[[Stephen Davenport|Davenport]] did not wait for silence. He produced it.

"Ladies and gentlemen. This is not a drill. This is not a simulation. This is not a scenario any of us were trained for." He placed his hands flat on the table. "Three days ago, a portal opened in the Celtic Sea connecting our world to another — a parallel Earth where Hell is a territorial power, God intervenes visibly in military affairs, and the Crown of England has been fighting an eight-hundred-year holy war against what its own clergy describe as the literal forces of damnation. An armed delegation from that world is currently being hosted on *[[HMS Dragon]]* and at [[Northwood Headquarters|Northwood]]. One of our officers — Lieutenant Commander [[Rebecca Osei]] — crossed into their world yesterday and has not yet returned, though her preliminary signal traffic indicates she has been received, alive and unharmed, by their chain of command. And the theological assessment from the Faithful delegation, compiled by our own chaplaincy and intelligence services, suggests that the senior priest of their expedition — one [[Father Beauchamp]] — has concluded that our world is, and I quote, 'an ark.' A vessel preserved by God to survive the flood of their apocalypse."

He paused. The room was so quiet that he could hear the ventilation system cycling.

"Everything we discuss today is classified above Top Secret. There is no press line. There is no public affairs strategy. There is only the question of what we do next. And we will not leave this room until we have an answer."

He turned to [[Hugh Renwick|Renwick]]. "Director, the intelligence picture. What do we actually know?"

### Scene 2: [[Cabinet Office Briefing Rooms]] — The Briefing

**[TIME]: Day 4 — 2026-06-21, 08:15 Zulu**

[[Hugh Renwick|Sir Hugh Renwick]] opened the black folder without ceremony. He was a grey man — grey suit, grey hair, grey voice — but his words cut.

"Prime Minister, the Faithful delegation consists of twelve individuals: six scientists attached to their Holy Science Academy, four priests, and two military observers. They crossed into our custody two days ago aboard a vessel they designate HMS *Herald*. They are currently quartered at Northwood under joint Royal Navy and MI5 supervision. They have been cooperative. They have been forthcoming. The problem is not what they are hiding. The problem is what they are telling us."

He removed a single sheet from the folder.

[[Hugh Renwick|Renwick]] continued: "Their chaplain — Father Beauchamp — claims to have performed a Rite of Discernment on Lieutenant Commander [[Rebecca Osei|Osei]]. He describes her soul as, and I quote, 'a blank page.' No taint. No grace. Neither marked by God nor by the Adversary — a condition that, according to their theology, should not be possible. Every soul in their world bears one mark or the other. Ours do not. To them, this is not a curiosity. It is a sign."

"What kind of sign?" [[William Ashford|Ashford]] asked.

[[Hugh Renwick|Renwick]]'s expression did not change. "Father Beauchamp has reportedly formulated a theological hypothesis — call it the 'ark' doctrine — which holds that modern Earth was deliberately hidden by the Creator at the moment of their world's divergence in 1099. Our world was sealed away from the Adversary to preserve a remnant of uncorrupted faith. A seed bank of souls. An ark."

He let the word settle. "I should add that this hypothesis has not — yet — been formally adopted by the Faithful command. But the delegation's senior military officer, Captain [[Alistair Pembroke|Pembroke]], has recorded it in his despatches. And the Faithful Crown — King Robert — has issued a royal decree declaring our world 'an instrument of Providence.' The Synod of their Unified Church concurred."

[[Michael Grantham]] put his red pen down with a click that sounded like a round being chambered. "So we are not a foreign power to them. We are a holy relic."

"Possibly," [[Hugh Renwick|Renwick]] said. "Or possibly we are a strategic asset. The distinction may not matter to them."

[[William Ashford|Ashford]] leaned forward. "The distinction matters enormously. If they believe we are a holy relic, they will treat our territory as sacred ground. That imposes obligations. That justifies interference. If they believe we are a strategic asset, they will want access — military access, resource access. Either way, we need to understand what they want before we offer anything."

"They have not asked for anything yet," [[Hugh Renwick|Renwick]] said. "They have been almost painfully correct. The problem is that they are correct in the way a crusader is correct before the walls of Jerusalem — with the patience of a man who believes history is on his side."

### Scene 3: [[Cabinet Office Briefing Rooms]] — The Debate

**[TIME]: Day 4 — 2026-06-21, 08:40 Zulu**

The debate turned, as [[Stephen Davenport|Davenport]] knew it would, along the axis of the oldest argument in government: diplomacy versus defence.

[[William Ashford|Ashford]] spoke first. He had been Foreign Secretary under the caretaker government that preceded the coalition and had retained the post through the unity settlement — a testament to his competence, or to the fact that no one else wanted the job. "Prime Minister, the precedent is clear. We are dealing with a sovereign power — unusual in character, yes, but a power with territory, a government, a military, and a legal framework. They have extended hospitality to our officer. They have invited a formal embassy. The correct response is to accept the embassy, establish a diplomatic channel, and engage on the basis of mutual recognition. No theological entanglement. No cosmic narrative. We treat them as a state, not a church."

"And if their entire state is a church?" [[Michael Grantham|Grantham]] said. "What then? You can't separate their foreign policy from their theology. Their King rules by divine right — not metaphorically, William. Literally. Their military blesses its ammunition. Their intelligence service is called the Office of the Propagation of Virtue. You want to treat them as a secular state, but there is no secular state to treat. You'd be talking to a pulpit."

"Every state has interests," [[William Ashford|Ashford]] replied, unruffled. "Even theocracies. Their interest is survival — they have been fighting an existential war for eight centuries. Our interest is also survival, but of a different kind. We need to understand the Anomaly, ensure it poses no physical threat to our world, and establish rules of engagement for whatever traffic passes through it. Those are diplomatic objectives. They do not require us to take a position on the nature of God."

"Unless God takes a position on us," [[Michael Grantham|Grantham]] said.

[[Alex Merrick|General Merrick]] had been silent until now. When he spoke, it was with the flat, uninflected tone of a man delivering a weather report. "Prime Minister, I have read Captain [[James Hawthorne|Hawthorne]]'s after-action reports and the intelligence assessments of Faithful military capability. I am going to give you three facts. One: their warships — dreadnought-era hulls by our standards — mount weapons and armour that contain a material called [[Orichalcum]]. This material has properties that our physicists cannot yet explain. Initial tests suggest it interacts with certain energetic phenomena in ways that violate our understanding of thermodynamics. Two: the Heretic Legion — the force the Faithful are fighting — is described in their briefings as possessing capabilities that, if even half of them are true, exceed anything in our order of battle. We are not talking about a conventional military threat. We are talking about what the Faithful themselves describe as supernatural warfare. Three: if the Anomaly is permeable and traffic can pass in both directions, then there is no guarantee that the Heretic Legion will respect the boundary. The Faithful did not know we existed until three days ago. The Heretics may not know yet either. But they will."

He paused. "I am not asking you to believe in Hell, Prime Minister. I am asking you to plan for the contingency that someone on the other side of that portal has capabilities we cannot counter and intentions we cannot predict. For that, I need a clear chain of command and unambiguous rules of engagement."

[[Stephen Davenport|Davenport]] turned to the Home Secretary. "Public order."

The Home Secretary — a compact woman with a Northern accent and the exhausted eyes of someone who had not slept since the leak of May — said: "The Anomaly has been designated a restricted maritime zone. Shipping has been rerouted. The press has been fed a story about a joint NATO exercise involving unexploded ordnance disposal. That will hold for another week, possibly two. But we cannot contain this forever. The Faithful delegation is twelve people. They can be managed. But if an embassy opens, if traffic becomes regular, if the public learns that a parallel England — an England that fought the Crusades into the twentieth century — exists a hundred miles off the Cornish coast, we will have a crisis of belief on our hands that makes the constitutional crisis look like a parish council dispute. We have six thousand imams in this country. We have synagogues, temples, gurdwaras, and a hundred thousand people who worship nothing at all. How do you tell them that Hell is real but only on one side of a portal, and God is visible but only if you're born in the right timeline?"

She did not wait for an answer. The question was rhetorical. The question was the point.

The Chancellor, who had been staring at a spreadsheet throughout the debate, looked up. "And who pays for this? The Treasury is already stretched to the limit. The domestic package is haemorrhaging money. If we are now talking about a new military cordon, a new diplomatic mission, new intelligence assets — I need to know where the money is coming from. And I need to know what I tell the markets."

"You tell them nothing," [[Stephen Davenport|Davenport]] said. "For now."

### Scene 4: [[Cabinet Office Briefing Rooms]] — The Archbishop

**[TIME]: Day 4 — 2026-06-21, 09:15 Zulu**

The Archbishop of Canterbury had been invited to attend the briefing as an observer — a courtesy extended, [[Stephen Davenport|Davenport]] suspected, by someone in the Palace who understood that this crisis had a spiritual dimension the Cabinet was poorly equipped to address. [[Peter Chisholm]] was not what the room expected. He was a northern man, short and broad, with the hands of someone who had done physical work before the Church claimed him. He wore a simple black clerical shirt under a grey jacket. No mitre. No crozier. No ceremony. He had arrived early, taken a seat at the far end of the table, and said nothing for the first hour and a half.

When he finally spoke, the room went still.

"Prime Minister, I have read [[Father Beauchamp|Beauchamp]]'s report. I have read the transcript of his conversation with [[Daniel Foster|Reverend Foster]]. I have read the Synod's declaration and the King's decree. And I have spent the last twenty-four hours doing something I have not done since my doctoral work thirty years ago: I have opened the Book of Revelation and tried to read it as a field manual."

He removed a pair of reading glasses and polished them on his sleeve — a gesture so ordinary, so human, that it seemed to shrink the room.

"Let me tell you what frightens me. It is not that the Faithful believe in miracles. I believe in miracles — in the theological sense, which is to say I believe that God, having created the universe, is not obliged to follow its rules. It is not that the Faithful believe in Hell. I believe in evil — real evil, not the therapeutic euphemisms we have invented to avoid the word. It is not even that they believe our world is an ark. The image of the ark — a vessel of preservation, a remnant spared from destruction — is a deep and valid theme in our own tradition, and I cannot fault them for reading the facts and arriving at the metaphor that fits."

He put his glasses back on. "What frightens me is the direction of the metaphor. In their reading, they are Noah. They are the ones who have weathered the flood. We are the cargo. We are the animals in the hold, preserved through no merit of our own, waiting to be released onto dry land by a saviour who has already been chosen. They are not wrong to see us as preserved. They are wrong about who preserves whom."

[[Stephen Davenport|Davenport]] felt the temperature in the room drop. "Go on."

[[Peter Chisholm|Chisholm]] folded his hands on the table. "The Faithful have something we have lost, Prime Minister. Or perhaps something we never had. They have a God who answers. They have a Church that commands visible power. They have saints who appear in battle and relics that stop bullets. They live in a world where the supernatural is not a matter of private belief but a matter of public record. That is not delusion. That is not mass psychosis sustained over eight centuries and a hundred million souls. That is reality. Their reality. And what do we have? We have a world where God has been silent — entirely, comprehensively silent — for the entire span of our recorded existence. No saints. No miracles. No Orichalcum. No visible mark on any soul, for good or ill."

"And you think this is significant," [[Stephen Davenport|Davenport]] said.

"I think it is the only significant thing," [[Peter Chisholm|Chisholm]] said. "The Faithful look at us and see a blank page. We look at them and see a medieval fever dream. But what if both readings are wrong? What if the blankness of our souls is not a defect but a design? What if someone — Someone — arranged for our world to be sealed away from the war, not because we are the cargo, but because we are the crew? Not because we need saving, but because we are meant to do the saving?"

[[Michael Grantham|Grantham]] let out a short, sharp breath that was almost a laugh. "Archbishop, with respect — are you suggesting that we, a secular democracy with an established church most of its citizens ignore, are meant to save a world that has actual, visible God backing it?"

"I am suggesting," [[Peter Chisholm|Chisholm]] said evenly, "that 'visible God' may be the problem. They have had eight centuries of divine intervention, and they have fought to a stalemate. They have had saints and relics and holy weapons, and they have not won. Perhaps the lesson of their world is not that God saves those who see Him. Perhaps the lesson is that visible faith — faith without the space to doubt, believe without the freedom to choose — cannot finish the war. Perhaps what they need is not more proof. Perhaps what they need is what we have: a faith that expects nothing and endures anyway."

The silence that followed was the silence of men and women who had come to a briefing expecting a geopolitical problem and had instead been handed a theological one.

[[Stephen Davenport|Davenport]] did not break the silence. He let it breathe. Then he said quietly: "Archbishop, if you were in my chair — if you had to set a course for this government — what would you recommend?"

[[Peter Chisholm|Chisholm]] considered the question for a long moment. "I would recommend what I suspect you have already decided, Prime Minister. Cautious, secular engagement. Treat them as a foreign power. Accept their embassy. Establish protocols. Avoid theological entanglement. You are not the head of a church. You cannot be. The moment this government claims any divine mandate — the moment it so much as implies that the Faithful might be right about the ark — it ceases to govern a secular state and becomes something else. A theocracy by proxy. A viceroyalty of heaven. You cannot do that. You must not do that."

He paused. "But you should also understand something. The Faithful will not accept your secularism. They cannot. To them, your refusal to acknowledge the theological dimension of this contact is not neutrality. It is blindness. It is the sin of the man who looks at a burning bush and sees only a fire hazard. They will be patient. They will be correct. But they will wait — because they believe that eventually, the fire will speak. And when it does…" He left the sentence unfinished.

"When it does," [[Stephen Davenport|Davenport]] finished for him, "we will have to decide whether we are the ark or the flood."

[[Peter Chisholm|Chisholm]] nodded slowly. "Yes, Prime Minister. That is exactly what we will have to decide."

### Scene 5: [[Cabinet Office Briefing Rooms]] — The Decision

**[TIME]: Day 4 — 2026-06-21, 10:30 Zulu**

[[Stephen Davenport|Davenport]] stood. The debate had run for nearly three hours. Coffee had been drunk and replaced. Biscuits had been eaten. Notes had been taken, annotated, and crossed out. The encrypted screens along the wall showed the naval cordon, the portal's position, the estimated positions of friendly and unknown vessels. Somewhere on those screens, a small icon marked [[Rebecca Osei|Osei]]'s last known position — somewhere on the other side, in the blood-coloured sky of the TC world.

He cleared his throat. The room, which had been murmuring in side conversations, went quiet.

"Here is the course. The Foreign Secretary will despatch a formal acceptance of King Robert's invitation. We will send an embassy — small, civilian-led, with a military escort for protection only. The embassy's mandate is diplomatic, not theological. It will not discuss the ark. It will not discuss divine purpose. It will discuss boundaries, protocols, threat assessment, and the terms under which our two worlds may peacefully coexist."

He turned to [[Michael Grantham|Grantham]]. "Defence Secretary, I want a full contingency plan for Heretic incursion on my desk within seventy-two hours. Assume nothing. Even if the Heretic Legion cannot cross the portal, I want to know what we would face if they could. Coordinate with the CDS."

"Sir."

[[Alex Merrick|General Merrick]], you will have your chain of command. All military assets in the cordon zone will report through a single operational commander — designate whoever you choose. Rules of engagement remain defensive. No first strike. No provocation. But no one crosses that portal with hostile intent without our knowing why."

"Yes, Prime Minister."

"Home Secretary, I want a public communications strategy within the week. We will not be able to keep the lid on this forever. When the story breaks, I want it to break on our terms — sober, factual, no speculation about theology or cosmic significance. The line is that we have made contact with a parallel human civilisation. Full stop. No angels. No demons. No ark."

The Home Secretary nodded, clearly relieved.

"Chancellor — the Treasury will find the money. We are a National Unity Government. If we cannot find the money for this, there is no point to any of us being in this room."

The Chancellor's expression suggested he had expected this and had already begun recalculating.

"And for all of you," [[Stephen Davenport|Davenport]] said, his voice hardening, "one final thing. The Faithful believe we are an ark. They believe we are preserved. They believe we are sacred. And I want to be very clear: we are none of those things. We are a government. We are a democracy. We answer to Parliament and the electorate, not to Providence. Whatever the Faithful believe about us is their affair. But we will not — I repeat, *will not* — allow their theology to shape our policy. Am I understood?"

A murmur of assent. [[Michael Grantham|Grantham]]'s jaw was still set, but he nodded. [[William Ashford|Ashford]] looked quietly satisfied. [[Hugh Renwick|Renwick]]'s expression had not changed, because it never changed. [[Peter Chisholm|Chisholm]] sat at the far end of the table and said nothing — but [[Stephen Davenport|Davenport]] caught his eye, and in that glance there was something that looked, to the Prime Minister's discomfort, a great deal like pity.

The meeting broke. Chairs scraped. Aides gathered papers. The screens flickered with updated telemetry.

### Scene 6: The Private Exchange — [[Cabinet Office Briefing Rooms|COBR]] Corridor

**[TIME]: Day 4 — 2026-06-21, 11:00 Zulu**

The corridor outside the briefing room was empty. The ministers had dispersed to their departments, their secure phones, their waiting red boxes. The security detail had withdrawn to a respectful distance. Only [[Peter Chisholm|Chisholm]] remained — he had paused at the end of the corridor, beneath a window that looked out onto the blank interior wall of a light well, as if he were waiting for something.

[[Stephen Davenport|Davenport]] walked toward him. He had not planned the conversation. He had not prepared for it. But in the fourteen months since the National Unity Government was formed, he had learned to recognise the moments when the real decisions were made — and they were never in the room.

"Archbishop," he said. "Walk with me."

They walked. Past the blast-proof doors, past the silent air filters, up a flight of stairs that led toward the Cabinet Office proper. Their footsteps echoed in the empty stairwell.

"You held something back in the room," [[Stephen Davenport|Davenport]] said. "I've seen the look before. What didn't you say?"

[[Peter Chisholm|Chisholm]] did not break stride. "I didn't say what I am about to say, because saying it in front of the Defence Secretary and the DG of MI5 would have been… unhelpful. Not because it is wrong. Because they are not equipped to hear it."

"I'm listening."

They reached the top of the stairs. [[Peter Chisholm|Chisholm]] stopped. The light from the window above fell across his face, and for a moment — just a moment — he looked not like a northern vicar who had risen to the primacy through a career of careful administration, but like something older. A man who had been asked a question he had spent his entire life preparing to answer.

"Prime Minister, the Faithful have a God who answers. We do not. They have saints who appear in battle. We have stained glass. They have miracles. We have metaphors. They look at us and see a world preserved — an ark, a remnant, a seed. And you — quite rightly — told that room that we will not play their game. We will be secular. We will be cautious. We will treat them as a foreign power and nothing more."

He paused. His voice dropped — not to a whisper, but to the register of real honesty, the voice a man uses when the performance is over.

"But here is the question I could not ask in that room, Stephen. What if the Faithful are not wrong?"

[[Stephen Davenport|Davenport]] felt the question land in his chest like a stone dropped into still water. He did not answer. He waited.

[[Peter Chisholm|Chisholm]] continued: "I do not mean that we should kneel to King Robert and declare ourselves a province of his holy empire. I do not mean that we should accept their theology wholesale and discard eight centuries of our own. I mean something smaller. And more dangerous."

"What?"

"What if they are correct that something — Someone — arranged for our world to be hidden? What if they are correct that our souls are different from theirs — unmarked, unclaimed, preserved? What if the ark theology is true, but the direction is wrong?"

His voice was very quiet now. The hum of the building's ventilation was louder than his words.

"Every reading of Scripture — ours and theirs — tells us that the ark is a vessel of salvation. But it does not tell us *who is inside it*. The Faithful look at us and see the cargo — the animals, the remnant, the seed waiting to be planted when the flood recedes. But what if that is backwards? What if we are not the cargo? What if we are the crew?"

[[Stephen Davenport|Davenport]] said nothing, his mind working.

"What if," [[Peter Chisholm|Chisholm]] pressed, "the Faithful have been fighting for eight centuries — with visible God, with saints, with miracles — and they have not won, because they were never meant to win alone? What if our world was hidden not to be saved, but to *arrive*? Not as a remnant, but as a rescue? Not as the ark that floats, but as the hand that reaches down into the water?"

[[Stephen Davenport|Davenport]] stared at him. "You're saying we're meant to save *them*."

"I'm saying the Faithful may be right about the ark, but wrong about who is the cargo and who is the crew." [[Peter Chisholm|Chisholm]]'s eyes were steady. "And if that is true — if it is even *possibly* true — then your policy of cautious secular engagement, correct as it is for the moment, will eventually collide with something it cannot manage. Because the Faithful are not waiting for a diplomatic communiqué. They are waiting for a sign. They are waiting for us to become what they think we are."

The silence stretched between them.

"Archbishop," [[Stephen Davenport|Davenport]] said at last, "are you telling me that we may be the instrument of Providence?"

"I am telling you," [[Peter Chisholm|Chisholm]] said, "that the most unsettling question in the room today was not whether the Faithful are right. It is whether they are wrong about what it means. And that question — that one question — will follow us through everything that comes next."

[[Stephen Davenport|Davenport]] was a secular man. He had been a secular man his entire public life. He had attended church for weddings, funerals, and the annual Remembrance Sunday service, and he had meant none of it — not because he was hostile to faith, but because faith had never spoken to him in a language he understood. But standing in that corridor, beneath the grey light of the Whitehall light well, with the most powerful clergyman in England looking at him with an expression that was not quite fear and not quite hope, he felt something shift.

Not a conversion. Not a revelation. A question.

"Peter," he said — and it was the first time he had used the Archbishop's first name, a small breach of protocol that neither of them remarked on. "If you're right — if the direction is wrong — what do you want me to do about it?"

[[Peter Chisholm|Chisholm]] was silent for a long moment. Then he said: "Nothing. Today. Today you did exactly what you should have done. You set a secular course. You kept the theology at arm's length. You preserved the state's neutrality. That was correct. That was necessary."

"But?"

"But the day will come — I do not know when, but I know it will come — when the Faithful ask you to choose. Not between diplomacy and war. Between arkhood and nationhood. Between being the cargo and being the crew. On that day, you will need to decide what you actually believe. And I am not sure any of us — you, me, the Cabinet, the Church, the country — knows the answer yet."

[[Stephen Davenport|Davenport]] looked at him. "And you? Do you know what you believe?"

[[Peter Chisholm|Chisholm]] smiled — a small, tired smile that did not reach his eyes. "I believe that God hides Himself for a reason. I believe that our world's silence is not an absence but a space — a space we were meant to fill with something. I believe that the Faithful, for all their visible miracles, have been fighting the same war for eight hundred years and have not won it, and I do not think that is an accident. I believe that our arrival — our discovery, our blank souls, our faith without proof — may be the thing they have been praying for without knowing it."

He paused. "But I believe these things in the way I believe in the Resurrection, Stephen. Which is to say: I believe them on my knees, in the dark, with no proof and no certainty. I believe them the way a drowning man believes in the shore. Because the alternative — that all of this is random, and we are alone, and the Faithful's God is a coincidence of physics — is a far harder thing to believe than any creed."

[[Stephen Davenport|Davenport]] said nothing for a long moment. Then he extended his hand. [[Peter Chisholm|Chisholm]] took it.

"Thank you, Archbishop. For the honesty."

"Thank you, Prime Minister. For the question."

They parted at the end of the corridor. [[Peter Chisholm|Chisholm]] walked toward the lobby and the waiting car that would take him back to Lambeth Palace. [[Stephen Davenport|Davenport]] walked toward the Cabinet Room, where a stack of red boxes and a domestic crisis that had not paused for the apocalypse were waiting.

But as he walked, he found he could not shake the Archbishop's question. It had lodged somewhere behind his sternum — a small, sharp splinter that refused to dissolve.

*What if we are not the cargo?*

*What if we are the crew?*

The door to the Cabinet Room closed behind him. Outside, in the ordinary London morning, traffic moved. Buses ran their routes. The city went about its business with the sublime ignorance of a civilisation that did not yet know it had been discovered by God.

And somewhere, in a world with a blood-coloured sky, Lieutenant Commander [[Rebecca Osei]] was walking through the streets of a city that had not known peace in eight hundred years, carrying a crucifix made of a metal that should not exist, heading toward a throne upon which sat a king who believed — with absolute, terrifying certainty — that she was the answer to a prayer he had not yet spoken.

The ark was open. The flood was waiting. And no one — not in Whitehall, not in Lambeth, not in the Fortress of the White Cliffs — knew whether the water was rising or receding.
