# Chapter 06.1 – A Stranger at the Table

## Summary

Captain Park Sung-ho has been handed to Paradis as a hostage — a gesture of good faith, a soldier traded for a soldier. He is not a diplomat. He is not a linguist. He is a thirty-eight-year-old Republic of Korea Marine carrying fifty Eldian phrases and a notebook, dropped into a pre-industrial walled city that has just learned the world wants it dead. Over the next week, Park eats at the scout table, spars in the training yard, walks through a village market, and writes his report in a language no Paradisian can read. He is excluded from secret meetings, watched by a traumatised survivor called [[Floch Forster|Floch]], and approached by a tall woman named [[Yelena]] who speaks Marleyan with him directly — no translation needed — and asks questions too sharp to be casual. By the time the helicopters return to collect him, Park has learned what the drones could not show: that the people of Paradis are not triggers, not powder kegs, not abstractions — and that the hardest thing to put in a report is the thing that changes you.

## Scenes

### Scene 1: The Ride In — Night of Day 104 / Dawn of Day 105

The horse's name was something in Eldian that [[Park Sung-ho]] could not pronounce.

He had learned to ride at twelve, on his grandfather's farm in Chungcheong — a brown mare named Ggot-nim that had bitten him twice before accepting his presence. That was twenty-six years ago. The horse beneath him now was a Paradisian cavalry mount, thick-necked and patient, navigating the dark grassland with the automatic attention of an animal that had walked this route before. The stirrups were leather and iron. The saddle was heavier than anything he had used in the ROK Marine Corps. And the rider ahead of him — [[Hange Zoë|Commander Hange]], her silhouette sharp against the pre-dawn grey — had not spoken in nearly an hour.

The silence suited him. He was exhausted in a way that went past muscle and bone into something older, something that lived in the space behind the eyes. The rendezvous. The helicopters. The lights. The handshake with [[Elias Vogler|Vogler]] — his last link to his own world, lifting off into the dark without him. He was alone now. Truly alone. The only man on an alien planet who knew what a microchip was.

The Wall appeared at the edge of the horizon like a seam between the earth and the sky.

[[Park Sung-ho]] had seen the Walls before — on drone footage, on GPR returns, on the tactical maps in the CATF intelligence archive at Grande Terre. He had counted the Colossal Titans embedded in Wall Maria, had reviewed the signatures on a screen in a climate-controlled module while drinking ship's coffee. None of that had prepared him for what fifty metres of vertical stone looked like from the ground.

It was not a wall. It was a fact. It was the kind of structure that stopped being architecture and became geology — a mountain range that had been carved into a circle by a god with a ruler. The stone caught the first grey light of dawn and threw it back at the sky without warmth. The gate — an iron portcullis set into the Wall's base, flanked by cannon emplacements — was open. Garrison soldiers in brown uniforms stood at the checkpoint. They had been told to expect him. Their stares told him they had not been told enough.

"What is he wearing?" one of them asked [[Hange Zoë|Hange]], in the kind of voice people used when they thought the stranger could not understand them.

"He's from further away than Marley," [[Hange Zoë|Hange]] said. "He's a guest. Treat him like one."

[[Park Sung-ho]] understood guest. He understood further away than Marley. He understood that the soldier who had asked the question was still staring at the flag patch on his shoulder — the red and blue of the Republic of Korea, meaningless on an island that had never seen a national flag beyond its own Walls.

The horse walked through the gate. The city opened around them — stone buildings, cobbled streets, gas lamps guttering in the dawn wind. A woman pulled a cart of vegetables across the road. A boy chased a dog through an alley. Smoke rose from a dozen chimneys, coal-black and heavy. The smell was different from Grande Terre — not diesel and salt and prefab metal, but woodsmoke, leather, bread, horse manure, the organic thickness of a city that ran on muscle and fire. It was the smell of the late nineteenth century, preserved in aspic on an island that had been cut off from history for a hundred years.

[[Park Sung-ho]] rode through it in his Korean duty uniform, on a horse he could not name, toward a headquarters he had only seen from satellite altitude.

He had never been this far from home.

### Scene 2: The Room — Day 105, Predawn

The room was small. A cot against one wall. A wooden table with a candle. A washbasin with cold water. A window that looked onto a courtyard where Survey Corps soldiers were already drilling — dull thuds of practice blades, the rhythmic hiss of ODM gas releases, a sergeant shouting corrections in Eldian that [[Park Sung-ho]] could not parse.

A woman — an adjutant, mid-twenties, with the watchful stillness of a soldier who had seen more than she would ever say — had led him here. She had pointed at the cot, at the basin, at the folded clothes on the foot of the bed. Civilian clothes. Plain linen shirt, brown trousers, woollen socks. His uniform would be washed and returned. Until then, he was to look like everyone else.

"Food comes at the bell," she said. She spoke slowly, for his benefit. "Sleep. You look like a corpse."

He understood sleep. He slept.

The cot was hard. The blanket was wool and smelled of cedar. The sounds of the courtyard — blades, gas, the sergeant — faded into a grey hum that pulled him under. He did not dream. He did not move. He slept the way a soldier slept when the body overruled the mind — completely, without protest, the deep unconsciousness of a man who had been awake for forty hours and had just been handed to an alien military on an alternate Earth.

When he woke, the light through the window was midday. The courtyard was quiet. The adjutant had left a bowl of water and a piece of bread on the table, and the bread was still warm.

He ate it standing up. Then he put on the civilian clothes, folded his uniform into a neat square on the cot, and walked out of the room into a building full of strangers who had spent their whole lives fighting monsters and had just learned that the monsters were not the worst thing in the world.

### Scene 3: The Mess — Day 105, Afternoon

The mess hall was a long stone chamber with a vaulted ceiling and wooden tables scarred by generations of soldiers. The windows were high and narrow, slots cut into the thick walls of the converted barracks, admitting thin shafts of grey afternoon light. The smell was stew and hearth-smoke and the faint, sharp tang of cleaning solvent — ODM maintenance fluid, [[Park Sung-ho]] guessed, the kind of smell that clung to everything in a military facility and became invisible to the people who lived there.

He stood in the doorway. The room was perhaps half-full — soldiers in Survey Corps green, a few in Garrison brown, eating at tables in groups of three and four. Conversations were low, punctuated by the scrape of bowls and the clink of metal cups. When he entered, the conversations did not stop. They slowed. Then they resumed, with the specific self-consciousness of people who were pretending not to notice a stranger.

[[Jean Kirstein|Jean]] waved him over. He was sitting at a long table near the window with [[Armin Arlert|Armin]] and two other soldiers — a man with grey eyes who did not look up from his bowl, and a woman with dark hair who was watching [[Park Sung-ho]] with the kind of attention that made him feel like a target acquisition.

"Captain," [[Jean Kirstein|Jean]] said. He pushed a bowl across the table. "It's not what you're used to. But it's hot."

The stew was a thick brown liquid with chunks of potato and what might have been mutton. It was salted heavily — the Paradisian approach to flavouring, he had already noticed, was to apply salt until the food stopped protesting. It was also, by the standards of a man who had eaten field rations for three months on Grande Terre, perfectly adequate.

"Thank you," [[Park Sung-ho]] said. His Eldian was halting, each word placed like a foot on uncertain ground. "It is — " he searched for the word. Warm. Hot. Good. He settled on: "Enough."

[[Jean Kirstein|Jean]] smiled — a brief, tired expression that was closer to a flinch. "Enough is more than we used to get."

[[Park Sung-ho]] ate. The soldiers around him talked. He listened to the cadence of their voices, the rise and fall of Eldian, the occasional borrowed Marleyan word — iceburst, cartridge, patrol — that he could pick out like stones in a river. [[Armin Arlert|Armin]] was explaining something about coastal supply routes, gesturing with a piece of bread, his voice calm and precise in the way that suggested he had already thought through every objection before speaking. [[Jean Kirstein|Jean]] interrupted with objections anyway, and [[Armin Arlert|Armin]] absorbed them without irritation, the way a teacher absorbed questions from a student who was learning faster than he admitted.

The grey-eyed man — [[Levi Ackerman|Captain Levi]], [[Park Sung-ho]] remembered from [[Elias Vogler|Vogler]]'s briefings — finished his bowl in silence, stood, and left without acknowledging anyone. The dark-haired woman — [[Mikasa Ackerman|Mikasa]] — watched him go, then returned her attention to her food without expression. Neither had spoken to [[Park Sung-ho]]. Neither had needed to. He understood, with the instinct of a soldier who had served with special operations units, that these two were not unfriendly. They were evaluating. And evaluation did not require conversation.

He was scraping the bottom of his bowl when [[Hange Zoë|Hange]] arrived.

She walked into the mess hall the way she walked everywhere — fast, distracted, her goggles pushed up on her forehead, her hair escaping from the tie at the back of her neck. She had not slept. [[Park Sung-ho]] could see it in the shadows under her eyes and the slight tremor in her hands — not fear or weakness, just the accumulated fatigue of a commander who had been awake for too many hours negotiating the survival of her species and was now running on whatever reserve fuel the human body kept for emergencies.

She dropped onto the bench across from him. "Captain Park. You're awake. You're eating. You're in civilian clothes. Three out of three."

[[Park Sung-ho]] understood about half of this. He nodded.

[[Hange Zoë|Hange]] looked at his bowl. "Is the food acceptable?"

"Yes." It was one of the fifty words he knew. He used it whenever possible.

He reached into his pocket — the civilian trousers had deep pockets, a small mercy — and pulled out his notebook and pen. The notebook was a standard-issue ROK military field journal, olive-green cover, waterproof pages. The pen was a stainless-steel ballpoint, unremarkable by Earth standards, the kind of object you could buy in a stationery shop for two thousand won. He opened the notebook to a blank page and wrote the Eldian word for food — kai — in Hangul beside it, building the personal dictionary he had been compiling since his language training with [[Frieda]] and [[Elias Vogler|Vogler]].

[[Hange Zoë|Hange]]'s hand shot across the table and stopped an inch from his pen.

"What is that?"

"A pen."

"May I?"

He handed it to her. She took it the way he had once seen a bomb disposal technician take a suspected IED — carefully, with the specific reverence of someone who understood that an unfamiliar object could be either a tool or a weapon and was waiting for evidence. She examined the ballpoint. The clip. The click mechanism. She clicked it twice — the soft plastic sound echoed in the quiet mess hall — and her eyes widened.

"It writes without ink."

"It has ink. Inside." He made a corkscrew gesture at the barrel of the pen. "Inside. Sealed."

She wrote on the back of her hand. A thin blue line appeared. She stared at it as though he had produced gold from his sleeve. "How long does the ink last?"

"Many pages. Many — " he didn't know the word for months. He settled on: "Long time."

"Long time." [[Hange Zoë|Hange]] looked at the pen again, then back at him. "In our world, a pen lasts until the ink runs dry. Then you buy a new one or you dip it in a pot. This — " she held it up, " — is a machine. A tiny machine. For writing." She placed it on the table like an artefact. "Your world has machines for writing. What else does it have machines for?"

"Everything," [[Park Sung-ho]] said. He did not know the Eldian word for everything, so he said the English word, and then, when [[Hange Zoë|Hange]]'s eyebrows went up, he spread his hands to indicate the scope of the concept.

"Everything," [[Hange Zoë|Hange]] repeated. She said the English word carefully, tasting it. "E-ve-ry-thing. That is a large word for a small pen."

Then she noticed the notebook.

She did not ask. She looked at it — the olive cover, the waterproof pages, the Hangul characters visible on the open leaf — and then she looked at [[Park Sung-ho]], and her expression was the expression of a scientist who had just been informed that there was a door she had not been allowed to open.

"May I?"

He hesitated. The notebook contained his initial observations — the terrain between the rendezvous site and the Wall, the layout of the gate checkpoint, the names of the officers he had met, rough phonetic transcriptions of Eldian words. It was not classified in any formal sense, because there was no classification protocol for a liaison officer who had been handed to an alien military. But it was his. And handing it over felt like handing over a piece of himself.

He handed it over.

[[Hange Zoë|Hange]] opened the notebook. She turned the pages slowly, one after another, her eyes tracing the lines of Hangul characters. Her expression shifted — first curiosity, then concentration, then something that might have been frustration and might have been delight. She reached the last page and turned it back to the first and stared at it for another ten seconds.

"I cannot read a single word," she said. She did not sound disappointed. She sounded fascinated. "This is — what do you call it?"

"Hangul. Korean." He pointed at the characters. "Han-gul."

"Hangul." She traced a character with her finger — the circle and cross of the syllable han. "Every shape means something. Every group of shapes means a sound. And every sound means a word. And I cannot read any of it." She looked up at him. "You let me read this, knowing I would understand nothing. That is either trust or strategy."

[[Park Sung-ho]] thought about this. His answer, when it came, was simpler than he expected. "You asked."

[[Hange Zoë|Hange]] laughed — a short, surprised sound, the laugh of a woman who had not expected to laugh today and was not sure she had earned it. "I did ask."

She slid the notebook back across the table. Then she pointed at the pen. "Write something for me. My name."

He wrote 한지. He wrote it carefully, the way he had learned to write his mother's name when he was seven, each stroke deliberate. He turned the notebook so she could see.

"Han-ji," he said.

[[Hange Zoë|Hange]] stared at the characters. Then she reached across the table, took the pen — her grip was wrong, too tight, the way a child held a crayon — and tried to copy the shapes onto the sleeve of her jacket. The result was a smudge of blue ink that resembled hangul the way a stick figure resembled a human being.

"That," she announced, studying the smudge, "is terrible."

"First time," [[Park Sung-ho]] said. "Always terrible."

"Show me again."

He wrote it again. She tried again. The second attempt was slightly less terrible. The third was recognisable as writing, if you were generous with the definition of writing. By the fourth, [[Armin Arlert|Armin]] had leaned over to watch, and [[Jean Kirstein|Jean]] had produced a scrap of paper from his pocket, and the mess table had become a classroom — four Eldian soldiers and one Korean Marine, passing a ballpoint pen between them, scratching out characters on paper and jacket sleeves and the wooden tabletop, laughing at each other's failures, achieving nothing of strategic value and everything of diplomatic value.

[[Mikasa Ackerman|Mikasa]] did not join the writing lesson. But she watched, and at one point — when [[Jean Kirstein|Jean]] produced a character that looked more like a dead spider than a syllable — the corner of her mouth moved in what might, in a different universe where Mikasa Ackerman smiled, have been called a smile.

[[Hange Zoë|Hange]] tapped the notebook. "I want to read this one day. Not the military reports. The rest of it. The words you are learning. The things you are writing about us. Teach me."

[[Park Sung-ho]] looked at the notebook. At the pen. At the Commander of the Survey Corps, who had spent her life studying the biology of creatures that ate her friends, and who was now asking to be taught a language from a world she had never known existed a week ago.

"It's a deal," he said. He said it in Korean, because he did not know how to say it in Eldian. But [[Hange Zoë|Hange]] understood the gesture — the hand extended, the nod, the smile that was tired and genuine — and she smiled back.

"Good. I am a fast learner."

"He is not," [[Jean Kirstein|Jean]] said, pointing at [[Armin Arlert|Armin]].

"I am literally writing it right now," [[Armin Arlert|Armin]] said, holding up his scrap of paper. It had fourteen Hangul characters on it. Four of them were correct.

The table laughed. They were not allies yet — not in any formal sense, not in any sense that would survive the second rendezvous and the committee report that [[Park Sung-ho]] would carry back to Grande Terre. But they were something. They were people who had shared a meal, and a pen, and the small, stubborn human determination to understand each other.

### Scene 4: The Training Yard — Day 106

[[Park Sung-ho]] woke at dawn, the habit of a career soldier, and went looking for the training yard.

He found it behind the main barracks — a rectangle of packed dirt marked with wooden posts and rope lines, the survey corps' equivalent of a gymnasium. Two soldiers were already there, sparring with practice blades under the eye of a veteran whose face had been rearranged by something sharp. They stopped when [[Park Sung-ho]] entered. He ignored them. He found a clear patch of dirt against the wall, dropped into a push-up position, and began his morning routine.

Fifty push-ups. Fifty sit-ups. A kilometre of laps around the yard's perimeter at a steady jog, breathing the cold morning air, letting his body remember that it was a body and not just a vehicle for his brain. The scouts watched from the sidelines, and as he moved through the routine — pull-ups on the practice frame, squats, the slow, deliberate stretches he had learned in special reconnaissance training — more soldiers drifted into the yard until there were perhaps twenty of them, standing at the edges, watching the alien exercise.

"What is that?" one of them asked. Young, maybe nineteen, with the broad shoulders of a farm boy and the scarred hands of someone who had held ODM blades for too many hours.

"Exercise," [[Park Sung-ho]] said.

"I know exercise. That is not exercise. That is — " the farm boy made a gesture that encompassed the push-ups, the pull-ups, the kilometre of jogging. "Punishment."

"Same thing," [[Park Sung-ho]] said.

The farm boy laughed. Then he stepped into the sparring square. "You know how to fight? With hands, not blades."

[[Park Sung-ho]] had been expecting this. A strange soldier in borrowed clothes, eating their food, sleeping in their barracks — eventually, someone would want to know if he could hold his ground. He stepped into the square.

The fight was not long. The farm boy was strong — stronger than [[Park Sung-ho]], probably, in raw muscle — but he was a brawler. He telegraphed every move the way a Titan telegraphed a charge. [[Park Sung-ho]] had spent two years in the ROK Marine Corps' special reconnaissance pipeline learning how to end fights before they started. He blocked the first punch. He sidestepped the second. He put the farm boy on the ground with a leg sweep that was more mechanics than force, and he offered his hand to pull him back up before the dust had settled.

The farm boy took it. "What was that?"

"Krav Maga." He said the Hebrew word, then, because the farm boy was staring at him, added: "From my world. Hand combat. For soldiers."

"Do it again."

He did it again. Slower this time, demonstrating, breaking the movement down into components the way a drill instructor broke down a field strip. The farm boy tried it. Failed. Tried again. Got halfway there. Other soldiers stepped in — asking to be shown, asking to try, asking to spar. The training yard became a class. [[Park Sung-ho]], who had not taught hand-to-hand combat since his last posting as an instructor at the Marine Corps Education and Training Group, found himself correcting grips and adjusting stances for an audience of soldiers who had fought Titans with grappling hooks and now wanted to learn how to fight humans with their hands.

After an hour, he was bruised in three places and winded in a way that felt earned. The scouts — more of them now, perhaps thirty — had stopped watching the alien and started watching the teacher. [[Floch Forster|Floch]] was among them, standing at the back with his arms crossed, his expression unreadable. He had not sparred. He had not asked any questions. He had watched the whole session in silence, and when [[Park Sung-ho]] caught his eye, [[Floch Forster|Floch]] did not look away. He held the gaze for three seconds — the specific duration of a challenge that was not yet a threat — then turned and walked back toward the barracks.

"Don't mind him," the farm boy said. "He's been like that since Shiganshina."

"Shiganshina," [[Park Sung-ho]] repeated. He knew the name from the briefing — the battle, the breach, the Beast Titan, the suicide charge. He knew the casualty figures. He knew that Floch was listed as the sole survivor of the unit that had charged the Beast's position.

"He was the only one who made it," the farm boy said. "Sometimes that's worse than not making it."

[[Park Sung-ho]] looked at the doorway where [[Floch Forster|Floch]] had disappeared. He understood that kind of survival. He had seen it in special reconnaissance — men who came back from missions alone, carrying the weight of everyone who had not, and never put it down. Those men were either the most reliable soldiers in a unit or the most dangerous, and you could not always tell which until it was too late.

### Scene 5: The Market — Day 107

[[Armin Arlert|Armin]] took him to the village.

It was not a military exercise. It was, [[Armin Arlert|Armin]] said as they walked through the Wall Rose gate toward the cluster of buildings visible across the fields, the kind of thing that could not happen at headquarters. "You can learn what a military is like by watching it drill," he said. "You learn what a country is like by watching it live."

The village was called Eichenhof — oak farm, the name a remnant of whatever Germanic root-language the Eldians had carried across the sea a century ago. It was small: perhaps two hundred people, a central square with a stone well, a blacksmith's forge at one end and a baker's shop at the other. The houses were timber-framed, whitewashed, their roofs thatched or shingled. Chickens scratched in the dirt. A dog slept in a patch of sun outside the baker's door. Children played with a ball made of rags in the square, their laughter carrying on the cold autumn air.

[[Park Sung-ho]] walked through it in his borrowed civilian clothes, his hands in his pockets, his notebook in his shirt. He looked, to the casual observer, like a traveller from a neighbouring district — a little older than the average farmer, a little stiffer in the shoulders, but unremarkable. The casual observer was not paying attention to the way he catalogued everything: the layout of the square, the number of exits, the sightlines from the well to the forge, the thickness of the walls. He was not doing it consciously. It was what his brain did with new places. Twenty years of special reconnaissance, and he had forgotten how to walk through a village without mapping it.

The baker was a woman in her fifties with flour on her elbows and a voice that carried across the square. She was arguing with a farmer about the price of rye, and she was winning. When she saw [[Armin Arlert|Armin]], her expression softened.

"Corporal Arlert," she said. "You haven't been here in weeks. They're working you too hard at headquarters."

"They're working everyone too hard," [[Armin Arlert|Armin]] said. "The railway. The coastal batteries. The new patrol schedules. I came to see if you were still here."

"Where would I go? Marley?" She laughed at her own joke — the kind of laugh that was half bitter and half genuine, the laugh of a civilian who had learned to live in a world that wanted her dead. Her eyes moved to [[Park Sung-ho]]. "Who is your friend?"

"A visitor," [[Armin Arlert|Armin]] said. "From very far away. He doesn't speak much Eldian."

"Where is he from?"

"Further than Marley."

The baker looked at [[Park Sung-ho]] for a long moment. Then she tore a piece of bread from a loaf on her counter, wrapped it in a cloth, and handed it to him.

"Hungry," she said. She said it slowly, the way people spoke to children. "Eat."

[[Park Sung-ho]] took the bread. "Thank you," he said, in his careful Eldian. "It is — " he searched for the word, found it, " — kind."

"It's bread," the baker said. "Bread is not kind. Bread is bread." But she smiled when she said it.

They walked through the rest of the village — past the blacksmith, who was repairing a ploughshare and did not look up; past the well, where two women were drawing water and talking in the quick, overlapping rhythm of old friends; past the children, who had abandoned their rag ball to chase a chicken that had escaped its coop. [[Park Sung-ho]] watched all of it, and somewhere between the baker's smile and the children's laughter and the blacksmith's hammer on the ploughshare, a thought formed in his mind that he had not been able to form from a drone feed or a personnel dossier or a classified biological report.

These were not subjects.

They were not triggers. They were not a biosecurity risk. They were not the abstract, terrifying potential of Titan transformation rendered in a committee report. They were a village. A baker who argued about rye prices. A blacksmith who fixed ploughs. Children who chased chickens. A woman who gave bread to a stranger because he looked hungry.

He had known this intellectually — had been briefed on it, had read the intelligence assessments that described the Paradisian civilian population in clinical language, had understood theoretically that genocide was wrong. But knowing and seeing were different things. Knowing was a report. Seeing was a baker handing you bread.

He would write that in his notebook. Not the strategic observation. Just the bread.

### Scene 6: Yelena — Day 108

She found him in the courtyard.

[[Park Sung-ho]] was sitting on a low stone wall near the barracks, notebook open on his knee, working through his vocabulary list — the Eldian word for wall (muro), the Eldian word for horse (mal), the Eldian word for trust (sinroe) — when a shadow fell across the page. He looked up. A tall woman with short blonde hair and a face that gave nothing away was standing above him, her hands clasped behind her back in the posture of someone who had been trained to look relaxed.

"Captain Park," she said. Her Marleyan was a different dialect from the one [[Elias Vogler|Vogler]] had taught him — harsher vowels, sharper consonants, the accent of a military upbringing rather than a scientific one. But it was the same language. After seven days immersed in Eldian, it fell on his ear like a familiar key finding its lock. "You are difficult to find alone."

[[Park Sung-ho]] closed his notebook. He answered in the same tongue — slower than she had spoken, but deliberate. "I did not know I was being looked for."

"Everything on Paradis is looked for, eventually." She sat on the wall beside him, keeping a careful distance — close enough to talk, far enough to run. "My name is [[Yelena]]. I am a Volunteer — a defector from Marley. I arrived on Paradis approximately two months ago, with a ship and a crew and a great deal of information about the empire's military dispositions. I have been assisting the Survey Corps with intelligence and military planning. And I have been trying to speak to you since the night you arrived."

"Why?"

"Because you are from the other world. The one with the flying machines. The one that has been watching us." She said it without accusation — a statement of fact, delivered in the same tone she might have used to describe the weather. "I want to understand what your world wants. Not the speech you gave Commander [[Hange Zoë|Hange]]. The truth."

[[Park Sung-ho]] looked at her. The intelligence officer in him — the part that had been trained to read defectors and double agents and the kind of people who volunteered information without being asked — catalogued the details. She was calm. Too calm. The calm of someone who had prepared for this conversation and was executing a script. The calm of someone who had more to hide than she was showing.

"The truth is what I told the Commander," he said. "Stability. Security. Research. Trade. We want to prevent the Rumbling. We want to understand Titan biology. We want access to Paradis's natural resources — "

"Iceburst stone."

"Iceburst stone. In exchange for technology, infrastructure, and medical support. It is not a demand. It is a negotiation."

[[Yelena]] studied him. Her eyes were pale, almost colourless, and they did not blink as often as they should. "Commander [[Hange Zoë|Hange]] has accepted this negotiation."

"In principle. The details will be discussed at the second rendezvous."

"And after the second rendezvous? What does your coalition intend? A permanent presence on Paradis? Military advisors? Infrastructure projects? Direct access to the Founding Titan?"

The Founding Titan. She had said it casually, as though it were one item on a list. But she had lingered on the word — the way a person lingered on a word they cared about more than the others. [[Park Sung-ho]] registered this and filed it.

"The Founding Titan is not ours to access," he said. "It is Paradis's. We have no interest in controlling it."

"You have interest in preventing it from being used."

"That is not the same thing as controlling it."

[[Yelena]] smiled. It was not a warm smile. It was the smile of a chess player who had just seen a move she had not anticipated and was recalculating. "You are careful, Captain. I respect that. Careful people survive. Careless people — " she looked toward the Wall, toward the gate, toward the world beyond it, " — become Titans."

She stood. "I hope our worlds can be friends. Truly. I have no loyalty to Marley, and I have no fear of your machines. I want what is best for Paradis. So do you, you say. Perhaps we want the same thing." She paused. "Perhaps we don't. I look forward to finding out."

She walked away. [[Park Sung-ho]] watched her go — the confident stride, the shoulders back, the hands still clasped behind her in that posture of deliberate relaxation. She did not look back.

He opened his notebook. He turned to a fresh page. He wrote, in Korean:

*Yelena is not what she claims. Do not share operational data. It's not clear what her goal is .She is waiting for something.*

He underlined the last sentence twice.

### Scene 7: The Report — Day 109

[[Park Sung-ho]] sat alone in his room, the candle burning on the table, the notebook open to a clean page, and wrote the report he would carry back to Grande Terre.

He wrote it in Korean. He wrote it by hand, in the careful script he had learned in officer training twenty years ago, each character placed with the same deliberate attention he had applied to writing Hange's name in the mess hall. The report was not long. He had been trained to write situation reports that a commanding officer could read in ninety seconds. But this one took him two hours.

The report said:

The Survey Corps is a competent, traumatised fighting force with approximately 200 active personnel and a civilian support network that extends into every district behind Wall Rose. Its leadership is stretched — Commander Hange is running a military, a government, and a scientific research programme simultaneously, and the strain is visible. Her subordinates are loyal and capable. Captain Levi is the most dangerous close-quarters combatant on either Earth. Corporal Armin is a strategic asset comparable to a general staff officer. The Corps' morale is fragile but functional — they have survived too much to break now, but they are one catastrophe from fracturing.

The civilian population is innocent. I have walked through a village. I have been handed bread by a baker. I have watched children chase chickens. These people are not the biosecurity risk that the EXNIHILO-1 classification implies. They are farmers and blacksmiths and mothers and children. They do not know about the Paths. They do not know about the mass creation. They do not know that their biology is a weapon waiting to be triggered. If the committee elects to intervene in Paradis — militarily or diplomatically — the civilian population must be treated as a protected class, not a threat vector.

Something is being hidden from me. Meetings I am not invited to. A figure — I do not know who — that the leadership discusses in lowered voices. I believe it is the Founding Titan. I believe he is being kept from me deliberately, and I believe the reason is concern for his psychological stability. If the Founder is unstable, the Rumbling threat calculation changes. I recommend that the committee authorise additional human intelligence collection on this subject before committing to any binding agreement.

Yelena of the Anti-Marleyan Volunteers is a variable. She speaks Marleyan — her dialect betrays a military upbringing, not a scientific one. She is probing for CATF's intentions. She asked specifically about access to the Founding Titan. I do not trust her. I recommend that CATF share no operational or strategic data with the Volunteer faction until Yelena's background and loyalties are independently verified.

The iceburst stone deposits are confirmed by local accounts. The scale is unknown to Paradisian surveyors — they lack the equipment to map the subsurface deposits fully — but the surface-level extraction alone has sustained Paradis's ODM operations for a century without depletion. The resource is real. The trade framework is viable.

My recommendation: proceed with the relationship. The Survey Corps is the best possible partner on the AOT world — morally, strategically, and operationally. Paradis is fragile but not hostile. The agreement in principle reached at the first rendezvous should be formalised. The committee should authorise the second rendezvous and provide me with a formal proposal to deliver.

He paused. He read the report from top to bottom. Then he added a final line, set apart from the rest, in smaller handwriting:

They are not what our reports say they are. They are less dangerous and more human. I do not know if the committee is ready to hear that, but it is the truth.

He closed the notebook. The candle burned low. Outside the window, the courtyard was silent. Somewhere in the barracks, a soldier laughed — a brief, tired sound, quickly extinguished. [[Park Sung-ho]] put the notebook in his pocket and lay down on the cot. He did not sleep for a long time.
