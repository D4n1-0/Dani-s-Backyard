# Chapter 07 – The Second Rendezvous

## Summary

Seven days after helicopters lifted off a dark grassland, a warship arrives at Paradis's eastern port carrying crates. [[Connie Springer]] watches his home resolve on the horizon — the Walls, the railway, the faces of his comrades waiting on the dock — and steps back onto Paradisian soil changed. What follows is not a negotiation. The negotiation happened off-screen, in a tent, while crates were unloaded. What follows is the first time Paradis receives gifts from another world: morphine, pens, books, coffee, clothes, tools, food that tastes of places that do not exist. [[Park Sung-ho|Captain Park]] watches the Survey Corps receive each object, each one a door. [[Eren Yeager]] watches from the edge of every scene, silent. [[Yelena]] calculates. [[Floch Forster|Floch]] does not unclench his arms. By the chapter's end, the formal treaty is signed off-screen, and the world widens — a three-month montage of aspirin in a village clinic, an atlas under candlelight, a coded letter being sealed, a bulldozer laying rail, a liaison office with a flag on a pole and a single officer at a desk. First contact ends. The relationship begins.

## Scenes

### Movement 1: The Return

#### Scene 1: The Rail — Day 111, 0600

The [[USS Tripoli (LHA-7)|Tripoli]] came around the headland at dawn, her grey hull cutting the grey water, and [[Connie Springer]] stood at the rail and watched his home become real.

He had been at sea for four days. The transit from [[Grande Terre Logistics Hub|Grande Terre]] — eight thousand kilometres north, the destroyer escort falling away at the hundred-kilometre mark to avoid spooking Paradisian coastal lookouts — had been quiet. He had eaten in the wardroom. He had slept in a bunk that did not vibrate. He had stood on the flight deck in the subantarctic wind and watched the ocean change colour from grey to blue to grey again as they crossed latitudes that had no names on any Paradisian chart.

Now the ocean was familiar. The grey was the grey of the sea outside Wall Maria — the cold, shallow water that stretched from Paradis's eastern coast to the edge of the known world. The sky was the low, overcast sky of autumn. The Wall — Wall Maria, the outer ring, fifty metres of vertical stone — was a seam on the horizon, and beneath it, resolving as the Tripoli closed the final kilometres, was the port.

He had left the port on horseback — six days ago, riding west through the dark toward the rendezvous grassland with a shipping manifest he did not yet understand and a future he could not imagine. He was returning to it on the flight deck of an amphibious assault ship from another world.

The port was familiar and alien at once. The stone pier was the same pier — granite blocks set by Paradisian labour, reinforced with fresh timber where the crane had been erected. The railway terminal was the same low building, the locomotive the same locomotive, the artillery emplacements the same emplacements. But he was seeing them from the sea, from a ship that had crossed an ocean that Paradisian sailors had never charted, and the perspective changed everything. The pier was small from here. The artillery was small. His comrades, lined up on the dock in their green cloaks, were small — and then they were not small, because the gangway was going down, and the small figures were resolving into faces, and among them was [[Jean Kirstein|Jean]], standing at the front with his arms crossed and his jaw set and the specific expression of a man who had spent a week waiting for a friend to come home.

"What did you expect?" [[Elias Vogler|Dr. Vogler]] said from beside him. The scientist had materialised at the rail sometime in the last minute, a cup of ship's coffee in his hand, his expression the tired, satisfied expression of a man who had spent four days coordinating a cargo manifest and was now watching the destination resolve.

"I don't know," [[Connie Springer|Connie]] said. "Smaller. Slower. I've been gone a week. I thought it would still be a week ago."

"A week on our side is a month on yours," [[Elias Vogler|Vogler]] said. "Infrastructure. Political consolidation. Intelligence integration. Your Commander [[Hange Zoë|Hange]] has been running a country and a military and a diplomatic negotiation simultaneously. She has probably slept twelve hours total since you left."

"That sounds like Hange."

[[Elias Vogler|Vogler]] looked at him. "You are nervous."

"I'm not nervous."

"You are gripping that railing the way a man grips a rope in a storm. You are nervous about something."

[[Connie Springer|Connie]] looked at his hands. [[Elias Vogler|Vogler]] was right. He was gripping the railing. He let go.

"I wrote Hange a letter," he said. "On Grande Terre. Before we left. It said — " he paused. "It said I think they're telling the truth. About the Rumbling. About the trade. About wanting to help. I'm a squad leader. I'm not Armin. I'm not a strategist. But I wrote it anyway. And now I have to hand it to her, and she's going to read it, and whatever happens after that is going to happen because I said I believed in something."

"That is called leadership," [[Elias Vogler|Vogler]] said. "It is uncomfortable. You are doing it correctly."

The Tripoli slowed. The harbour pilot — a Paradisian fishing boat pressed into service, its captain signalling with flags he had learned from a Volunteer manual — guided the massive ship toward the stone pier. The pier had been reinforced at some point in the past week: fresh timber, new stone, iron bollards that looked like they had been cast in a hurry. Someone had anticipated a ship larger than any Paradisian vessel. Someone, [[Connie Springer|Connie]] thought, had been planning for exactly this.

The gangway went down at 0647.

[[Connie Springer|Connie]] walked down it. The stone of the pier was solid beneath his boots — Paradisian stone, the same volcanic basalt that underpinned the Walls. The air was cold and smelled of salt and coal smoke and horse. The Survey Corps soldiers on the dock were watching the Tripoli with the same expressions he had worn seven days ago when he first saw the [[R-V Isabu|Isabu]]. Disbelief. Awe. The specific stillness of people who were processing something that did not fit inside any category they had been given.

[[Jean Kirstein|Jean]] was at the front of the formation. [[Armin Arlert|Armin]] beside him. [[Mikasa Ackerman|Mikasa]] slightly apart, as always, her dark hair pulled back, her expression unreadable. [[Sasha Braus|Sasha]] was trying not to fidget and failing. [[Levi Ackerman|Levi]] was at the rear — the Captain's position, the place where you could see everything and everyone and decide what to kill first if the situation required it.

[[Connie Springer|Connie]] stopped at the foot of the gangway. His comrades were looking at him. He was looking at them. For a long, strange moment, no one spoke.

"You're wearing different clothes," [[Jean Kirstein|Jean]] said.

[[Connie Springer|Connie]] looked down at himself. He was. The thermal base layer under his Survey Corps jacket. The Garmin on his wrist — still there, he had never returned it, [[Elias Vogler|Vogler]] had told him to keep it. The haircut he had gotten from a Korean barber on Grande Terre. He had forgotten all of it.

"They have machines for everything," he said. "Including that."

[[Jean Kirstein|Jean]] stared at him for another second. Then he smiled — the brief, tired smile of someone who had spent a week drilling coastal batteries and negotiating with Garrison officers and waiting for a ship that might not come.

"Welcome home, idiot."

[[Connie Springer|Connie]] handed his letter — folded, sealed with a dab of wax he had borrowed from [[Frieda]] — to [[Hange Zoë|Hange]]. She took it without opening it, tucked it into her jacket, and nodded once. Then she looked past him, at the Tripoli, at the crates being winched onto the deck, at the CATF personnel assembling near the gangway. Her expression was the expression of a Commander who had just been told that the negotiation was now a delivery, and the delivery was the size of a fortress.

"Captain [[Park Sung-ho|Park]]," she said. "You owe me a very long conversation. But first — " she looked at the crates, " — what did you bring us?"

### Movement 2: The Gifts

[[Park Sung-ho]] had spent a week on Paradis. He had eaten at the scout table. He had sparred in the training yard. He had walked through a village and been handed bread by a baker. He had been approached by [[Yelena]] in the courtyard and written *Yelena is not what she claims* in his notebook. He had slept on a cot in a room with a candle and woken each morning to the sound of soldiers drilling in the yard below. He had learned forty more Eldian words. He had not learned enough.

Now he stood on the dock of Paradis's eastern port, a Korean Marine in a borrowed Survey Corps jacket, watching the [[USS Tripoli (LHA-7)|Tripoli]] disgorge the first physical objects from one Earth to another as a gift, and he understood — with the clarity of a soldier who had spent his career reading situations before they read him — that he was watching history. Not the kind of history that got written in reports. The kind that got remembered by the people who were there, in the cold, on a stone pier, while crates were opened and the world changed.

The port was a horseshoe of stone and timber around a sheltered bay. The Tripoli sat at the deep-water pier — too large for any of the inner berths, her grey flanks towering over the Paradisian fishing boats and the captured Marleyan cruiser moored nearby. The railway terminal was at the western edge of the harbour, a low stone building with a slate roof and a platform where the locomotive stood venting steam. The cliffs rose on either side, crowned with Garrison artillery emplacements that had been emplaced in the past two months and were now manned by soldiers who kept looking at the Tripoli and then at their cannons and then back at the Tripoli, as though trying to calculate whether a six-inch shell would even dent the hull.

The unloading took most of the morning. CATF personnel — Marines in utility uniforms, sailors in deck gear, the logistics detachment that had spent four days at sea organising the manifest — moved crates down the gangway and onto the pier. The crates were olive-green plastic, the standard CATF logistics container, each one labelled in English and Korean and, in [[Park Sung-ho|Park]]'s careful handwriting, Eldian.

The Survey Corps leadership assembled on the pier. [[Hange Zoë|Hange]] at the centre, her goggles pushed up on her forehead, her notebook open but her pen — the ballpoint [[Park Sung-ho]] had given her in the mess hall, still the one he had given her in the mess hall — motionless. [[Levi Ackerman|Levi]] to her right, his arms crossed, his expression the expression of a man who was reserving judgement until the crates were open and the contents could be assessed as threats or tools. [[Armin Arlert|Armin]] to her left, his eyes moving across the labels, his mind already cataloguing. [[Mikasa Ackerman|Mikasa]], [[Jean Kirstein|Jean]], [[Connie Springer|Connie]], [[Sasha Braus|Sasha]] — the Scout core, the survivors of Shiganshina, the soldiers who had fought Titans for five years and were now standing on a pier receiving presents from another world.

And at the back of the formation — not in the formation, but near enough to be part of it and far enough to be apart — [[Eren Yeager|Eren]]. He had spoken to no one since arriving. He had not greeted [[Connie Springer|Connie]]. He had not looked at the Tripoli with anything resembling curiosity. He stood with his hands in the pockets of his Survey Corps jacket, his green eyes fixed on the crates with an expression that was not hostile and not curious and not anything [[Park Sung-ho|Park]] could name. He was watching. That was all. He was watching, and he was silent, and his presence on the dock was a weight that everyone in the formation could feel.

[[Floch Forster|Floch]] stood ten metres behind [[Eren Yeager|Eren]], his arms crossed, his expression the same unreadable mask he had worn through every interaction [[Park Sung-ho|Park]] had observed in the past week. He was not part of the formation. He was not part of any formation. He was a man who had survived the suicide charge against the Beast Titan by being the only survivor, and he had been watching [[Park Sung-ho|Park]] with the same intensity for seven days, and now he was watching the crates with the same intensity, and [[Park Sung-ho|Park]] did not know which was worse.

[[Yelena]] was on the cliffside, above the port — a vantage point that let her see everything without being in it. She had not approached the pier. She had not spoken to any CATF personnel. She was filming the event with her eyes, [[Park Sung-ho|Park]] thought, storing every crate and every reaction and every exchange for a purpose she had not disclosed. Her expression was placid. Her posture was relaxed. And [[Park Sung-ho|Park]] remembered the note in his report — *She is waiting for something* — and wondered if this was what she had been waiting for.

#### Scene 2: Morphine — Dockside Warehouse

The warehouse was a converted stone boathouse at the western edge of the harbour — cold, damp, lit by gas lanterns that someone had supplemented with a CATF portable LED work light, the kind that ran on a rechargeable battery and produced a clean white beam that made the Paradisian lanterns look like candles in a jar.

[[Han Ji-won|Petty Officer Han Ji-won]] had set up a folding table near the centre of the warehouse. On the table: two morphine auto-injectors, a bottle of amoxicillin, a bottle of ciprofloxacin, a sealed pouch of povidone-iodine, a sterile bandage in its plastic wrap, a pre-packaged suture kit, and a printed instruction booklet in Eldian — translated by [[Elias Vogler|Vogler]] and [[Frieda]], printed on Grande Terre's single office printer, bound with a plastic comb spine. The booklet was the first medical text in the Eldian language to be printed on Earth paper. It was not a thick book. It was twenty-three pages. It had taken three weeks to translate and two hours to print. And it was about to change Paradisian medicine more than any single drug.

The audience was five Survey Corps field medics — soldiers who had been patching wounds with boiled sewing needles and silk thread for years, who had amputated conscious soldiers with saws and leather straps, who had watched comrades die of infections that a bottle of broad-spectrum antibiotics would have cured in three days. They sat on crates and folding chairs, their expressions wary, their hands still in their laps. They had been told to attend a training session. They had not been told what the training was about. The best of them — a woman named Elise, in her early thirties, with burn scars on her forearms from pulling soldiers out of a burning Titan cooking pot — was watching [[Han Ji-won|Han]] the way a scout watched a new Titan variant: alert, calculating, waiting for the thing to show its nature.

[[Hange Zoë|Hange]] stood at the back of the warehouse. She had not been invited. She had come anyway — the Commander's prerogative, the scientist's hunger. She leaned against a wooden support beam, her arms crossed, her goggles still on her forehead, her eyes on the Petty Officer's hands.

[[Park Sung-ho|Park]] stood beside her. He was not translating — [[Han Ji-won|Han]]'s Eldian was functional, and the booklet existed, and the demonstration was visual. He was watching. Like [[Hange Zoë|Hange]], he was watching.

[[Han Ji-won|Petty Officer Han]] was a hospital corpsman — a Navy medic, not a doctor, but trained in field trauma and pharmaceutical instruction. He had volunteered for this assignment when the manifest was being assembled, because he had read the intelligence brief on Paradisian medical capability and had not slept well afterward. He was young — twenty-six, with the clean-shaven face and squared-away posture of a sailor who had been promoted twice for competence rather than charisma. His Eldian was functional: book-learned from [[Elias Vogler|Vogler]]'s materials, practiced on the transit with the help of a translator's tablet and three hours of daily drills. His accent was terrible. His grammar was worse. His determination was absolute.

"Thank you for being here," he said, in his careful, accented Eldian. "I am Petty Officer Han Ji-won. I am a medic of the Republic of Korea Navy. I am here to show you some of the medical supplies that we are giving to Paradis today. These supplies — " he gestured at the table, " — are not weapons. They are not strategy. They are for suffering. For making suffering less. Do you understand?"

The field medics nodded. Elise, the burn-scarred woman, said: "We understand."

[[Han Ji-won|Han]] picked up the morphine auto-injector. It was a small plastic cylinder, white and blue, the size of a marker pen, with a safety cap on one end and a needle housing on the other. He held it up so everyone could see.

"This is morphine. Morphine is a medicine for pain. Strong pain. The kind of pain that happens when a soldier — " he paused, searching for the word, " — when a soldier's leg is broken. Crushed. When the bone is outside the skin. When the pain is so bad that the soldier cannot think, cannot move, cannot be carried. Do you understand this kind of pain?"

The medics did not answer. They did not need to. Their faces were the answer.

"This device — " [[Han Ji-won|Han]] held up the injector, " — is an auto-injector. It has morphine inside. Pre-loaded. One dose. You remove the safety cap — " he removed the safety cap, " — you press the end against the soldier's thigh, here — " he demonstrated against his own leg, without triggering the needle, " — and you press the button. The needle goes in. The morphine goes in. The pain stops. Not all the pain. But the worst of it. In less than a minute."

The silence in the warehouse was the silence of five people absorbing the fact that the thing they had just been told was possible. Elise spoke first.

"How many doses?"

"Each auto-injector is one dose. We are giving you two hundred injectors. Two hundred doses. More can be provided. This is the first shipment."

Two hundred doses. The number sat in the air. [[Hange Zoë|Hange]], at the back of the warehouse, did not move. But her hand — the hand holding the ballpoint pen — tightened.

[[Han Ji-won|Han]] moved through the rest of the table. The antibiotics. Amoxicillin and ciprofloxacin — broad-spectrum, bottles of one hundred capsules each, with printed instructions in Eldian. He explained the concept of a course — *take two capsules daily until the course is complete, even if you feel better* — and the concept of antibiotic resistance, which required a brief detour into the nature of bacteria, which required a brief detour into the nature of germs, which was a concept that the Paradisian medical establishment had only begun to theorise. The medics listened. They asked questions — sharp, practical questions about dosage, storage, contraindications, the kind of questions that came from people who had been practising medicine without a theory of disease and were now being handed the theory in a twenty-three-page booklet.

The antiseptic. Povidone-iodine, in sealed bottles. [[Han Ji-won|Han]] demonstrated the application — *clean every wound before bandaging, every time, no exceptions* — and the medics absorbed the instruction with the specific intensity of people who had been losing patients to infections they could not see and were now being told that the enemy was visible, nameable, defeatable.

The suture kits. Pre-packaged, sterile, with curved needles and absorbable thread. [[Han Ji-won|Han]] opened one and laid the components on the table — the needle driver, the forceps, the suture material in its sealed inner pouch. Elise picked up the forceps. They were lighter than anything she had ever held. The needle was curved in a way that made her hands — hands that had sutured dozens of wounds with sewing needles — flex in recognition.

"These are for closing wounds," [[Han Ji-won|Han]] said. "The thread dissolves. You do not need to remove it. The needle is pre-curved. It enters the skin at the correct angle. The whole kit is sterile — " he paused. "Do you know sterile?"

"We boil things," Elise said.

"This is better than boiling. This is — " he searched for the word, couldn't find it, switched to the English, " — sealed at the factory. No germs. None. Zero. Open the packet. Use the tools. Throw the packet away. Everything inside is clean."

Elise looked at the suture kit in her hands. Then she looked at [[Han Ji-won|Han]]. Her expression was the expression of a person who had just been told that the thing she had been doing wrong for a decade could be done right, starting now, with the tools in front of her.

"Show me," she said. "Show me on something real."

[[Han Ji-won|Han]] pulled a practice pad from his kit — a slab of synthetic flesh, designed for suture training, the kind of thing that every Navy corpsman learned on in A-school. He laid it on the table. He picked up the needle driver. He demonstrated a simple interrupted suture — needle in, needle out, knot, cut — and his hands were steady and his voice was calm, and the five Paradisian field medics leaned forward and watched, and the gas lanterns flickered against the LED work light, and the warehouse that had once stored fishing boats became, for twenty minutes, a classroom.

[[Hange Zoë|Hange]] watched all of it from the back of the warehouse. She did not speak. She did not take notes — her notebook stayed closed, her pen motionless. She was not cataloguing. She was feeling something that the scientist in her did not have a name for.

[[Park Sung-ho|Park]] had been watching her for a week. He had learned to read her silences. This silence was not calculation. It was something older — something that lived in the space between a Commander's responsibilities and a scientist's curiosity and a human being's capacity for hope.

"They want us to suffer less," [[Hange Zoë|Hange]] said quietly. She said it to herself, not to [[Park Sung-ho|Park]]. But he heard it.

"Yes," he said.

"For a hundred years, we have fought Titans. We have amputated soldiers on tables without anaesthesia. We have watched them die of infections that a bottle of alcohol couldn't stop. We have buried people whose only wound was pain — they gave up. Not because the wound was fatal. Because the suffering was too much. And now — " she looked at the table, at the auto-injectors, at the antibiotics, at the suture kits, at the twenty-three-page booklet printed on paper from another world, " — now a man from a country I cannot find on any map is teaching my medics how to make suffering less. And he is not asking for anything in return except that we listen."

She was silent for a moment. Then she said: "I have been negotiating with your coalition for a week through you. I have been thinking of it as strategy. Deterrence. Trade. Survival. I have been thinking of it the way Erwin would have thought of it — as a calculation, a wager, a move on a board that is larger than any board we have ever played on. But this — " she gestured at the warehouse, at the medics, at the LED work light and the suture kits and the morphine auto-injectors, " — this is not a move on a board. This is a hand reaching across a table. And I have spent so long fighting that I had forgotten what a hand reaching across a table looked like."

[[Park Sung-ho]] looked at her. The Commander of the Survey Corps. The scientist who had studied Titans for a decade and led a military for less than a year. The woman who had negotiated the survival of her species in a dark field and was now watching a Navy corpsman teach her medics how to close a wound.

"Commander," he said. "The hand has been reaching across the table since the night the helicopters landed. You are the first person to take it."

[[Hange Zoë|Hange]] looked at him for a long moment. Then she looked back at the training. [[Han Ji-won|Han]] was demonstrating a subcuticular closure — the technique for wounds that needed to heal without visible scarring. His hands moved with the automatic precision of someone who had done this a thousand times. The medics were taking notes on scraps of paper. Elise was already holding a needle driver, her burn-scarred hands adjusting to the unfamiliar weight.

"When this is over," [[Hange Zoë|Hange]] said, "I want to send my medics to your base. To learn. Properly. For as long as it takes."

"I will recommend it," [[Park Sung-ho|Park]] said.

"That is not a recommendation. That is a request. From the Commander of the Survey Corps to the liaison officer she has been hosting for a week. Write it in your report."

"I will."

The training continued. The LED work light hummed. The gas lanterns flickered. Outside the warehouse, the crates continued to come off the Tripoli, and [[Connie Springer|Connie]] watched a forklift — a machine he had never seen before Grande Terre — carry a pallet of clothing toward the railway terminal, and thought about the petty officer who had taught him to use a shower, and wondered if that petty officer knew that his student was now standing on a Paradisian dock watching his comrades learn the same way he had learned: from strangers who wanted to help.

#### Scene 3: Pens and Notebooks

The pens were in a box of fifty.

The box was olive-green plastic, CATF standard, with a label that [[Park Sung-ho]] had written in careful Eldian: *PENS — WRITING INSTRUMENTS — 50 UNITS + REFILLS*. The refills were in a separate box, smaller, labelled with instructions that [[Frieda]] had translated: *Replace when ink runs dry. Twist to open barrel. Insert refill. Twist to close. Write.*

[[Hange Zoë|Hange]] opened the box on the pier, under the grey autumn sky, surrounded by her command staff and a pile of crates that stretched the length of the dock. She lifted the lid. She looked inside. And she laughed — the same short, surprised laugh that [[Park Sung-ho]] had heard in the mess hall seven days ago, when she had first held a ballpoint pen and declared it a tiny machine for writing.

There were fifty of them. Fifty ballpoint pens, identical to the one [[Park Sung-ho]] had given her. Fifty tiny machines. A lifetime supply.

"Captain Park," she said. "You told me your world had machines for everything. You did not tell me your world had so many machines that it could give away fifty of them as a gesture."

"It is not a gesture," [[Park Sung-ho|Park]] said. "It is a statement. We have so many of these that we do not count them. We make them by the millions. Fifty is a small number for us. We want you to have enough that you stop thinking of them as special and start thinking of them as normal."

[[Hange Zoë|Hange]] lifted one of the pens from the box. She held it the way she had held the first one — carefully, reverently, the way a bomb disposal technician held an unfamiliar device. Then she clicked it. The soft plastic sound echoed on the pier. She clicked it again. She smiled — a real smile, not the Commander's mask, not the scientist's curiosity, but the smile of a person who had just been given something that made her happy for no strategic reason whatsoever.

"Fifty pens," she said. "And notebooks?"

[[Park Sung-ho|Park]] opened the crate beside the pens. Notebooks — leather-bound, Moleskine-style, in three sizes. Blank. Ruled. Waterproof pages. Not the olive-green CATF field journals, but something more civilian, something that felt like it belonged in a study rather than a command post. He handed one to [[Hange Zoë|Hange]].

She opened it. The pages were white — whiter than any paper produced on Paradis, where the pulping process left a faint brown tint that everyone had accepted as the natural colour of paper. She ran her thumb across the edge of the pages. The texture was smoother than anything she had ever touched. She held the notebook up to the grey light and looked at the binding — the stitched signatures, the cloth spine, the leather cover that was softer than a saddle and stronger than a saddle and would outlast any book in her personal library.

"This is beautiful," she said. She said it quietly, the way a person said something that was not meant to be overheard.

"The knowledge you write in it will be more beautiful," [[Park Sung-ho|Park]] said.

"Hange's learning Hangul," [[Jean Kirstein|Jean]] said. He had materialised beside the crate, his arms full of clothing boxes that he was carrying toward the railway terminal and had set down for exactly long enough to make the comment. "Seven days. Fifty pens. Bad handwriting. I've seen the practice sheets."

"My handwriting is improving," [[Hange Zoë|Hange]] said.

"Your handwriting looks like a spider fell in ink and then died on the page."

"The spider was expressing itself."

[[Park Sung-ho|Park]] looked at [[Jean Kirstein|Jean]]. The squad leader was smiling — not the tired flinch he had worn at the first rendezvous, but a real smile, the smile of a soldier who had just watched his Commander be happy about something and was happy about it in return. It was the first time [[Park Sung-ho|Park]] had seen [[Jean Kirstein|Jean]] smile without reservation. It would not be the last time today.

[[Armin Arlert|Armin]] had already taken three notebooks from the crate. He had not asked permission. He had not offered explanation. He had simply seen the notebooks, recognised them for what they were, and claimed three before anyone else could get to the box. He was now holding them against his chest with the same protective grip he had once used to hold classified documents in the war council.

"That's three notebooks," [[Jean Kirstein|Jean]] said.

"There are more in the crate."

"That's three notebooks you haven't written anything in yet."

"I will write things in them. Many things. Important things. You'll see."

"You've had them for forty seconds."

"Forty-one," [[Armin Arlert|Armin]] said. "I've been thinking about what to write for forty-one seconds."

[[Jean Kirstein|Jean]] looked at [[Hange Zoë|Hange]]. [[Hange Zoë|Hange]] looked at [[Armin Arlert|Armin]]. [[Armin Arlert|Armin]] looked at his notebooks and did not offer to return any of them.

"Armin gets three," [[Hange Zoë|Hange]] said. "The rest are distributed by rank and necessity. That is an order."

"That is theft," [[Jean Kirstein|Jean]] said.

"That is command discretion," [[Hange Zoë|Hange]] said. She was still smiling.

#### Scene 4: Books

The books were in a crate of their own.

[[Elias Vogler|Vogler]] had supervised the translation and printing himself. It had been a three-week project — his linguists working with [[Frieda]] and the Eldian engineers to produce accurate, readable translations of five books, printed on Grande Terre's single office printer, bound in hardcover by the base's fabrication shop using materials that had been intended for equipment manuals. The covers were navy blue. The paper was Earth paper — lighter than anything Paradis produced, brighter, the kind of paper that made text feel like it was floating on the page rather than embedded in it. The binding was cloth and board and glue. Each book weighed less than a Paradisian volume of equivalent size. Each book was a physical argument that another world existed and that world wanted Paradis to read.

[[Armin Arlert|Armin]] opened the crate.

He had heard about the books from [[Connie Springer|Connie]], who had been told about them by [[Elias Vogler|Vogler]] during the transit. He had not slept much in the past four days — the anticipation of books from another world had been a kind of hunger that food could not satisfy. He had imagined what they might look like. He had imagined what they might contain. He had not imagined this.

The books were:

**A Basic Medical Textbook** — translated from a Korean field medicine manual, annotated by [[Ahn Jae-hyun|Dr. Ahn Jae-hyun]] to account for Paradisian epidemiological and pharmacological context. Twenty chapters. Four hundred pages. Diagrams of the human circulatory system, the lymphatic network, the bacterial replication cycle. The first comprehensive medical text in the Eldian language.

**An Agricultural Science Primer** — crop rotation, soil nitrogen, irrigation engineering, selective breeding. Translated from a Chinese agricultural extension manual, adapted for Paradisian climate and crop types by an agronomist on the CATF science staff. Three hundred pages. The book that would, within a year, increase Paradisian crop yields by a percentage that no one would believe until the harvest came in.

**A World Atlas of Earth** — full-colour plates, sixty pages, showing the continents of a planet that was identical to Paradis's own in shape and utterly different in politics. An introduction written by [[Elias Vogler|Vogler]] himself, explaining the concept of parallel worlds — the Gate, the inversion, the 112-year offset. Maps of Korea, China, Japan, America. Maps of oceans that had the same names as AOT oceans but different borders. A map of the Antarctic, where Grande Terre was marked with a small red dot and the label *Combined Anomaly Task Force Forward Operating Base*. A map of Paradis, rendered in satellite precision, with a note: *This is your home, rendered by machines from our world. We hope to add your names to our maps, and ours to yours.*

**A History of Science** — from Galileo to the Higgs boson. Four hundred pages. Translated from an English popular-science text, adapted for an audience that had no prior knowledge of the scientific method, the Enlightenment, the industrial revolution, or the concept of peer review. The book that would teach [[Armin Arlert|Armin]] that the world he had been born into was not the only way a world could be.

**And a fifth book** — smaller than the others, thinner, eighty pages. A collection of writings about exploration and discovery. Magellan's circumnavigation. Amundsen's South Pole expedition. Yuri Gagarin's first orbit. The Apollo 11 landing. The epigraph was a quote from a man named Carl Sagan, translated into Eldian by [[Frieda]]: *Somewhere, something incredible is waiting to be known.* The book had been assembled at the last minute — [[Elias Vogler|Vogler]] had argued for its inclusion, because he had read [[Armin Arlert|Armin]]'s intelligence profile and understood that the boy who had dreamed of the ocean while trapped behind Walls would respond to a book about people who had gone further than anyone thought possible.

[[Armin Arlert|Armin]] lifted the exploration book first. He held it in both hands — the navy cover, the white pages, the text that was lighter and sharper than any text he had ever read. He opened it to a random page. The page was about Amundsen's South Pole expedition — the dogs, the sledges, the five men who had reached a point on the planet where every direction was north. He read two paragraphs. He closed the book. He opened it again. He closed it again.

Then he sat down on the crate and began to cry.

He did not make a sound. He did not cover his face. He sat on the crate with the book open in his lap and the tears running down his cheeks and his expression completely still, the way a person cried when the emotion was too large for the face to express.

[[Mikasa Ackerman|Mikasa]] was the first to notice. She moved — not quickly, not obviously, but with the instantaneous protective instinct that had defined her relationship with Armin since they were children. She stood beside him. She did not touch him. She just stood there, a barrier, a presence, her dark eyes scanning the dock for anyone who might intrude.

[[Hange Zoë|Hange]] saw and did not approach. The Commander understood that some gifts required privacy to be received.

[[Levi Ackerman|Levi]] saw and said nothing. His expression did not change. But he did not look away, and the quality of his stillness was the quality of a man who was bearing witness.

[[Armin Arlert|Armin]] sat on the crate for a long time. When he finally looked up, his eyes were red but his voice was steady.

"There are five hundred pages in this crate," he said. "The pages were printed in another world. The knowledge was gathered by people who have been dead for centuries, on a planet that is identical to ours but different. And someone — " he looked at [[Elias Vogler|Vogler]], " — someone translated all of it into our language. For us. So that we could read it. So that I could read it."

"Yes," [[Elias Vogler|Vogler]] said.

"Why?"

"Because you asked the right question at the first rendezvous. Because you are the person on this island who will understand what these books mean. And because — " [[Elias Vogler|Vogler]] paused, " — because the person who writes the future should know what the past looked like."

[[Armin Arlert|Armin]] looked at the book in his lap. The page was still Amundsen. The five men at the South Pole. The point on the planet where every direction was north.

"Thank you," he said. He said it in Eldian, and then, carefully, in English: "Thank you."

[[Elias Vogler|Vogler]]'s expression shifted — a small shift, the shift of a man who had been thanked in his first language by a boy who had learned the word an hour ago. "You're welcome, Corporal."

[[Armin Arlert|Armin]] stood. He gathered the five books into his arms — the medical textbook, the agricultural primer, the atlas, the history of science, the exploration collection. He carried them toward the railway terminal, his back straight, his pace steady, his eyes still red. [[Mikasa Ackerman|Mikasa]] walked beside him. She did not speak. She did not need to.

[[Park Sung-ho|Park]] watched them go. He had been watching the crate of books for most of the unloading — waiting for [[Armin Arlert|Armin]] to find it, because he had known, from the moment the manifest was assembled, that the books were the gift that would land hardest. Not the morphine. Not the pens. Not the coffee. The books. The books were a message that the relationship was not just about survival. It was about knowledge. And knowledge, once given, could not be taken back.

#### Scene 5: Coffee and Chocolate

[[Sasha Braus|Sasha]] found the food crate by smell.

It was not a rational process. She had been helping unload clothing boxes — thermal base layers, waterproof jackets, the kind of garments that were clearly valuable but did not interest her — when a scent cut through the coal smoke and the salt air and the horse-and-leather smell of the Survey Corps formation. Her head came up. Her nostrils flared. She turned in a slow circle, like a hunting dog catching a scent trail, and then she was walking — not running, not yet, but walking with the focused, unstoppable gait of a predator who had locked onto prey.

The crate was at the southern end of the pier, stacked between a pallet of backpacks and a box of solar-powered lanterns. The label, in [[Park Sung-ho|Park]]'s careful Eldian, read: *FOOD — COFFEE, CHOCOLATE, SPICES — PERISHABLE — OPEN SOON.*

[[Sasha Braus|Sasha]] did not read the label. She did not need to. The label was irrelevant. The smell was not.

"Is that — " she said. She was speaking to no one. She was speaking to the crate.

[[Jean Kirstein|Jean]] had been following her at a distance, because he had learned, across five years of friendship, that [[Sasha Braus|Sasha]] following a smell usually meant something was about to be eaten and he wanted to be present when it happened. He caught up to her as she crouched in front of the crate.

"Sasha. You don't know what's in there."

"I know exactly what's in there."

"You don't."

"The nose knows," [[Sasha Braus|Sasha]] said. "The nose always knows."

[[Connie Springer|Connie]] arrived a moment later, drawn by the same gravitational field that pulled anyone who knew [[Sasha Braus|Sasha]] toward any situation involving food. He was carrying a box of merino wool socks that he set down with the careful deliberation of a soldier who had learned, on Grande Terre, that some boxes were worth more than their contents. "That's the food crate. Vogler told me about it. Coffee. Chocolate. Spices."

[[Sasha Braus|Sasha]] looked up at him. Her expression was the expression of a person who had just been told that her god was real and had sent presents.

"Chocolate."

"Yes."

"From another world."

"Yes."

"How much chocolate?"

[[Connie Springer|Connie]] looked at the crate. The crate was medium-sized — a standard CATF logistics container, the kind that held approximately forty kilos of cargo. "I don't know. A lot. Enough that they put it in a separate crate."

[[Sasha Braus|Sasha]] turned back to the crate. She did not have a crowbar. She did not have a knife. She was considering, with the visible intensity of a hunter who had once brought down a deer with a bow and arrow, whether the crate could be opened with her hands.

"Let me," [[Connie Springer|Connie]] said. He produced the multi-tool from his pocket — the Leatherman-style device that [[Elias Vogler|Vogler]] had given him on the Tripoli, the kind that was supposed to be distributed to every Scout at the rendezvous. He had been carrying it for three days and had not yet used it for anything. He unfolded the knife. He cut the packing tape.

[[Sasha Braus|Sasha]] opened the crate.

The coffee was on top — vacuum-sealed bricks, six of them, each one a kilo of dark-roast beans ground and compressed and sealed in foil packaging. The bricks were labelled in Korean and English, with a small Eldian sticker that read: *COFFEE — BREW WITH HOT WATER — ONE SPOON PER CUP*. [[Sasha Braus|Sasha]] lifted one of the bricks. It was denser than bread, heavier than a book, and it smelled like the thing [[Connie Springer|Connie]] had been drinking on Grande Terre for a week — bitter, dark, the kind of smell that made your brain wake up before your body did.

"This is the thing you were talking about," she said to [[Connie Springer|Connie]]. "The drink that tastes like dirt but makes you feel like you've slept."

"It doesn't taste like dirt."

"It tastes like dirt. I can smell it. It smells like dirt that someone set on fire."

"You haven't tried it yet."

"I'm going to try it. I'm going to try all of it." But she set the coffee brick aside, because her hands had found the chocolate.

The chocolate was in insulated packaging — silver foil, the kind of thermal wrap that CATF used for biological samples in transit, repurposed to keep chocolate from melting during the four-day voyage from Grande Terre. [[Sasha Braus|Sasha]] unwrapped the first bar with the reverence of a priest opening a reliquary. The chocolate was dark — seventy percent cacao, a bar from a Korean confectionery that had been requisitioned by CATF logistics in bulk. It was not military chocolate. It was not survival rations. It was the kind of chocolate that someone might buy in a shop because they wanted to eat something that made them happy.

[[Sasha Braus|Sasha]] broke off a piece. She put it in her mouth. And she stopped moving.

The dock continued around her — crates being unloaded, soldiers calling to each other, the steam locomotive venting at the railhead, the gulls crying overhead. [[Sasha Braus|Sasha]] was not part of any of it. She was standing on a stone pier on the eastern coast of Paradis with a piece of chocolate from another world in her mouth, and her eyes were closed, and her hands were still, and she was having a religious experience.

"It's melting," she said. Her voice was quiet. Almost reverent. "It's melting and it tastes like — " she stopped. She did not have words for what it tasted like. There was no word in Eldian for seventy-percent cacao dark chocolate from a Korean confectionery, because there was no thing in Eldian cuisine that corresponded to it. The Paradisian approach to sweetness was honey on bread and fruit when it was in season and a kind of boiled-sugar candy that children bought at village fairs. This was not any of those things. This was something else.

"Like what?" [[Jean Kirstein|Jean]] said.

"I don't know. Like the opposite of salt. Like something that was made to be tasted slowly." She opened her eyes. She looked at the chocolate bar in her hands the way a scholar looked at an ancient text. "There are seventeen more bars in this crate."

"There are actually thirty," [[Connie Springer|Connie]] said.

[[Sasha Braus|Sasha]] looked at him. Then she looked at the crate. Then she looked at the insulated packaging and the silver foil and the bars of chocolate from another world.

"I am going to eat one of these every day until they are gone," she said. "That is not a plan. That is a statement of fact."

"Share them," [[Jean Kirstein|Jean]] said.

"I will share them. I am a generous person. I will share one bar. The rest are for science."

"The science of what?"

"The science of whether chocolate from another world tastes different on different days. It's important research. Commander Hange would understand."

[[Hange Zoë|Hange]], who had been walking past with a notebook in one hand and a box of ballpoint pens in the other, stopped. "I would understand what?"

"Chocolate research," [[Jean Kirstein|Jean]] said.

"I approve of chocolate research. Save me a piece." She kept walking.

[[Sasha Braus|Sasha]] broke off another piece of chocolate. She held it out to [[Jean Kirstein|Jean]]. He took it. He ate it. His expression went through three phases — scepticism, surprise, acceptance — and landed on something that was almost, but not quite, envy.

"That's good," he said. "That's really good."

"I know," [[Sasha Braus|Sasha]] said. She was already unwrapping a second bar.

#### Scene 6: The Remainder — The Quiet Gifts

The rest of the crates were opened over the next three hours. Not every gift got a scene. Not every gift needed one. Some gifts were simply there — physical facts, objects that had travelled eight thousand kilometres across an alien ocean to arrive on a stone pier and change the texture of daily life on Paradis.

**The clothing.** Shirts — plain cotton, black and grey and olive, in sizes that had been estimated from drone-surveillance anthropometry. Underwear — the kind that Paradisian tailors made from linen and elastic substitutes, but lighter, softer, with waistbands that did not chafe. Sport attire — sleeveless shirts, shorts, the kind of garments that Survey Corps soldiers could wear under their ODM harnesses and be grateful for in the summer. Thermal base layers — the same fabric that [[Connie Springer|Connie]] had worn on his morning jog around Grande Terre's harbour, that trapped heat and wicked sweat and weighed almost nothing. Waterproof jackets — lightweight, breathable, with sealed seams and hoods that folded into the collar and zippers that sealed against the rain. The jackets were the most alien garment: Paradisian waterproofing was oilcloth, heavy and stiff and prone to cracking in the cold. These jackets moved like fabric and shed water like oil. [[Levi Ackerman|Levi]] examined one for thirty seconds, said nothing, and put it on. It fit. He did not take it off.

**The backpacks.** Internal-frame, lightweight, with padded shoulder straps and multiple compartments and a waist belt that distributed weight across the hips. The Survey Corps had been carrying their equipment in canvas rucksacks for a century — bags that pulled on the shoulders and soaked through in the rain and wore out in a season. These backpacks were machines for carrying. The Scouts who received them turned them over in their hands, opened every compartment, tugged every strap, and then stood there holding them with the expression of people who had just been given a tool they didn't know they needed.

**The merino wool socks.** Fifty pairs in a bag, with a note in Eldian: *Wash in cold water. Do not wring. Lay flat to dry. Do not put near fire. These will keep your feet warm when wet. They will not shrink if you follow these instructions. If you do not follow these instructions, they will become socks for children.* The note had been written by [[DeSilva]], who had read her own translation and laughed at the last line and decided to keep it.

**The multi-tools.** One for each Scout at the rendezvous. Pliers, knife, screwdriver, bottle opener, file — all folded into a single stainless-steel device that fit in the palm of a hand. [[Levi Ackerman|Levi]] took his, unfolded each tool in sequence, tested the knife against a piece of dock rope, and pocketed it. It was the first gift he had acknowledged with anything other than silence. The acknowledgment was: "Useful." It was the highest praise [[Levi Ackerman|Levi]] had ever given an object.

**The flashlights.** Hand-crank, LED. No batteries. A device that produced light from mechanical motion — the same wonder that the light switch had been for [[Connie Springer|Connie]], now in a form that could be carried into the field. [[Connie Springer|Connie]] demonstrated one for [[Jean Kirstein|Jean]] — the crank turning, the LED flaring, the perfect white beam cutting through the grey afternoon — and [[Jean Kirstein|Jean]]'s expression was the expression of a soldier who had just been told that he would never again need to light a torch on a night operation.

**The solar-powered lanterns.** For the civilian villages, or for field camps. A flat panel that charged in sunlight, a bulb that produced light all night without fuel, without flame, without the smoke that filled Paradisian homes with the smell of burning oil. The lanterns were a direct challenge to Paradis's dependence on finite resources — a machine that harvested energy from the sky and gave it back as light. [[Armin Arlert|Armin]], who had returned from the railway terminal with his books and was now helping distribute the smaller items, held one of the lanterns up to the grey sky and watched the charge indicator flicker.

"Sunlight," he said. "Just sunlight. And then light all night."

"The sun is the same on both Earths," [[Elias Vogler|Vogler]] said. "The technology is just different."

"Everything is different," [[Armin Arlert|Armin]] said. "And the sun is the same. That's — " he paused, " — that's a good thing to build on."

**The spices.** Black pepper, cinnamon, vanilla, chilli — sealed in glass jars, each one labelled in Eldian with suggested uses. Paradisian food was heavily salted but otherwise bland — the culinary tradition of an island that had been cut off from the global spice trade for a hundred years. The jars were small. The contents were potent. And the woman who opened the crate — a Garrison cook named Helene who had been sent to the port to manage the food distribution — smelled the pepper and began to cry. Not the silent tears of [[Armin Arlert|Armin]] on his crate of books. The open, unashamed tears of a cook who had spent her life making food taste of salt and was now holding a jar of something that would make food taste of *elsewhere*.

The remainder unpacking continued through the afternoon. The pile of empty crates on the pier grew. The pile of gifts in the railway terminal grew faster. And through it all, [[Eren Yeager|Eren]] stood at the edge of the dock — not helping, not interfering, not speaking. His hands were in his pockets. His eyes were on the crates. And his silence was the loudest thing on the pier.

#### Scene 7: The Silence on the Dock

[[Park Sung-ho|Park]] found himself standing beside [[Eren Yeager|Eren]] near the end of the unloading. It was not deliberate. He had been walking the length of the pier, checking the manifest, making sure the crate counts matched the shipping log, and when he looked up he was three metres from the Founding Titan.

[[Eren Yeager|Eren]] did not acknowledge him. His eyes were on the Tripoli — the grey hull, the flight deck, the elevator that had carried a captured Titan eight thousand kilometres south to a cage on Grande Terre. The ship that had taken something from Paradis and was now giving something back.

"You are Eren Yeager," [[Park Sung-ho|Park]] said. He said it in Eldian. His accent was still heavy. The words were still placed like stones. But he had learned eight more words in the past week and he was going to use them.

"I know who I am." [[Eren Yeager|Eren]]'s voice was quiet. Flat. The voice of a person who had been asked the same question too many times and had stopped caring about the answer.

"My name is Park Sung-ho. I am the liaison. I have been on your island for seven days."

"I know. I saw you. In the mess. In the yard. You were teaching hand-to-hand."

"You were not there."

"I was watching from the window." [[Eren Yeager|Eren]] turned his head — just slightly, just enough to look at [[Park Sung-ho|Park]] directly. His eyes were green. His expression was empty. Not hostile. Not curious. Empty in the way that a room was empty after the furniture had been removed. "You fight well. For someone who doesn't know what a Titan looks like from the ground."

"I have seen a Titan. On our base. In a cage."

"I know. [[Connie Springer|Connie]] told me. In his letter to Hange. She let me read it." [[Eren Yeager|Eren]] looked back at the Tripoli. "You captured one of my people. You chained it in a box. And now you are giving us chocolate and socks and medicine for pain. Do you think that makes you our friend?"

"No," [[Park Sung-ho|Park]] said. "I think it makes us people who are trying to be your friend. The friendship has not happened yet. The gifts are an offer. You can refuse them."

"I'm not going to refuse them. They'll save lives. My people's lives. I'm not going to refuse something that saves my people's lives." He paused. "But I'm not going to thank you for it either. Because the reason my people's lives need saving is that the world you came from spent a hundred years deciding we were better off dead. And the first thing you did when you arrived was capture a Titan and study it like a specimen. And now you are giving us chocolate. And I am supposed to believe that this is altruism and not leverage."

[[Park Sung-ho|Park]] looked at the boy. Fifteen years old. The Founding Titan. The trigger for the Rumbling. The weapon that half a million Colossal Titans in the Walls were waiting for. And he was standing on a dock in a borrowed jacket, watching an alien warship disgorge crates of gifts, and he was not grateful and he was not hostile and he was not anything that could be predicted from an intelligence briefing.

"You are right," [[Park Sung-ho|Park]] said. "The gifts are leverage. Not leverage against you. Leverage against fear. Our coalition is afraid of the Rumbling. The gifts are a way of saying: we do not want to use our contingency plan. We want to make the contingency plan unnecessary. We want to build a relationship that makes the Rumbling impossible — not because we are stopping it, but because you do not need to use it. The chocolate is not altruistic. It is strategic. But it is also real. The morphine is real. The books are real. The fact that we want to help you is real. And the fact that we are afraid of you is also real. Both things can be true."

[[Eren Yeager|Eren]] was silent for a long time. The unloading continued behind them. The gulls cried. The locomotive vented. The grey sea stretched to the horizon.

"When I was ten," [[Eren Yeager|Eren]] said, "I watched a Titan eat my mother. I could not stop it. I was not strong enough. I have spent five years trying to become strong enough. And now I am the strongest person on this island, and the thing I am strong enough to do is destroy the world. And you are standing here telling me that you want to make it so I never have to. And I do not know whether to believe you or to kill you."

"Which one are you leaning toward?"

[[Eren Yeager|Eren]] looked at him. For a long moment, something moved behind the emptiness in his eyes — something that was not anger and not fear and not hope, but a combination of all three that had not yet resolved into a decision.

"Neither," he said. "Yet." He turned and walked away, toward the railway terminal, toward the crates of gifts that were being loaded onto the train. His silhouette was small against the grey sky. His hands were still in his pockets. And [[Park Sung-ho|Park]] watched him go and thought about the report he would write — the report that would go back to Grande Terre and be read by four governments and become the basis for policy toward the most dangerous person on either Earth.

He did not know what he would write. He knew only that the boy in the borrowed jacket was not what the intelligence briefings described. He was less predictable and more human. And that was worse.

### Movement 3: The Widening

#### Scene 8: Armin — The Atlas at Midnight, Month 1

The candle was burning low. The room was the same room [[Park Sung-ho|Park]] had slept in during his week on Paradis — a cot, a table, a window that looked onto the training yard. It had been reassigned to [[Armin Arlert|Armin]] after [[Park Sung-ho|Park]]'s departure, because [[Armin Arlert|Armin]] had requested a room with a lock and a window and no one else in it, and [[Hange Zoë|Hange]] had understood that the request was not about privacy. It was about books.

The atlas was open to the page that showed the Korean Peninsula.

[[Armin Arlert|Armin]] had been studying it for two hours. His notebook — one of the three he had claimed from the crate, the leather-bound one with the waterproof pages — was open beside the atlas, half-filled with notes in his small, precise handwriting. He had been tracing the coastlines. Comparing the geography of Earth to the geography of his own world — the same landmasses, inverted, the same mountains and rivers and bays, the same shape of the continents viewed through a mirror. The Gate was marked on the map: a red dot in the southern ocean, with coordinates that corresponded to latitude and longitude on a world that was and was not his own.

He had been thinking about what it meant to live on a planet that had a twin. Not a copy. A twin. Identical geography, different history. The same sun. The same moon. The same stars — inverted, the constellations that had guided Paradisian sailors for centuries rearranged into patterns that no Paradisian had ever seen. The same human species, diverged by 112 years and a single cataclysmic difference that had occurred in 1099: Hell opening in Jerusalem, on one Earth, and not on the other.

He turned the page. The Americas. Two continents, north and south, connected by a narrow isthmus that corresponded to — he checked his own world's geography, the maps he had memorised from [[Grisha's Books|the basement]] — a region that was uninhabited on his Earth, unexplored, unknown. On the other Earth, the Americas had been colonised by Europeans. On his Earth, the same landmass was empty. The same shape. The same soil. The same potential. And no one had ever walked on it.

He closed the atlas. He opened the history of science. Galileo. Newton. Darwin. Curie. Einstein. Names that meant nothing to anyone on Paradis except him — and would, if he did his job, mean something to everyone in ten years. He read the chapter on the scientific method — the concept of hypothesis, experiment, falsification, peer review. The concept that knowledge was not something you received from authority. It was something you built, piece by piece, by asking questions and testing answers and being willing to be wrong.

He had been wrong about the world his whole life. He had believed humanity was extinct outside the Walls. He had believed the ocean was the edge of existence. He had believed the Titans were the enemy. And every single thing he had believed had turned out to be incomplete — not false, but partial, a fragment of a truth that was larger and more complicated than any fragment could contain. The scientific method was a way of being wrong that made you less wrong over time. It was the most beautiful idea he had ever encountered.

He wrote in his notebook: *The world is larger than any map. The truth is larger than any belief. The method is to keep asking questions, even when the answers are uncomfortable. Especially when the answers are uncomfortable.*

The candle guttered. He lit a new one — not a candle, but the solar-powered lantern from the crate of gifts, the one that had charged in the afternoon sun and was now producing cool white light without flame. He placed it on the table beside the atlas. The light was steady. The light was clean. The light was from the sun, converted by a machine he did not fully understand, and it would last until morning.

He opened the atlas again. The Korean Peninsula. The country that had sent the ships. The country that had built the machines. The country that was, on his Earth, a stretch of uninhabited coastline with the same shape and a different name. He traced the outline with his finger. He would go there one day. He would stand on the soil of the other Earth and look at the Gate from the far side and understand, finally, what it meant to live in a universe that was larger than one world.

He closed the atlas at 3 AM. He did not sleep. He was still writing in his notebook when the sun rose.

#### Scene 9: Yelena — The Coded Letter, Month 1

The room was in the Volunteer compound — a converted storage building near the port, one of the structures that the Survey Corps had assigned to [[Yelena]] and [[Onyankopon]] and their crew after the defection. The window faced the harbour, where the Tripoli had departed seven days ago and the captured Marleyan cruiser still rode at anchor. The desk was a wooden table with a single drawer. The chair was hard. The light was a gas lamp, because [[Yelena]] preferred gas to the solar lanterns that the Scouts had started distributing to the civilian sectors. Gas was familiar. Gas was controllable. Gas did not run on sunlight that came from machines she did not understand.

She was writing a letter.

The pen was a fountain pen from the crate of gifts — the kind that [[Park Sung-ho|Park]] had included for officers, with bottled ink, as a gesture toward the Paradisian aesthetic. She had not been given the pen. She had acquired it. There was a difference. The paper was Paradisian — rough, brownish, the kind that left a texture under the nib. The ink was black. The words were Marleyan.

The letter was short. It described the second rendezvous in clinical terms: the gifts, the distribution, the reactions of the Survey Corps leadership, the presence of the Tripoli, the absence of a formal treaty signing, the presence of the Founding Titan on the dock, silent. It did not describe [[Yelena]]'s own reaction. It did not need to. [[Yelena]]'s reaction was not the purpose of the letter.

She folded the letter. She sealed it with wax — the seal was blank, no insignia, no identifying mark. She placed it in a drawer that had a false bottom, beneath a stack of Volunteer logistics reports. She did not know when the letter would be delivered. She did not know if it would be delivered at all. The letter was not for now. The letter was for later — for the moment when its contents would be useful, to someone, for something. She was patient. She had been patient for years. She would be patient for years more.

She closed the drawer. She extinguished the gas lamp. She sat in the dark and watched the harbour, where the captured cruiser rode at anchor, and the grey sea stretched to a horizon that contained a world she had not yet figured out how to use.

#### Scene 10: Sasha and Niccolo — Culinary Collision, Month 2

The kitchen was in the Survey Corps headquarters at Trost — a converted mess storage room that [[Niccolo]] had claimed three weeks after his capture and turned into something that resembled, in its disorganised intensity, a professional galley. The stove was Paradisian cast iron. The knives were a mixture of Marleyan steel and Volunteer imports. The ingredients were whatever the Survey Corps supply chain could spare — potatoes, mutton, root vegetables, the occasional fish from the coastal patrols, and, now, a small jar of black pepper, a stick of cinnamon, a vial of vanilla extract, and a sealed pouch of what the label in Eldian called *chilli — use sparingly*.

[[Niccolo]] had been staring at the chilli for ten minutes.

He had tasted it once — a single flake on the tip of his tongue — and his entire understanding of flavour had been recalibrated. The heat was not like horseradish. It was not like the sharp burn of raw garlic. It was something else — a heat that built instead of struck, that lingered instead of faded, that hit the back of the throat and said *this is not from your world*.

[[Sasha Braus|Sasha]] was sitting on a crate in the corner of the kitchen, eating a bar of chocolate that she had hoarded from the rendezvous. She was down to her last four bars. She had been rationing them. The rationing was not going well.

"You're looking at the chilli again," she said.

"I am thinking about the chilli."

"You've been thinking about the chilli for three days."

"The chilli is not from this world. It is from a world that has spices we cannot imagine. And they gave us a jar of it. A single jar. As though it were salt. As though it were ordinary. And I am supposed to cook with it. To put it in food. For people who have never tasted anything spicier than black pepper. Which we also did not have until two weeks ago."

"So don't use it."

"I have to use it. I am a chef. A chef who has been handed an ingredient from another world and told to cook with it. If I do not use it, I am a failure. If I use it wrong, I am a failure. If I use it right, no one will understand what they are tasting because the baseline for 'right' does not exist."

[[Sasha Braus|Sasha]] broke off a piece of chocolate and held it out. "Eat this. You think better when you're not hungry."

[[Niccolo]] took the chocolate. He ate it. He had eaten chocolate from another world fourteen times since the rendezvous — each time a small piece, each time a revelation. The chocolate was a study in contradiction: bitter and sweet, smooth and complex, a flavour that changed as it melted and then changed again. He had tried to replicate it with Paradisian ingredients. He had failed. He was not sure if he was failing because the ingredients were wrong or because the chocolate was impossible to replicate on any world except the one that had made it.

"I am going to make a dish," he said. "With the chilli. And the pepper. And the cinnamon. And the mutton. And I am going to serve it to the Survey Corps leadership. And they are either going to understand that food can be art, or they are going to ask why the meat is burning their mouths. And either way — " he picked up the jar of chilli, " — I am going to know that I cooked with an ingredient from another world. And that is worth any amount of failure."

[[Sasha Braus|Sasha]] smiled — the same smile she had worn when she first tasted chocolate, the smile of a person who had just been told that food was about to get better.

"Save me a plate."

"I always save you a plate."

#### Scene 11: The Railway — Earth-Movers, Month 2

The bulldozer was a Komatsu D65 — twenty tonnes of Japanese engineering, painted CATF orange, shipped from Grande Terre on a chartered freighter with a detached blade and two crates of spare parts. It was the first piece of heavy earth-moving equipment to operate on Paradisian soil. It would not be the last.

The railway spur was a twelve-kilometre extension from the eastern port to the foothills of Wall Maria — terrain that the Paradisian work crews had been cutting by hand for two months. Steam shovels. Pickaxes. Horses dragging carts of gravel. A thousand workers, civilian and military, laying track at a rate of approximately two hundred metres per day. The bulldozer, operated by a CATF engineering detachment led by a Japanese sergeant named [[Saito Hiroshi]], cut the same distance in a morning.

The Paradisian workers had stopped working to watch. They stood at the edge of the track bed — farmers and labourers and Garrison auxiliaries, their hands calloused from months of shovelling — and watched a machine push twelve tonnes of earth in a single pass. They were not afraid. They were not angry. They were watching the way [[Connie Springer|Connie]] had watched the light switch on the Isabu — with the specific, focused attention of people who were being shown that the world was larger than they had been told.

A foreman named Aldo — a stonemason from Wall Rose, fifty years old, whose hands had been laying track since he was twelve — approached the bulldozer when it stopped to refuel. He walked around it. He examined the treads, the blade, the hydraulic lines, the exhaust stack. He did not touch anything. He just looked.

"How does it move?" he asked.

The CATF engineer — a young Korean private named Choi who had learned enough Eldian to explain basic diesel mechanics — pointed at the engine compartment. "Diesel engine. It burns fuel. Like a steam engine burns coal. But the fuel is liquid. The engine turns the fuel into motion. The motion turns the treads. The treads push the earth."

"No horses."

"No horses."

"How much earth can it push in a day?"

"Depends on the terrain. Here, on this grade — " Private Choi looked at the track bed, calculated, " — maybe one kilometre. Maybe more. Two hundred passes with the blade at full angle. By one person."

Aldo looked at the bulldozer. Then he looked at his shovel. Then he looked at the hundred workers behind him, who were still holding their picks and shovels and staring at the machine.

"We've been building railways for two months," he said. "We've laid forty kilometres of track. With a thousand people. Working twelve-hour days. And your machine — "

"One kilometre per day. One person. For the earth-moving. The track-laying still needs people. The levelling. The ballast. The spike-driving. But the digging — " Private Choi gestured at the blade, " — the digging is done."

Aldo was silent for a moment. Then he laughed — a short, rough sound, the laugh of a man who had just realised that his entire profession was about to change.

"Can you teach me to use it?"

Private Choi looked at the foreman. Fifty years old. Hands like stone. Eyes that had been looking at the world for half a century and had just been told that the world looked back.

"Yes," he said. "But it takes time. And the manual is in Korean."

"I don't need the manual," Aldo said. "I need the machine."

#### Scene 12: The Village Clinic — Aspirin, Month 3

The clinic was a single room in Eichenhof — the same village that [[Park Sung-ho|Park]] had visited during his week on Paradis, the same square with the stone well and the blacksmith's forge and the baker's shop. The baker was still there, still arguing about rye prices. The children were still chasing chickens. The clinic had been a storage room before the second rendezvous. Now it was a storage room with a shelf of aspirin bottles, a box of sterile bandages, a bottle of antiseptic, and a doctor — a retired Garrison medic named Helena who had been treating the village for twenty years with nothing but a saw, a needle, and a prayer.

The old farmer's name was Giuseppe. He was seventy-one years old, and his hands had been twisted by arthritis since he was fifty-five, and he had not been able to close his right hand into a fist for sixteen years. He had come to the clinic because his daughter had told him there was new medicine — medicine from the people with the flying machines — and he had nothing left to lose except the pain.

Helena gave him two aspirin tablets and a cup of water.

"What is it?" Giuseppe asked.

"Aspirin. It is a medicine for pain. From the other world. The instructions say one or two tablets, with water, up to three times per day. I am starting you with two."

"Will it work?"

"I don't know. I have never used it before. No one on this island has ever used it before. You are the first person in Eichenhof to take aspirin. Possibly the first person on Paradis outside the Survey Corps." She paused. "If it works, you will owe me a chicken."

"I don't have a chicken."

"Then you will owe me a promise to tell your neighbours that the medicine from the other world works."

Giuseppe took the tablets. He drank the water. He sat on the wooden bench in the clinic and waited.

The pain stopped in twenty minutes.

He did not notice it at first — the way you did not notice a sound until it stopped, the way you did not notice a weight until it was lifted. He was sitting on the bench, talking to Helena about the weather, and then he realised that the thing that had been living in his hands for sixteen years was gone. Not reduced. Not dulled. Gone.

He looked at his hands. He flexed his fingers — slowly, the way a man flexed fingers that had not moved without pain in sixteen years. The knuckles still protested. The swelling was still there. But the pain was absent. The pain — the thing that had defined every interaction he had ever had with the physical world since he was fifty-five years old — was absent.

He closed his right hand into a fist. It was the first time he had done so in sixteen years.

He began to cry.

Helena had seen people cry many times in her twenty years of practising rural medicine on Paradis. She had seen people cry from pain. She had seen people cry from grief. She had seen people cry from relief. This was relief. This was the specific, unguarded relief of a person who had just been told that the thing they had accepted as permanent was temporary, and the cure came from a bottle with a label in a language they could not read.

"I don't have a chicken," Giuseppe said. His voice was shaking. "But I will tell everyone. Everyone in the village. Everyone in the next village. Everyone who will listen."

"Start with your daughter," Helena said. "She's been worried about you for sixteen years."

Giuseppe stood. He flexed his fingers again. He looked at the bottle of aspirin on the shelf — the white plastic bottle, the label in Eldian, the childproof cap that he had not been able to open and Helena had opened for him.

"How much does this cost?" he asked.

"Nothing," Helena said. "The people with the flying machines gave it to us. They said to give it to anyone who needs it. They said they would send more."

"The people with the flying machines," Giuseppe repeated. He was still flexing his fingers. "Are they angels?"

"I don't know," Helena said. "I have never met an angel. But I have met a lot of people who said they would help and didn't. These ones actually showed up."

#### Scene 13: The Liaison Office — Trost, Month 3 (Closing)

The office was a converted shipping container in the Survey Corps headquarters compound — the same kind of container that had been a containment pen for the Titan on Grande Terre, now cleaned, painted grey, fitted with a desk and two chairs and a radio. It was not large. It was not elegant. It was a rectangular box of corrugated steel, eight metres by three, with a door that stuck in the humidity and a window that looked onto the training yard where [[Park Sung-ho|Park]] had sparred with the farm boy three months earlier.

The flag outside the door was the flag of the [[Combined Anomaly Task Force (CATF)|Combined Anomaly Task Force]] — a blue field with four stars, one for each contributing nation, above a stylised Gate that was open. It had been designed by a committee in Seoul and approved by three other committees in Beijing, Washington, and Tokyo, and it was the first flag on either Earth that represented a coalition of nations from one world stationed on the soil of another. It snapped in the autumn wind with the same sound as the Survey Corps banner on the next pole.

The officer behind the desk was [[Park Sung-ho|Captain Park Sung-ho]].

He had rotated back to Paradis two weeks ago — a three-month posting as the first permanent CATF liaison to the Paradisian government. His orders were to maintain communication, coordinate the next phase of technology transfer, and serve as the coalition's eyes and ears on the ground. His real job — the job that was not written on any order — was to be present. To be in the room. To be the person that Hange could call at 2 AM when a report came in that didn't make sense, or that Levi could nod at in the corridor, or that [[Armin Arlert|Armin]] could bring a question to when the question was too large for a notebook.

The office was quiet. The radio crackled with routine traffic from Grande Terre — supply updates, weather reports, the daily situation summary that [[Tanaka Kenji|Tanaka]]'s intelligence section compiled and transmitted to every CATF asset in the AOT world. The window was open. The autumn air was cold. Outside, in the training yard, a new class of Survey Corps recruits was drilling with practice blades, and among them — [[Park Sung-ho|Park]] could see, if he leaned back and looked through the open door — was a young soldier in a CATF-issue waterproof jacket, learning hand-to-hand combat from a Paradisian instructor who had learned it from a Korean Marine three months ago.

The jacket was olive green. The instructor was the farm boy from Chapter 6.1 — the one who had stepped into the sparring square and asked if [[Park Sung-ho|Park]] knew how to fight. He was teaching Krav Maga now. Badly. Enthusiastically. The recruits were falling over and laughing and getting back up. The training yard was the same training yard it had always been — packed dirt, wooden posts, the distant sound of ODM gas releases from the advanced drill course on the other side of the compound. But something had changed. Something that [[Park Sung-ho|Park]] could feel but not name.

He opened his notebook — the same waterproof olive-green notebook he had carried across three months and two worlds, the one that now contained two hundred Eldian words, thirty pages of operational notes, and the bread recipe that the baker in Eichenhof had given him on his second visit. He turned to a clean page. He wrote:

*The liaison office is open. The flag is flying. The radio is working. The relationship is no longer an experiment. It is an institution — small, fragile, dependent on the goodwill of people who have every reason to distrust each other. But institutions are built from small things. A room. A desk. A flag. A person who answers the radio at 2 AM. We have all of those. That is more than we had three months ago.*

He closed the notebook. He looked out the window at the training yard, at the recruits falling and laughing and getting back up, at the instructor who had learned Krav Maga from a stranger and was now teaching it to strangers who would one day be comrades.

The wind moved through the Walls. The flag snapped on its pole. The radio crackled with the voice of a continent that was 8,000 kilometres away and, now, in a small steel box in a compound in Trost, closer than it had ever been.
