# Chapter 07.3 – Langley

## Summary

Three days after [[Levi Ackerman|Levi]]'s body was measured at Grande Terre, the data reaches a different room. The room is a SCIF in Langley, Virginia — sealed, soundproofed, windowless. The three people in it have never met [[Levi Ackerman|Levi]]. They have never been to Paradis. They have never crossed the Gate. They have read a report — fifty-three pages of measurements, graphs, and analyses — and they have drawn a conclusion that the report's author would not recognise. The Ackerman is a weapon. The Ackerman can be controlled. The technology exists, or nearly exists, or can be made to exist if the funding is authorised and the ethics are deferred. This chapter is not long. It does not leave the room. It is a conversation in a SCIF among people who will never be in the same room as the man they are discussing. Their conclusions will not surface for months, or years, or never. But the conclusions exist. The conversation happened. The file exists, somewhere, in a drawer that does not close. And somewhere in a long-term care facility under an Agency cover, a man named Subject Seven sits in a chair with his mouth half-open, and the word that was on his tongue at 11:14 AM will stay there for the rest of his life.

## Scenes

### Scene 1: The SCIF

The room was in the basement of the Original Headquarters Building. It had no windows. It had one door, steel, with a combination lock and a biometric reader that had scanned three sets of retinas before the door opened. The air handling system ran on an isolated circuit — no external ducting, no shared infrastructure. The table was grey laminate. The chairs were black mesh, the kind of chairs that federal procurement contracts delivered by the thousand. On the wall, behind the chair at the head of the table, was a seal — the eagle, the shield, the compass rose. Central Intelligence Agency. The intelligence part, [[Harris Vance|Director Harris Vance]] had learned across thirty years of government service, was the easy half. The central part was the lie. Nothing in this building was central. Everything was a spoke, and most of the spokes did not know the other spokes existed.

Three people sat at the table. Three tablets. One screen on the wall, currently dark.

[[Harris Vance]] opened the meeting at 09:00 precisely. He was fifty-two years old, Senate-confirmed eighteen months earlier, a former inspector general on oversight committees who had learned that the difference between a legal program and a criminal conspiracy was often the thickness of a single signature. He did not open meetings with pleasantries. The people in this room had not been invited for pleasantries.

"Three days ago," he said, "a subject of interest underwent a comprehensive biological battery at the Grande Terre medical facility. The battery was authorised by the Quadripartite Executive Committee under the existing CATF research mandate. The data was shared within standard intelligence-sharing protocols. We received a copy through channels I am not going to specify, because the channels do not exist for the purposes of this conversation."

He tapped his tablet. The screen on the wall lit up. The image was a brain scan — a cross-section of the cerebellum, rendered in blue and orange. The orange overlay was the secondary motor layer. The image was labelled with measurements, density readings, percentile comparisons against a normative database. The subject identifier in the corner was redacted. The name was not on the screen.

"This is the cerebellum of an Ackerman," [[Harris Vance|Vance]] said. "Specifically, a male Ackerman in active military service, age approximately thirty-three. The structure marked in orange does not appear in any normal human brain. It is a secondary motor-processing layer — a superstructure — that processes movement commands independently of the primary motor cortex. The density of the neural tissue is approximately thirty-seven percent above the ninety-ninth percentile of the normative distribution. The volume of the cerebellar cortex is proportionally larger than any individual measurement on record. The structure is not a mutation. It is an addition. It was engineered."

He looked at the two people across the table. Dr. [[Oliver Cross]] — neurologist, DARPA SUBNETS veteran, forty-six years old, no idealism left. Dr. [[Maya Patel]] — neuropsychologist, former Neuralink, thirty-three years old, too much idealism of the wrong kind. He had selected them personally. He had not told them about each other until this morning. The compartmentalisation was not personal. It was procedural. He trusted procedures more than he trusted people, and he trusted people more than he trusted history, and history had taught him that the only safe secrets were the ones that died in the room where they were born.

"You have both read the full report," he said. "Dr. Cross — you flagged the subject as a potential BCI target. Explain why."

---

[[Oliver Cross|Cross]] stood. He was not a man who presented well — his posture was academic, his hands moved when he spoke, his glasses were perpetually smudged. But his voice was steady. The voice of a man who had briefed generals and review boards and colleagues who were smarter than him, and had learned that confidence in the data was more important than confidence in the delivery.

"The standard barrier to brain-computer interface is target acquisition," he said. "The human brain is a diffuse network — billions of neurons, trillions of synapses, no clean boundaries between functional regions. When you place an electrode array in the motor cortex, you are placing it in a region that also processes sensory input, language, abstract reasoning. The signal is noisy. The targeting is approximate. Every BCI implant in history has fought this problem."

He swiped his tablet. The screen changed — a diagram of the Ackerman cerebellum, the superstructure highlighted with callouts and arrows.

"The Ackerman superstructure is the opposite of diffuse. It is a structurally distinct neural region — a second layer, a sheath of tissue draped over the primary cerebellum. It has clear boundaries. It processes a single function: motor coordination. It is electrically active. It is accessible. It is, in effect, a natural landing pad for electrodes — a region of the brain that was *designed* to receive and process motor commands, sitting on the surface where an implant can reach it. No normal human brain provides this target. The Ackerman brain was built with it."

[[Harris Vance|Vance]] studied the diagram. "You are saying the modification that makes him dangerous also makes him controllable."

"I am saying the modification provides access. Whether access translates to control depends on the device architecture. The data suggests three levels of intervention are feasible. I have outlined them in the annex."

"The three-level BCI," [[Maya Patel|Patel]] said. She had not spoken before. Her voice was calm, unhurried — the voice of a person who had been thinking about this conversation for longer than the conversation had been running. "I've read the annex. It's a good architecture. I have some notes on the Medium level."

"Let Dr. Cross finish the architecture," [[Harris Vance|Vance]] said. "Then Dr. Patel will present the psychological assessment. Then we discuss feasibility."

[[Oliver Cross|Cross]] nodded. He advanced the diagram. Three protocol modes appeared — Low, Medium, Total — each with specifications.

"One device," he said. "The hardware is identical across all three modes — a subcranial electrode array targeting the cerebellar superstructure, with secondary leads into the ventral tegmental area and the motor cortex. The modes are software protocols. The same implant does all three. The operator switches protocols based on mission requirements."

He started at the top.

"Surveillance — what we're calling Low. The device reads neural activity from the superstructure in real time. Every motor intention, every preparatory signal, every readiness state is logged and transmitted. The data tells the operator what the subject is about to do before he does it — a seventy-millisecond predictive window, based on his reaction time. That window is tactically significant. It means the operator sees the swing before the blade moves."

"In addition to reading, the device can disrupt. Specifically, it can interfere with the encoding and retrieval of targeted information. If the subject is being interrogated — or is about to volunteer something the operator wants withheld — the device applies a minimal inhibitory signal to the hippocampal-cerebellar pathway. The information is not erased. It is suppressed. The subject knows the thing he wanted to say, but he cannot formulate the words. To an observer, it looks like hesitation. To the subject, it feels like — " he paused. "A senior moment. The word on the tip of the tongue that won't come."

"Damage?" [[Harris Vance|Vance]] asked.

"Minimal. The suppression is transient. Once the signal stops, normal retrieval resumes. The subject would notice a pattern if it happened repeatedly, but that is a tradecraft problem rather than a technology problem. How often you suppress, and in what contexts — that is an operational decision. Not a design constraint."

[[Harris Vance|Vance]] made a note. "And if the subject realises he is being suppressed?"

"Then Low mode becomes Medium mode. You switch protocols. The subject's awareness of the implant is an operational variable, not a system failure. Some operators may want the subject to know he is being monitored. Deterrence has its own value."

[[Oliver Cross|Cross]] advanced to the second level.

"Medium — what we're calling Incentive. The device delivers targeted electrical stimulation to the ventral tegmental area and the insular cortex. Reward and punishment. The stimulation is not a command. It is a compulsion. When the subject performs an action that aligns with the operator's objective, the device delivers a small reward signal — dopamine-level activation, the same neurochemical signature as genuine satisfaction. The subject *feels good* about complying. When the subject resists or diverges, the device delivers an aversive signal — a spike of dysphoria, anxiety, the somatic sensation of being about to make a terrible mistake."

"The subject can still disobey," [[Maya Patel|Patel]] said. She was leaning forward now, her tablet forgotten, her eyes on the diagram. "That's the critical distinction. Medium mode does not override volition. It steers it. The subject remains capable of independent action. The compulsion is strong — the aversive signal can be calibrated to be genuinely painful, in a neurological sense — but a subject with sufficient willpower or sufficient counter-motivation can override it. The risk of disobedience is real. The risk is the price of the subject's retained agency."

"And that is where the psychologist enters," [[Harris Vance|Vance]] said.

"Yes. My assessment — which I will present after Dr. Cross finishes the architecture — suggests that the subject's psychological profile makes Medium mode unusually viable for this specific subject. But I will wait."

[[Oliver Cross|Cross]] advanced to the third level.

"Total — what we're calling Puppet. The device bypasses the subject's decision-making entirely. Direct motor-command signals are sent from the BCI into the cerebellar superstructure and the motor cortex. The subject's body moves. The subject's mind is a passenger."

He paused. The pause was significant. He had practiced it.

"The problem is the superstructure. The Ackerman motor system is designed for autonomous high-speed processing. It receives input from the subject's own sensory and decision-making pathways, integrates the input, and produces motor output at approximately seventy milliseconds. When an artificial signal attempts to override this pathway, the superstructure treats the signal as noise. The natural integration process rejects the foreign input. The result is signal conflict — two competing command streams, the subject's own neural signal and the BCI signal, fighting for control of the motor output."

"And the output?"

"Sluggish. Delayed. The subject's reaction time degrades from seventy milliseconds to approximately four hundred — worse than baseline human. The subject moves slowly, awkwardly, with the specific clumsiness of a body that is receiving contradictory instructions. The subject is fully controlled. But the subject is no longer an Ackerman. The very quality that makes the subject valuable — the hyperdeveloped motor capability — is destroyed by the mode that controls it."

The silence in the room was the silence of three people absorbing an irony.

"You could still use it," [[Harris Vance|Vance]] said. "If the subject was about to do something catastrophic. If the only alternative was letting him act. Total Control would stop him. He would move badly. But he would not move in the direction you wanted to stop."

"Yes. That is the correct framing. Total Control is not an operational posture — it is a failsafe. You use it when the alternative is unacceptable, and you accept the performance penalty as the price of intervention."

[[Oliver Cross|Cross]] placed his tablet on the table. "That is the architecture. One device, three modes. Low for surveillance and information control. Medium for compelled compliance. Total for emergency intervention. The hardware requirements are identical. The software protocol is selected from the operator's terminal. The transition between modes can happen in real time."

"Has any of this been tested," [[Harris Vance|Vance]] said. It was not a question. It was a probe.

[[Oliver Cross|Cross]] hesitated. The hesitation was the length of a calculation — how much to disclose, how much to hold back, how much the Director already knew. He had worked with intelligence-contracted science long enough to know that the Director always knew more than he appeared to.

"There is an existing subject," he said. "Subject Seven. A male in his early thirties. Terminal diagnosis — ALS, early-onset. The original program was a humanitarian offshoot of DARPA's closed-loop paralysis research. The subject volunteered. The implant was placed forty-two months ago. First-generation hardware — a single-array device targeting the primary motor cortex. No reward-punishment capability. No real-time surveillance architecture. The baseline Low mode, essentially — the ability to read motor intention and suppress response. The subject can stop himself from speaking. He can stop himself from moving. He can — with training — prevent himself from forming specific motor sequences that the operator identifies in advance."

"A human who can be muted," Vance said.

"Crudely. The system is more a leash than a BCI. It can prevent action. It cannot compel action. The Medium and Total modes exist in theory, but the hardware was never designed to deliver the required stimulation density. The SUBNETS architecture — the current generation — is a significant upgrade. Subject Seven's hardware is effectively obsolete."

Vance studied him. "Then why did you mention him."

"Because he proves the concept works. The electrode array functioned without rejection for thirty-eight months — the subject experienced electrode degradation in month thirty-nine, consistent with glial scarring, but the core reading-signal path remained intact. The subject can still be muted. The suppression protocol still functions. The proof of concept is real. The cost was two hundred and fifty million dollars. For a single subject."

The number sat in the room. Two hundred and fifty million dollars. A single human being, wired into a machine that could stop him from acting, after forty-two months of development and implantation and clinical maintenance. The number was the reason the program had been shelved. The number was the reason the program was now worth revisiting.

"And the subject's current status," Vance said.

"Alive. Housed in a long-term care facility under an Agency cover. The implant is still active. The maintenance protocols are automated — a quarterly firmware update, a monthly electrode calibration. The cost of keeping the subject alive and the implant functional is approximately sixty thousand dollars per year. The cost of the program was never the maintenance. It was the development. The surgery. The proprietary fabrication of the electrode array. The closed-loop software architecture. The insurance — " Cross paused. "The insurance against litigation. Two hundred and fifty million dollars for one subject. The math never made sense for a conventional soldier."

"And for an Ackerman?"

"For an Ackerman — " Cross looked at the brain scan on the screen. The orange overlay. The superstructure that no normal human brain possessed. "For an Ackerman, the math changes. Two hundred and fifty million dollars for a soldier is unacceptable. Two hundred and fifty million dollars for a weapon that cannot be stopped by any known force on either Earth — a weapon that can kill a Titan, that can kill a Shifter, that can disable any human combatant alive, and that is currently aligned with a foreign power whose long-term intentions we can only guess at — that is a different calculation. The per-unit cost is the same. The strategic value per unit is not. Subject Seven proved the technology works. The Ackerman justifies the expense."

"You are telling me that for one hundred and seventy million dollars — pocket change — we could own him. His body, his speed, his decisions. We could point him at a target and make him want to destroy it. We could stop him from telling anyone what he knows. We could override his limbs and make him walk into a room he doesn't want to enter."

"Yes."

"Then why are we still talking about Subject Seven."

"Subject Seven was an investment in a technology we did not yet know how to monetise. The Ackerman is the first asset that makes the investment worth recouping."

Harris Vance leaned back. He was not a strategist. He was not a patriot. He was a man who had spent thirty years learning that the only thing better than owning a weapon was being the only person who knew you owned it.

"The subject," Vance said. "Subject Seven. Does he know what he is."

"He knows he can be muted. He does not know the technology exists for anyone else. He believes he is the only one — a medical outlier, a proof of concept that was abandoned when the funding ran out. We have not told him about the Ackerman. We will not."

"Good."

[[Harris Vance|Vance]] looked at the screen. The three protocol modes, the specs, the Ackerman constraints. "The Paths," he said. "You mentioned the Paths in the annex as a variable."

"Yes." [[Oliver Cross|Cross]] straightened. "The Ackerman bloodline is connected to an extradimensional neural network — the Paths — that we cannot detect, measure, or model. Every Ackerman receives information through this network. The inherited combat memory that defines Ackerman combat capability is transmitted through the Paths. The immunity protein — the one that blocks the Founding Titan — is a Path-related mechanism. The subject is already interfacing with a supernatural neural architecture. We are proposing to add an artificial one."

"What happens if the two interfaces conflict."

"I don't know. No one knows. The Paths may ignore the BCI signal entirely — different frequencies, different substrates, no interaction. The Paths may amplify the BCI signal — an existing neural interface acting as a carrier wave for the artificial one, making the BCI more effective than it would be in a normal subject. Or the Paths may interfere — the Ackerman protein that blocks the Founder might recognise the BCI signal as an external intrusion and block it at the cellular level. If the latter, the implant is a paperweight inside his skull."

"Which of the three outcomes do you consider most likely?"

"Amplification," [[Oliver Cross|Cross]] said. He said it without hesitation. "The Paths transmit information. They are designed to transmit information. A BCI is information — electrical signals, encoded data, command protocols. The Paths may treat the BCI as just another signal on the network. And if they do — if the Paths carry the BCI signal the same way they carry the Founder's commands — then the implant is not merely functional. It is resonant. The same channel that makes the Ackerman immune to the Founder could make the Ackerman uniquely receptive to an artificial interface."

[[Maya Patel|Patel]] was nodding slowly. "That's an elegant hypothesis. It's also completely untestable until the implant is in the subject."

"Which is why Phase 1 is a cellular model, not a human trial," [[Oliver Cross|Cross]] said. "We culture Ackerman neurons from the germline sample. We expose them to calibrated electrical stimulation. We measure the response. If the cells respond to external stimulation, we know the Paths are not blocking BCI signals. If the cells ignore the stimulation — or actively reject it — we know the Paperweight Hypothesis is correct, and we terminate the program before anyone knows it existed."

[[Harris Vance|Vance]] looked at the screen a long moment. Then he turned to [[Maya Patel|Patel]].

"Dr. Patel. The psychological assessment."

[[Maya Patel|Patel]] stood. She was thirty-three, the youngest person in the room by thirteen years, and she did not carry herself as the youngest person in the room. She carried herself as someone who had been thinking about a problem for longer than anyone else at the table and was about to explain why.

"I have read the subject's profile," she said. "His operational history. His survival record. The psychological assessment compiled from the interviews conducted by the CATF liaison officer and the medical staff at Grande Terre. The profile is not a formal psychological evaluation — the subject has never submitted to a formal psychological evaluation — but it is sufficient for a preliminary assessment."

She advanced the screen. A new image appeared — not a brain scan, but a timeline. The Underground. The Survey Corps. Erwin Smith's death. The decision to give the Titan injection to Armin instead of Erwin. The subjects of loyalty: Hange Zoë, the soldiers under Levi's command, the Survey Corps as an institution, Paradis as a survival imperative.

"The subject's psychological architecture is built for exploitation," she said. "From childhood — his mother's death in the Underground, his formation under a serial killer who taught him that survival is the only law. The subject has spent his entire life trading parts of himself for causes that do not deserve him. His obedience to a dead commander. His body to an institution that burns through soldiers faster than it recruits them. His germline to a science division that will publish his biology for the world to read. The pattern is not generosity. It is vacancy. He does not withhold himself because he does not consider himself worth withholding."

She advanced to a second slide. The slide listed three vulnerabilities.

"One — loyalty transfer. The subject's loyalty is active, not passive. He does not follow orders because he is told to follow orders. He follows orders because he has chosen to trust the person giving them. His loyalty to Hange Zoë is real, but it is contingent — it exists because Hange has proven herself worthy of it. If Hange were to die, or be removed, or lose his confidence, the loyalty would become available for transfer. A new authority figure could claim it."

"Two — utilitarian self-perception. The subject perceives his own body as a tool rather than a self. He submitted to the Grande Terre battery without external pressure — the pressure was internal, the belief that if the testing would help Paradis, the testing was a duty. This perception makes him unusually receptive to medical and biological intervention. He would not resist a procedure he believed was necessary for a cause he believed in. The implantation would not need to be covert, if the justification were framed correctly. If the justification were framed *incorrectly* — if he believed the implant was for Paradisian security rather than American intelligence — he would cooperate."

"Three — the Medium-level sweet spot. Dr. Cross's architecture identifies Medium mode as the most operationally useful but the most psychologically fragile — the subject can still disobey. My assessment is that this subject will disobey *less* than a baseline subject, because his psychological architecture makes him predisposed to compliance with what he perceives as legitimate authority. The BCI would not need to force him. It would need only to steer him — and he would steer, because steering feels like choice, and choice is what he has been making his entire life."

[[Harris Vance|Vance]] was quiet for a moment. Then: "You are describing a psychological profile that makes the subject uniquely vulnerable to exactly the control architecture we are discussing. That is either a remarkable coincidence or a remarkable piece of selection bias."

"It is not a coincidence," [[Maya Patel|Patel]] said. "It is the consequence of a life lived under conditions of extreme duress. Soldiers who survive what this subject has survived develop this architecture or they die. He developed it. He survived. The architecture that kept him alive in the Underground and in the Survey Corps is the same architecture that makes an incentive-based BCI viable. The same qualities that make him valuable as a soldier make him controllable as an asset."

"And if the BCI fails? If Medium mode produces resistance — the disobedience you acknowledge is possible?"

"Then you escalate to Total. Total is sluggish. Total is inefficient. Total defeats the purpose of controlling an Ackerman. But Total will stop him. And the operator will know, within seconds of switching to Total, that the protocol has failed — because the subject will slow down. His speed is the canary. When he stops moving like an Ackerman, you know he is no longer choosing to cooperate."

[[Harris Vance|Vance]] leaned back. He had heard everything. The architecture. The psychology. The Pathways variable. The three modes and their constraints. He had the information he needed to make a decision, and the decision was the same decision he had made before the meeting began, because the meeting was not about the decision. The meeting was about the justification.

"Ladies and gentlemen. You have presented a feasible architecture for a subject who may or may not be biologically receptive to a signal that may or may not be blocked by a supernatural neural network — " he looked at [[Oliver Cross|Cross]], " — and who may or may not be psychologically configured to respond to the compliance protocols — " he looked at [[Maya Patel|Patel]], " — in a way that has never been tested on any human subject, enhanced or otherwise. The uncertainties are significant. The legal exposure is — " he paused. "Significant is not the word. The legal exposure is *undefined*. The program you are describing does not fit any existing authorisation framework. It does not fit any oversight structure. It does not fit any category of activity that the Agency has previously conducted."

He stood. The meeting was not over, but the phase of the meeting where people sat and presented was over. He walked to the dark screen. He stood in front of it — the eagle, the shield, the compass rose behind him.

"I am not authorising a BCI program. That language will not appear in any document, any budget item, any classified annex. What I am authorising is a six-month feasibility study under a codename that will not be associated with any of you. I am calling it [[Project HARNESS]]. The name is deniable. If someone finds it in the budget, it is a muscular-support orthotic research grant. Nothing more. Dr. Cross, you will culture the Ackerman germline cells — the sample from Grande Terre, which we have — and expose them to calibrated electrical stimulation. You will measure the response. You will determine whether the Path-immunity protein blocks external neural signals or specifically the Founder's signal. You will produce a report that says either 'the cellular model responds' or 'the cellular model does not respond.'"

"Dr. Patel, you will produce a companion report. A psychological feasibility assessment — not of this specific subject, but of the general category of subject that this subject represents. The report will not mention the subject by name. It will not mention Ackerman biology. It will describe a theoretical enhanced-human profile and assess the viability of incentive-based compliance protocols for that profile. If anyone asks, the report is a speculative white paper. No operational implications."

"The budget for both reports will be allocated from existing research authorisations. The personnel will be drawn from existing contractor pools. There will be no new hires, no new facilities, no new program codes. If the feasibility study produces negative results — if the cells do not respond, if the psychological assessment concludes that the profile is not compatible with incentive-based protocols — the program terminates. No records. No briefings. The data is archived under a classification that does not permit retrieval. It never happened."

"If the feasibility study produces positive results — " he paused. The pause was the length of a decision that would be made later, by people who were not in this room. "If the results are positive, we discuss Phase 2. The discussion will happen in a different room, with different people, under a different authorisation framework. That discussion is not this discussion. That discussion is not today."

He looked at [[Oliver Cross|Cross]]. He looked at [[Maya Patel|Patel]].

"Are we clear."

"Clear," [[Oliver Cross|Cross]] said.

"Clear," [[Maya Patel|Patel]] said.

"Then this meeting is concluded. Dr. Cross, your report is due in six months. Dr. Patel, the same. I will not contact you before then. You will not contact me. If you require resources, you will route the request through the biological research directorate — the existing one, not a new one. The existing directorate does not know what you are doing. They do not need to know. They will process the request as a standard line item."

He walked to the door. He placed his hand on the biometric reader. The reader beeped. The lock disengaged. He paused at the threshold.

"One more thing," he said. "Elizabeth Cross — the Deputy Secretary of State, our representative on the CATF Quad Committee. She is not read into this. She will not be read into this. If the feasibility study reaches her through any channel — if she asks about Ackerman biological research, if she references a BCI concept, if she uses language that suggests she has gotten wind of this conversation — the program terminates immediately and I was never in this room. Are we clear on that as well."

"Clear," they said.

He left. The door closed behind him. The lock re-engaged. The two consultants were alone in the SCIF with the dark screen and the tablets and the silence of a room that had just hosted a conversation that would never have happened.

[[Maya Patel|Patel]] did not stand immediately. She was looking at the screen — not the dark one, but her tablet, where the Ackerman brain scan was still displayed. The orange overlay of the superstructure. The measurements. The percentile comparisons. The thing that should not exist, and existed.

"Do you think he'll do it," she said. "If the feasibility study passes."

[[Oliver Cross|Cross]] was gathering his materials. He did not look up. "The Director?"

"Yes."

"The Director will do whatever the Director's political calculus says is safe. If the feasibility study passes and the legal exposure is manageable, he will escalate. If the feasibility study passes and the legal exposure is unacceptable, he will terminate. The Director is not a believer. He is a bureaucrat. Bureaucrats do not take risks. Bureaucrats take authorisations."

"And if the authorisation never comes?"

"Then the cells die in a petri dish and the report goes into an archive and the Ackerman goes back to Paradis and none of this ever happened." He looked up. "Is that a problem for you, Dr. Patel? If it never happened?"

She did not answer immediately. She was still looking at the brain scan. The superstructure. The thing that had been built eighteen hundred years ago, by scientists who were dead, for a purpose that was still active. The thing that was now sitting in a petri dish in a laboratory somewhere in this building, waiting to be wired.

"No," she said. "It's not a problem. I'm just curious."

"About what."

"Whether the subject would ever know. Whether he would feel it. The suppression — the Low mode. When we make him forget to say something. Does it feel like anything. Or does it feel like nothing, and he just — doesn't speak."

[[Oliver Cross|Cross]] looked at her. He had not expected curiosity. He had expected complicity, or resistance, or the silence of someone who was already thinking about their next project. But curiosity was better. Curiosity meant she was engaged.

"Subject Seven cannot tell us," he said. "He tries. Sometimes. He opens his mouth. The word is on the surface of his mind. And then the machine adjusts, and the word sinks, and he closes his mouth and stares at the wall. The expression on his face is not confusion. It is the frustration of a man who knows he is being blocked and cannot explain it because the block prevents the explanation. He knows something is wrong. He cannot say what. That is the closest we have to an answer."

"That's elegant."

"It is functional. That is the standard we judge by."

She stood. She picked up her tablet. The brain scan was still on the screen — the orange, the blue, the distinction between normal and never normal. She looked at it a moment longer, then switched the display off.

"The feasibility study," she said, "will probably pass. The cellular model will respond. The Paths will not block the signal. The psychology is textbook. And then we will have a decision."

"Which decision."

"Whether to tell the Director that the Ackerman would consent if we asked. Or whether to let the Director believe we cannot ask."

She walked to the door. The biometric reader beeped. The lock disengaged. She paused at the threshold.

"Consent is a variable," she said, "like any other variable. It can be optimised. It can be bypassed. It can be retroactively constructed. The question is not whether he consents. The question is whether we prefer the efficiency of a willing subject over the security of an unwitting one. I recommend we bypass consent. A subject who does not know the implant exists cannot resist it. A subject who does not know he is being steered cannot steer back. Subject Seven knew he was being muted, and it caused measurable psychological resistance — the frustration, the trying to speak, the failure. The Ackerman would resist harder. The cleaner approach is zero knowledge. The implant goes in. He never knows. He lives his life believing the decisions are his. The betrayal — that word only exists if he knows he has been betrayed. If we take that knowledge off the table, there is no betrayal. There is just a man doing what we want him to do, believing he chose it."

She left. The door closed. The lock re-engaged. The room was empty — the grey table, the black chairs, the dark screen, the seal on the wall. The conversation was over. The file was nowhere. The file was everywhere. The file was in three minds and one petri dish and the body of a dying man in a long-term care facility who had opened his mouth five minutes after his last calibration and closed it again without making a sound.

## Key Decisions

- CIA obtains Levi's Ackerman biological data via ONI intelligence backchannel — bypassing the State Department, the Quad Committee, and Elizabeth Cross
- Three consultants (Vance, Cross, Patel) meet in a SCIF at Langley — none have ever been to Paradis or met the subject
- Cross argues the cerebellar superstructure is a "natural landing pad" for BCI electrodes — a structurally distinct neural region no normal brain provides
- Patel argues Levi's psychological architecture (sacrificial utilitarianism, loyalty transfer, self-as-tool perception) makes Medium-level incentive-based compliance uniquely effective on this specific subject
- Three-tier BCI: Low (surveillance + information suppression), Medium (reward/punishment stimulation — retains subject agency, risk of disobedience), Total (full motor override — sluggish, slow, not Ackermanlike)
- Cross reveals Subject Seven — existing first-generation BCI test subject, terminal ALS patient, $250M development cost, currently housed under Agency cover. Proof that the technology works; the per-unit cost was unacceptable for conventional soldiers but shifts dramatically for an Ackerman
- Cross raises the Paths variable: unknown whether the supernatural neural network will interfere with, amplify, or ignore the BCI signal
- Director Vance authorises nothing — he authorises a feasibility study: six months, cellular modelling only, no written records beyond the room, no program code
- Elizabeth Cross is explicitly excluded — if she learns of the program, it terminates immediately
- Patel recommends bypassing consent entirely — a subject who does not know the implant exists cannot resist it. Consent is a variable like any other variable: optimisable, bypassable, retroactively constructible
