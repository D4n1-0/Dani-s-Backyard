"The control room is the old supervisor's office," he said. His voice was steady. The training had held. "Second level, west wall. We'll set up the monitoring hardware there first. Camera mounts are pre-mapped — teams A through D will handle installation. Cable runs are marked on the schematic. I'll be distributing copies. We break for lunch at thirteen-hundred, resume at fourteen-hundred. Any questions?"

A brief silence — the kind of silence that meant there were questions but no one was asking them first. And then a man stepped forward from the rear of the group, tool bag slung over his shoulder, his jumpsuit worn at the elbows, his face unreadable.

"Moreau," he said. "Senior electrician. Where do you want my team?"

The man was thirty-eight, give or take, with the build of someone who had spent two decades carrying equipment up and down industrial stairwells. His hair was receding in the resigned way of a man who had stopped caring about it in his early thirties. His eyes were the colour of old steel and just as communicative — flat, hard, offering nothing. He had not called Lukas *sir*. He had not called him *Mr. Delacroix*. He had offered his surname and his job title and a question that was technically a question but felt like something else.

Lukas looked at [[Bastien Moreau]] and saw, in the space of a single glance, everything he needed to know. This man had been at Wild Pines longer than Lukas had been alive. This man had wired terminals in three districts. This man had watched a twenty-five-year-old with a Koenigstein degree and a patron's favour get the position that should have been his — or someone like him, some veteran who had done the work and put in the years and never quite caught the eye of the right executive. This man would not cause trouble. He would not be insubordinate. He would do his job, quietly, competently, and every hour of his quiet competence would be an indictment.

"Main power distribution, Mr. Moreau," Lukas said. "The terminal's grid hasn't been maintained in six weeks. I need you to assess the load capacity before we bring the servers online. Your team runs cable from the generator bay to the control room. I've marked the routing on the schematic."

He handed Moreau a copy of the diagram. The electrician took it, glanced at it, folded it once without looking away from Lukas's face.

"Got it," he said. And walked toward the generator bay without another word.

The tension went with him — not resolved, merely relocated. Around the bay, the other technicians had already begun to break into teams, following the schematic's logic. Lukas watched them fan out through the terminal and felt, for the first time since arriving in Martinaise, something like authority. It was borrowed authority. Leased. The same way his body was leased. But it held, and for now, that was enough.

Joyce appeared at his elbow without seeming to have moved. She had a way of doing that — the silent reappearance, the sudden proximity.

"You've met Moreau," she said, quietly enough that no one else could hear.

"You knew he'd be a problem."

"I knew he'd be a test. You passed. He'll be useful. He's very good at his job, and he's too professional to sabotage it. The resentment will keep him sharp. Use it."

She drew on her cigarette. The smoke wreathed her face like an ambiguity. "I'm leaving for Grand Couron within the hour. You have technical control of the site — the hardware, the installation, the engineering timelines. I'll be on the human side: hiring guards, writing the operating protocols, building the policy architecture that makes your equipment enforceable. Two halves of one piece. Progress updates every forty-eight hours. If Evrart makes a move, I want to know before he does." She met his eyes — and for a fraction of a second, almost imperceptibly, her gaze dropped to his waist, to the place beneath his trousers where the cage sat against his skin, and then back up. A reminder. Or a greeting. With Joyce, the two things were identical. "Don't disappoint me, Lukas."

She walked to the waiting company launch without looking back. The boat pulled away from the jetty ten minutes later, slicing through the grey water toward Grand Couron, and Lukas stood on the dock and watched it go until it was a speck on the horizon, indistinguishable from every other ambition that had ever sailed out of Martinaise.

### Scene 5: The Installation — Throughout the Day

The work proceeded with the mechanical rhythm of a well-rehearsed operation. Lukas moved between teams like a conductor between sections of an orchestra — a metaphor that would have occurred to him, had he been the kind of man who thought in metaphors. He was not. He thought in systems.

Team A mounted the first camera above the east loading bay. Team B pulled cable through the overhead conduits, their work lights bobbing in the darkness like fireflies caught in the rafters. Team C unloaded the server racks — black monoliths marked with Wild Pines asset tags — and hauled them up the iron staircase to the control room. Team D, Moreau's team, ran power from the generator bay.

The terminal was transforming. Not into something beautiful, but into something functional — the aesthetic of surveillance, all bare metal and black glass and the soft hum of machines that never slept. The dockworkers' graffiti still covered the walls, but now it was being overlaid with cable runs and mounting brackets, the old language of resistance being rewritten in the new language of control. Lukas watched the two vocabularies intersect and thought, briefly, of palimpsests — ancient manuscripts scraped clean and written over, the old text still faintly visible beneath the new. He had read about palimpsests at Koenigstein. Joyce had given him the book.

At thirteen-hundred hours the teams broke for lunch. The technicians ate in the loading bay, seated on crates and coiled cable spools, their conversations low and work-tired. Lukas ate alone in the control room, standing at the window that overlooked the main floor, watching the men he was supposed to lead. He did not join them. He was not one of them. He was management. He was Joyce's. The distinction was invisible to most of them but not, he suspected, to Moreau.

The electrician was sitting on a crate below, eating a sandwich wrapped in wax paper, talking to no one. His tool bag was at his feet. His expression had not changed since that morning — flat, competent, unreadable. He had run the power diagnostics in forty minutes less than the estimated time. The grid was viable. The servers could come online by end of week. He had delivered the report to Lukas with the same economy of motion he applied to everything: a single sheet of paper, a single nod, and gone.

Lukas understood the mechanism. Moreau was operating at peak professionalism precisely to demonstrate the gap between his competence and Lukas's authority. Every perfect cable run was an argument. Every under-budget power assessment was a question: *what exactly do you do here, Mr. Delacroix, that I could not do better?*

The answer was simple. Lukas had Joyce. Moreau did not. In the corporate calculus of Wild Pines, that was the only answer that mattered.

By fourteen-hundred hours the work had resumed. By seventeen-hundred the control room had monitors — six of them, mounted on articulated arms, their screens still dark but their presence already altering the geometry of the space. A desk had been installed beneath the window. A rolling chair. A filing cabinet for the schematics. It looked, Lukas thought, like the bridge of a small ship — the kind of room where someone sat and watched and made decisions that affected people who would never see the room or know its occupant's name.

The boxes of remaining equipment were stacked along the eastern wall, their inventory tags facing outward, their contents labelled in block capitals: DOOR ACTUATORS – BAY 3, CAMERA MODULES – SPARE, SERVER RACK 2 – PENDING POWER, CABLE REELS – CAT5. The installation was preliminary — the real work would take weeks — but the skeleton was in place. The cage had bones.

### Scene 6: The Resentment — Evening, the Control Room

Lukas was alone in the control room at eighteen-hundred hours, configuring the first monitor's display settings, when the door opened and [[Bastien Moreau]] walked in without knocking. He carried a toolbox and a cable tester and an expression that had not changed since nine that morning.

"Power grid's stable," he said, setting the cable tester on the desk. The desk that was Lukas's desk. "I'm running a second diagnostic in the morning. The old wiring's got corrosion on the junction boxes — nothing critical, but it'll need replacing before the servers draw full load."

"I'll put it on the schedule," Lukas said.

"Good." Moreau didn't leave. He stood in the doorway, the toolbox hanging from his hand, his eyes moving across the control room — the monitors, the desk, the filing cabinet, the man seated behind them — with the slow, deliberate assessment of someone who had been doing this job since before the Coalition occupied Revachol. "You designed this system."

"Yes."

"At Koenigstein."

"In collaboration with Ms. Messier, yes."

"Ms. Messier." Moreau repeated the name as if he were tasting the word for something hidden in it. "She's been with the company — what — seven years? Eight? I've been here fifteen. You know how many project leads I've worked under?"

"I don't."

"Eleven." He let the number sit in the air between them. "Most of them were good. Some of them were bad. All of them had something I didn't. Connections. Degrees. The right handshake at the right fundraiser. I'm not stupid, Mr. Delacroix. I know how the company works. I know how Ms. Messier works. I'm not going to make trouble. I'm going to wire your terminal and test your junctions and file my reports and go home. But you should know — I know. And one day, when whatever you've got with her runs out, and she finds a newer model, and you're standing in a terminal in some district no one's heard of, wondering what happened — that's when you'll understand what eleven project leads taught me."

"What's that?"

"That the cage you're building isn't for the dockworkers. It's for you. It was always for you." He picked up the cable tester. "I'll have the diagnostic by oh-nine-hundred tomorrow."

He left. The door closed behind him with a soft, pneumatic hiss — the kind of sound that was meant to be unobtrusive but that filled a room when there was nothing else to hear.

Lukas sat motionless at the desk. The monitor in front of him displayed a configuration menu — white text on black, the clean emptiness of a machine waiting for instructions. Outside the window, the terminal floor was dark and silent. The technicians had returned to their barracks — temporary housing in a requisitioned warehouse down the coast. The arc lamp was off. The only light in the room was the monitor's pale glow, casting his face in the colours of a system that had not yet gone online.

He thought about what Moreau had said. He thought about the chair on Joyce's boat, the cuffs, the blindfold, the click of the Polaroid, the key around her ankle. He thought about the control room he was sitting in — the same shape, really, as the cabin below deck. A small space with a single occupant and a single purpose: to watch. To document. To control. He had become, in the span of three years, the thing she had trained him to be — and now he was building another one, scaled to industrial dimensions, for people he had never met and would never know.

He reached down, beneath the desk, and adjusted the cage — a small movement, a private adjustment. Six months. He could not remember the last time. That was the point.

The monitor blinked. The configuration menu waited. Lukas Delacroix reached for the keyboard and began to type, and outside the terminal the bay was black and silent, and somewhere in Grand Couron Joyce Messier was undressing for bed with a key still warm against her ankle, and a Polaroid still drying on her nightstand, and the quiet satisfaction of a woman who had built something beautiful and knew exactly what it cost.
