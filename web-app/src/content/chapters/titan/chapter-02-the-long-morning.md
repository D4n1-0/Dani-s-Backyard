# Chapter 2 – The Long Morning

## Summary

The morning after the Gate opens. With the initial shock absorbed, authority shifts from the men on the water to the men in the capitals. In Seoul, Senior Secretary [[Lee Jun-ho]] convenes a crisis meeting at the Blue House, balancing a defence minister who wants to call the Americans with a navy chief who fears three fleets converging on one patch of water. In Beijing, Deputy Director [[Zheng Weimin]] chairs a military-civilian incident group where a PLAN vice admiral pushes for immediate assertion and a Foreign Ministry diplomat urges restraint. The chapter builds toward the first direct conversation between the two men — the Seoul-Beijing hotline call that produces a tentative agreement: no one crosses the Gate unilaterally, a five-nautical-mile exclusion zone enforced by the ships already on scene, and a joint scientific committee to be formed. On the water, [[Park Do-hyun|Park]] and [[Chen Shaoqiang|Chen]] receive their new orders. They are no longer the primary actors. They are something stranger: the first partners of a world that has just become larger.

## Scenes

### Scene 1: The Blue House — Seoul — 06:15 KST

Lee Jun-ho had been in the building since 03:40, which meant he had already been awake when the first albatross crossed through.

He had watched the reports accumulate in real time. The initial dispatch from the Korea Coast Guard command centre in Incheon — a patrol vessel reporting an "anomalous contact of unknown origin." The encrypted follow-up — a description so improbable that the duty officer had asked Lieutenant [[Park Do-hyun|Park]] to repeat his position three times. The satellite tasking request from the National Intelligence Service. The first infra-red imagery, grainy and under-resolved, showing a thermal bloom spreading across the Yellow Sea like a wound bleeding heat into cold water. And then, at 04:52, a single sentence from Incheon that Lee had read four times before he believed it:

*Something crossed through. A bird. Not one of ours.*

The crisis management meeting convened at 06:15 in a windowless room on the second floor of the Blue House. The room was designed for exactly this purpose — soundproofed, electronically shielded, equipped with encrypted video links to every ministry and command centre in the country — and it was the first time Lee had used it for something that was not a North Korean missile launch. The familiarity of the setting was almost comforting. The subject matter was not.

Three people sat at the polished conference table. A fourth appeared on the wall-mounted screen, his connection unstable, his face pixelating every thirty seconds.

Minister of National Defence [[Kim Dong-wook]] had arrived first. He was sixty-three years old, a retired ROK Army lieutenant general who had commanded III Corps along the DMZ before entering government, and he moved through the room the way he had once moved through a command post — taking possession of it by sheer physical presence. He sat at the head of the table because no one had told him not to, and because Lee did not care enough about seating arrangements to start the morning with a territorial dispute.

"Tell me what we know," [[Kim Dong-wook|Kim]] said, before Lee had finished pouring his coffee.

Lee sat. He did not rush. He had learned, across fifteen years of crisis management, that haste was a currency you spent once and could never recover. "At 21:47 last night, a Taegeuk-class patrol vessel — KCG 506 — detected an anomalous radar return in the Yellow Sea. Bearing 0-4-7 relative from its patrol grid, approximately eighty nautical miles southwest of Incheon. The commanding officer, a Lieutenant [[Park Do-hyun|Park]], closed to investigate. He found a hard vertical seam in the sea. Five hundred metres across, two hundred metres high. Half-submerged. The other side appears to be an ocean — different stars, different water composition, different swell patterns. It is open. It is stable. And it is not closing."

[[Kim Dong-wook|Kim]] stared. "You're telling me there is a hole in the ocean."

"I am telling you," Lee said, "what a coast guard lieutenant reported to his chain of command. KIOST has preliminary data that appears to confirm it."

Admiral [[Yoon Seok-jin]], Chief of Staff of the Republic of Korea Navy, leaned forward. He was fifty-seven, a destroyer man who had commanded a Gwanggaeto the Great-class vessel before rising through fleet command, and he spoke the way engineers speak — precisely, without ornament. "The thermal signature. Is it consistent with a sustained geometric structure or a transient event?"

"Consistent with sustained," Lee said. "Dr. [[Ahn Jae-hyun|Ahn]] can speak to the details."

Dr. [[Ahn Jae-hyun|Ahn]] was the fourth man on the screen — the Director-General of the Korea Institute of Ocean Science and Technology, video-conferencing from KIOST's operations centre in Busan. He looked like a man who had been awake for thirty hours. His tie was loosened. His glasses were smudged. His voice, when he spoke, carried the specific exhaustion of someone who had spent the night staring at data that made his entire field of expertise obsolete.

"The sonar returns," [[Ahn Jae-hyun|Ahn]] said, "show a perfectly rectangular discontinuity in the water column. Five hundred metres horizontal. The vertical extent is harder to measure — our sonar can read to the seabed at approximately forty metres, but the thermal gradient suggests the structure extends at least two hundred metres above sea level. The water on the far side is cold. Two to four degrees Celsius, brackish, with a chemical signature consistent with the southern Indian Ocean. The mixing zone is widening. We are seeing Yellow Sea water flowing through at an estimated rate of — "

"How long," [[Kim Dong-wook|Kim]] interrupted, "until we lose the site?"

[[Ahn Jae-hyun|Ahn]] blinked. "Lose it? Minister, it is not moving. The structure appears fixed relative to the Earth's surface. There is no indication that it is expanding, contracting, or degrading. It is simply — there."

"Then it is a national security asset," [[Kim Dong-wook|Kim]] said. "And I want the Navy on site within the hour."

Admiral [[Yoon Seok-jin|Yoon]] did not move. "Minister. With respect. The Chinese are already there."

The room went quiet.

"Explain," Lee said.

"At approximately 22:31 local, a China Coast Guard cutter — Shucha II-class, Haijing 2306, Captain [[Chen Shaoqiang|Chen]] commanding — arrived on scene and established station. The two vessels are maintaining a mutual standoff. Neither has approached the anomaly beyond visual range. Both commanding officers have received hold-and-wait orders from their respective commands. The situation on the water is — for the moment — contained."

[[Kim Dong-wook|Kim]]'s expression did not change, but his knuckles tightened on the table. "The Chinese are sitting on our discovery."

"At fourteen nautical miles from the nearest Korean territorial baseline," Lee said, "the international law of the sea does not give us a clean argument for exclusivity. It gives us an argument for an overlapping Exclusive Economic Zone. Which is the same argument Beijing has been making about these waters for thirty years. The Chinese cutter's presence is legally provocative but not legally invalid."

"I am not asking about the law," [[Kim Dong-wook|Kim]] said. "I am asking about the reality. The reality is our coast guard found it first. The reality is the Chinese are going to steam in with a destroyer and plant a flag unless we stop them. And the reality is we cannot stop them alone."

He looked at Lee.

"Call the Americans."

"The Americans," Lee said, "have already called us."

He produced a tablet from his briefcase and slid it across the table. On the screen was a single paragraph of text — a readout from the US Forces Korea (USFK) liaison channel, timestamped 05:37 KST:

*COMMANDER SEVENTH FLEET ANNOUNCING FREEDOM OF NAVIGATION OPERATION YELLOW SEA. ONE ARLEIGH BURKE-CLASS DESTROYER WILL CONDUCT TRANSIT WITHIN VISUAL RANGE OF REPORTED ANOMALY. ETA 48 HOURS. REQUEST EMBEDDED ROK LIAISON OFFICER FOR OPERATIONAL COORDINATION.*

[[Kim Dong-wook|Kim]] read it twice. Then he smiled — the tired, satisfied smile of a man who had been vindicated.

"Good," he said. "The cavalry is coming."

"The cavalry," Admiral [[Yoon Seok-jin|Yoon]] said quietly, "is a single destroyer with a forty-eight-hour transit window, during which time a Type 055 cruiser and a maritime militia flotilla will already be on station. When the Americans arrive, they will find themselves staring at a Chinese cruiser across a corridor of water that neither side controls. Minister, I have commanded destroyers in these waters for twenty years. Three navies converging on one patch of contested sea is how accidents happen. Accidents become incidents. Incidents become — "

"I am aware of the escalation ladder," [[Kim Dong-wook|Kim]] said.

"Then you are aware," [[Yoon Seok-jin|Yoon]] said, "that calling the Americans is an escalatory step. Beijing will read it as a provocation. They will respond by hardening their posture. Your 'cavalry' may start a war before it arrives."

"Admiral, the Chinese are going to harden their posture regardless. The difference is whether we face them alone or with the Seventh Fleet at our back."

"The difference," Lee said, cutting between them, "is that we have a choice about how this morning unfolds. And that choice is not binary."

Both men turned to him.

"Minister [[Kim Dong-wook|Kim]] is correct that the Chinese will act unilaterally unless we create a countervailing structure. Admiral [[Yoon Seok-jin|Yoon]] is correct that an American destroyer's arrival hardens Beijing's posture and raises the risk of miscalculation. Both of you are describing the same problem from different angles. The question is not whether to call Washington. The question is what we want the Americans to find when they arrive."

He paused. The room waited.

"I want them to find a framework," Lee said. "An agreement. A set of rules that Seoul and Beijing have already negotiated — before the Americans get there. If the US arrives and finds two responsible powers operating under a mutual protocol, they have no reason to escalate. If they arrive and find chaos, they will impose order. And order imposed by the Seventh Fleet is not something we can control."

[[Kim Dong-wook|Kim]]'s jaw tightened. "You want to negotiate with Beijing before Washington arrives."

"I want to negotiate with Beijing *because* Washington is arriving. Their arrival is our leverage. Beijing knows the clock is ticking. If we call Zheng Weimin right now — "

"Zheng Weimin?"

"Deputy Director, Office of the Central Foreign Affairs Commission. He will be running the Chinese response. I have dealt with him before. He is political, not military. He will prefer a deal to a confrontation. But he needs cover if he is going to sell it to the PLA Navy faction in his own room — and the cover is the American destroyer. The fact of its approach makes a negotiated framework more attractive, not less."

[[Kim Dong-wook|Kim]] looked at [[Yoon Seok-jin|Yoon]]. [[Yoon Seok-jin|Yoon]] looked at Lee. The Deputy Minister of Foreign Affairs, who had been silent on the video feed for the duration of the exchange, cleared his throat.

"The hotline," he said. "It is presidential-level. Senior Secretary Lee — you are not the President."

"No," Lee said. "But I am the person in this room who speaks Mandarin. And the President is not awake yet."

He stood.

"I am going to call Beijing. Minister [[Kim Dong-wook|Kim]], I want your authorisation to tell them a Republic of Korea Navy destroyer is also en route for 'observation.' That gives me leverage. Admiral [[Yoon Seok-jin|Yoon]], I need you to ensure that destroyer's orders are to hold position outside a mutually agreed perimeter — not to approach the Gate itself. Dr. [[Ahn Jae-hyun|Ahn]], prepare your data for a joint scientific briefing. If we are going to co-chair a committee with the Chinese, you need to be ready to lead it."

[[Kim Dong-wook|Kim]] nodded — grudging, but sold. [[Yoon Seok-jin|Yoon]] was already reaching for his secure phone. [[Ahn Jae-hyun|Ahn]], on the screen, looked like a man who had just been handed the most extraordinary and terrifying assignment of his career.

Lee left the room.

The hotline terminal was in a neighbouring office — a dedicated Tandberg secure phone with a single-button connection to the Central Foreign Affairs Commission switchboard in Beijing. Lee had used it twice in his career. Both times, the call had gone straight through. Both times, the voice on the other end had already known why he was calling.

He picked up the handset.

He did not dial.

He waited for the information to travel through channels — for Zheng Weimin's people to see the request coming, to brief their principal, to prepare the room on their side. A hotline call was not a phone call. It was a diplomatic event. You did not rush a diplomatic event.

He waited.

On the water, two hundred kilometres southwest, the albatross was still flying.

### Scene 2: The Central Foreign Affairs Commission — Beijing — 06:45 CST (07:45 KST)

Zheng Weimin had been briefed at 05:10. He had convened the incident group by 06:00. They sat now in a tenth-floor conference room in the CFAC compound, a grey mid-rise building in Xicheng District that looked — by design — like every other grey mid-rise building in Xicheng District. The room was spare: a long table, twelve chairs, a single screen on the north wall showing a live satellite feed of the Yellow Sea. The feed was six minutes old. The Gate showed as a thin white line on the thermal overlay — a seam between worlds rendered as a line of false colour on a screen.

Three people sat at the table. A fourth stood by the window, declining to sit, as though sitting would imply permanence and permanence would imply consent.

Vice Admiral [[Zhang Haifeng]] was the one standing. He was fifty-nine, a destroyer man who had commanded a Type 052D and later a flotilla before rising to Deputy Commander of the Northern Theater Command Navy, and he regarded the situation in the Yellow Sea with the same clarity he had once applied to South China Sea operations: territory was territory, and territory not defended was territory conceded. He had already ordered the [[CNS Nanchang (101)]], a 13,000-ton Type 055 cruiser, to make best speed toward the Gate site. A maritime militia flotilla — twenty to thirty fishing trawlers under nominal civilian registration — was assembling at the edge of the EEZ, waiting for instructions.

"Do we know what it is?" [[Zhang Haifeng|Zhang]] asked.

"We know what it appears to be," Zheng said. "A stable portal connecting the Yellow Sea to an unidentified ocean on the far side. The ocean appears to be the southern Indian Ocean — but the stars do not match. The astronomical data is inconsistent with any location on Earth."

"A portal," [[Zhang Haifeng|Zhang]] said, as though the word itself was a contamination. "To where?"

"We do not yet know."

"Then we must secure it until we do."

[[Guo Yong]], Assistant Minister of Foreign Affairs for Asian Affairs, sat at the far end of the table, his hands folded, his posture immaculate. He was fifty-one, had spent seven years at the Embassy in Washington D.C., and spoke with the careful cadence of a man who had learned diplomacy in the country where words moved markets.

"Secure it how, Admiral?"

"I have a Type 055 cruiser en route. She can be on station within — "

"The South Koreans already have a ship there," [[Guo Yong]] said. "So do we. A coast guard cutter. The situation on the water is stable. Two ships, two crews, neither approaching the anomaly. If we steam a warship into that standoff, we change the equation. We escalate from a coast guard incident to a naval confrontation."

"The equation has already changed," [[Zhang Haifeng|Zhang]] said. "The question is who writes the new one."

Dr. [[Wang Lijuan]], Director of the Oceanographic Survey Bureau, sat between them — a civilian in a room of soldiers and diplomats, acutely aware that her institutional rank was the lowest at the table and her actual importance was the highest. She was forty-six, a marine biologist by training, and she had spent the hours since 03:00 analysing water samples relayed from the Haijing 2306's environmental sensors.

"The water on the far side," she said, "has a salinity of 33.8 parts per thousand, a temperature of 2.4 degrees Celsius, and a dissolved oxygen profile consistent with the Antarctic Convergence. It is not the southern Indian Ocean. It is a body of water that resembles the southern Indian Ocean — but the isotopic ratios are wrong. The deuterium excess is off by nearly ten percent. I cannot explain it."

She paused.

"Admiral [[Zhang Haifeng|Zhang]], if you put a warship through that gate — or even within its near vicinity — you will contaminate a scientific sample the size of an ocean. The water mixing is already accelerating. We are losing baseline data with every hour that passes. Whoever controls the science controls the narrative. If we let the Koreans lead the research, they get to define what this is. They get to name it. They get to write the history."

[[Zhang Haifeng|Zhang]] turned from the window. "Dr. [[Wang Lijuan|Wang]], with respect, the history is already being written. It is being written by the fact that a South Korean coast guard vessel arrived first. It is being written by the fact that a United States destroyer will arrive in less than forty-eight hours. If we do not assert control now — physically, militarily — then the international community will write the history without us."

"The international community," [[Guo Yong]] said, "is not an abstraction. It is the White House. It is the State Department. It is the UN Secretary-General, who will call the Foreign Minister within the hour. It is the Japanese Prime Minister's office, which is already drafting a statement. If we steam a cruiser in and lock the site down before any scientific or diplomatic framework exists, we hand Washington exactly the narrative it wants: China is a revisionist power acting unilaterally to seize an asset that belongs to all of humanity. That narrative will cost us more than any temporary military advantage can buy."

"The Americans will spin that narrative regardless," [[Zhang Haifeng|Zhang]] said. "The only variable is whether we face them from a position of strength or a position of deference."

Zheng Weimin let the argument run for another ninety seconds. Then he raised one hand.

The room stopped.

"The Americans," he said, "are not the immediate problem. The South Koreans are. I have just received word that [[Lee Jun-ho]] — Senior Secretary for Crisis Management in the Blue House — is requesting a hotline call. He wants to speak directly."

[[Zhang Haifeng|Zhang]]'s face tightened. "He should be speaking to our Foreign Ministry. Not to you."

"The hotline exists for exactly this kind of situation," Zheng said. "Lee is competent. He will not have made the request without his Minister's authorisation. He will not have made it without something to propose. The question is: what does he want?"

"He wants us to stay on our side of the line," [[Zhang Haifeng|Zhang]] said. "He wants us to recognise their first-responder status. He wants to lock us into a framework where Seoul defines the terms and we accept them. We should not take the call."

"We should take the call," [[Guo Yong]] said, "and we should listen. Lee is asking to speak before the Americans arrive. That means he wants to give us something — or he wants us to give him something — that pre-empts whatever framework Washington will try to impose when its destroyer gets there. Either way, a call costs us nothing. Refusing it costs us leverage."

Zheng looked at [[Wang Lijuan|Wang]].

"Dr. [[Wang Lijuan|Wang]]. If you were sitting across from a South Korean oceanographer right now, what would you ask for?"

She did not hesitate. "A joint scientific protocol. Co-chaired. KIOST and SOA as leads, with other nations as observers. First access to the water-column data. Joint publication rights. If we are going to define what this is, we should define it together — and we should define it before the Americans or the Europeans or the Japanese get a vote."

"And if we define it together," Zheng said, "Seoul gets equal credit. Dr. [[Ahn Jae-hyun|Ahn]] — KIOST's Director-General — will stand next to you at the press conference. His name will be on the first paper. The history will say South Korea discovered the Gate and China co-investigated it. Not that China seized it."

"I can live with that," [[Wang Lijuan|Wang]] said. "Science is collaborative by nature. The alternative — Admiral [[Zhang Haifeng|Zhang]]'s approach — means we get no science at all until the military standoff ends. And military standoffs do not end quickly."

[[Zhang Haifeng|Zhang]] did not reply. He had returned to the window.

Zheng folded his hands on the table. "Admiral. The Type 055 cruiser is en route. We are not recalling it. But neither are we ordering it into the exclusion area. Let it loiter at the perimeter as a statement of presence. That gives you your position of strength. In exchange, I want your assurance that Captain [[Chen Shaoqiang|Chen]] on Haijing 2306 — the man on the water, the one who has been there all night — retains operational command of the immediate Gate vicinity. Not the cruiser captain. The coast guard officer who has been managing the standoff for eight hours without a single shot fired."

[[Zhang Haifeng|Zhang]] said nothing for a long moment. Then: "Chen is PAP. Not PLAN."

"He is Chinese. That is sufficient."

Another long silence. Then a single nod.

Zheng stood. "I will take the call from Lee. Assistant Minister [[Guo Yong|Guo]] — I want you in the room. Dr. [[Wang Lijuan|Wang]] — prepare your scientific framework proposal. I want it in writing within ninety minutes, in both Mandarin and English, for transmission to Seoul. Admiral — your cruiser holds position outside the five-nautical-mile perimeter. No vessel approaches the Gate itself until both governments agree on a protocol."

He walked to the door.

"None of us has done this before," he said. "There is no manual. There is no precedent. The only thing worse than negotiating with the Koreans is not negotiating with the Koreans. Because if we do not — " he paused at the threshold, " — the Americans will."

He left the room.

Behind him, [[Wang Lijuan|Wang]] was already opening her laptop. [[Guo Yong]] was reaching for his secure phone. [[Zhang Haifeng|Zhang]] remained at the window, watching a satellite image of a white line on blue water, his hands clasped behind his back, his expression unreadable.

### Scene 3: The Blue House — Seoul — 08:20 KST

The hotline call had not yet come.

Lee sat in the secure communications office, the Tandberg handset resting on its cradle, waiting. The delay was procedural — Zheng's people would be briefing him, preparing talking points, ensuring alignment with the Politburo Standing Committee. A call of this magnitude could not be taken cold. Lee understood this. It did not make the waiting easier.

At 08:20, the door opened and Admiral [[Yoon Seok-jin|Yoon]] entered. He was carrying a tablet.

"The Chinese Type 055 has altered course," he said. "She was making thirty knots on a direct vector toward the Gate. Two minutes ago she reduced to twelve knots and adjusted bearing. Her new track will bring her to a station approximately seven nautical miles from the Gate — outside what would be a standard five-nautical-mile exclusion perimeter, if such a perimeter existed."

"Which it does not yet," Lee said.

"No. But they are behaving as though it does. Someone in Beijing has given them an order to hold."

Lee allowed himself a thin smile. "Zheng Weimin is running his room."

"Or [[Zhang Haifeng|Zhang]] is running his — and is positioning his cruiser for a sprint. He could be at the Gate in twenty minutes from seven nautical miles if Beijing gives him the green light."

"Then we make sure Beijing never gives him the green light."

The Deputy Minister of Foreign Affairs appeared on the secure video feed on Lee's wall monitor. He was still in his home office — he had not yet made it to the Ministry building — and his face carried the particular tension of a diplomat who had just received a call he did not want to take.

"Senior Secretary. The Americans have just briefed us on the FONOP's rules of engagement (ROE). The Arleigh Burke will approach to within visual range of the Gate — approximately two nautical miles — and conduct a standard transit. They will not cross the threshold. They will not interfere with scientific activity. But they will not request permission. The language was — specific on that point."

Lee nodded. "They are asserting a right, not asking for a favour. That is what freedom of navigation operations do."

"The problem," the Deputy Minister said, "is that the Chinese Type 055 will be watching. And the Chinese Type 055 has orders to hold. If the Arleigh Burke approaches within two miles while the Chinese are holding at seven, Beijing will read it as a deliberate humiliation. The Chinese cruiser captain will feel compelled to respond. We need a protocol *before* the American destroyer arrives — not after."

"We need a protocol before this morning is over," Lee said. "Zheng will call. He is preparing his position. When he does — "

The Tandberg phone rang.

Lee and [[Yoon Seok-jin|Yoon]] exchanged a glance. The Admiral nodded once and stepped back against the wall. The Deputy Minister on the screen went silent.

Lee picked up the handset.

"This is [[Lee Jun-ho]]."

The voice on the other end was calm, deliberate, with the faintest trace of a Henan accent beneath the Mandarin. It was speaking in English — the neutral ground of maritime communication.

*"Senior Secretary Lee. This is Zheng Weimin. I trust you have had as little sleep as I have."*

"Deputy Director [[Zheng Weimin|Zheng]]. I have had exactly the amount of sleep the situation permits. Which is to say — none."

A pause. Then, with what might have been the ghost of a smile:

*"Good. Then neither of us is operating at an advantage."*

### Scene 4: The Hotline — Seoul/Beijing — ~08:25 KST

Lee had prepared for this conversation the way a prosecutor prepares for a cross-examination: not by scripting his lines, but by mapping the ground. He knew what he needed. He knew what he could concede. He knew what Zheng Weimin needed — cover from his own military faction, a framework that let Beijing claim co-leadership without appearing weak. The trick was to give Zheng enough of those things that he could take them back to his room and sell them as victory, without giving so much that Seoul's own position collapsed.

He began with the facts.

"Deputy Director. At this moment, two coast guard vessels — your Haijing 2306 and our KCG 506 — are maintaining station at the anomaly. Neither has approached the threshold. Both commanding officers have received identical hold-and-wait orders from their respective commands. The situation on the water is contained. But it will not remain contained for long. A Chinese Type 055 cruiser is en route. A Republic of Korea Navy destroyer is en route. A United States Navy destroyer is en route. By this time tomorrow, there will be three warships from three nations orbiting the same patch of contested sea. This is not a crisis. It is the prelude to something worse — an accident, a miscalculation, an incident that neither of us wants to explain to our presidents."

Zheng's voice was steady. *"I agree with your assessment. What do you propose?"*

"A mutual exclusion zone. Five nautical miles from the anomaly in all directions. No military vessel enters the perimeter. Enforcement is by the ships already on scene — KCG 506 and Haijing 2306. Their commanding officers — Lieutenant [[Park Do-hyun|Park]] and Senior Captain [[Chen Shaoqiang|Chen]] — have been managing the standoff for nine hours. They know the water. They know each other. They have not escalated. Let them continue."

A pause. *"And the incoming warships?"*

"They hold outside the five-nautical-mile perimeter. Observation only. No approach. The zone is enforced by mutual consent, not by threat of force."

Another pause — longer this time. Lee could hear, faintly, the murmur of voices on Zheng's end. Someone — almost certainly [[Guo Yong]], the diplomat — was speaking in low, rapid Mandarin.

*"Senior Secretary Lee. The exclusion zone is acceptable in principle. But I require clarity on two points."*

"Name them."

*"First. The zone must be a mutual agreement, not a South Korean declaration. The language must be jointly issued — or better, unissued. A quiet handshake between our two offices, communicated to our respective naval commands, with no public announcement until the framework is settled. I do not want a press conference where Seoul announces it has 'established a security zone' and Beijing looks like it was asked to comply."*

"Agreed. No public announcement until we agree on language. The orders go to the ships. The world finds out when we choose to tell it."

*"Second. The scientific investigation. You have KIOST. We have the State Oceanic Administration. I will not accept a framework in which Seoul leads and Beijing follows. We co-chair. Joint access to all data. Joint publication. Joint press briefings. The international community can send observers — Americans, Japanese, Europeans — but they are observers. Not principals."*

Lee had anticipated this. He had discussed it with [[Ahn Jae-hyun|Ahn]] thirty minutes earlier, while waiting for the call. [[Ahn Jae-hyun|Ahn]] had resisted at first — KIOST wanted exclusivity — but Lee had walked him through the logic. A joint committee gave both sides ownership. Ownership gave both sides incentive to maintain the exclusion zone. The exclusion zone kept the Gate open for science. It was a chain of dependencies, and if you broke one link, the whole thing collapsed.

"Co-chairmanship is acceptable. KIOST and SOA as joint leads. First meeting within seventy-two hours. I propose the first session be held virtually — a video conference — with an in-person meeting at a neutral location once logistics allow."

*"Seventy-two hours is aggressive."*

"The water is mixing," Lee said. "Every hour we wait, we lose baseline data. Dr. [[Ahn Jae-hyun|Ahn]] and Dr. [[Wang Lijuan|Wang]] both know this. I suspect they are the only two people in either of our governments who truly understand what is at stake scientifically. Let them speak. Let them build the framework. They will move faster than either of us can."

A silence. Then:

*"I will put [[Wang Lijuan|Wang]] in contact with [[Ahn Jae-hyun|Ahn]] directly. They can begin preliminary coordination within the hour."*

"Good."

*"There is a third matter."*

Lee tensed. "Name it."

*"The United States Seventh Fleet has announced a freedom of navigation operation. An Arleigh Burke-class destroyer will transit to within visual range of the anomaly. I need to know: did Seoul invite this operation?"*

The question was a trap, and Lee knew it. If he said yes, Beijing would read the exclusion zone as a delaying tactic while Seoul waited for American muscle. If he said no, Beijing would read it as a confession of weakness — that Seoul could not control its own ally. Neither answer was safe.

"The United States Seventh Fleet does not request permission from the Republic of Korea to conduct freedom of navigation operations. That is the nature of a FONOP — it is an assertion of navigational rights under international law, not a request for allied coordination. We were informed of the operation. We did not invite it. We do not control it."

*"Can you delay it?"*

"Can you recall your Type 055 cruiser?"

The silence on the line was all the answer Lee needed. Zheng could no more recall a PLAN cruiser than Lee could redirect a US Navy destroyer. Both men were powerful. Neither was omnipotent. The warships were coming whether they agreed or not.

"Then we manage what we can manage," Lee said. "The exclusion zone keeps the warships outside. Your cruiser holds. Our destroyer holds. When the American destroyer arrives, it is met by a functioning framework — two coast guard vessels, two responsible governments, a zone of mutual restraint. The American captain will see a stable situation. He will report back that the situation is under control. And the FONOP becomes a footnote in the after-action report, not a flashpoint on the evening news."

*"You are asking me to trust that an American destroyer captain will behave rationally."*

"I am asking you to trust that three converging navies are safer when the water they are converging on has rules," Lee said. "And I am asking you to help me write those rules before anyone gets hurt."

Zheng Weimin was silent for eleven seconds. On the other side of the Yellow Sea, in a conference room in Xicheng District, a Chinese vice admiral stood at a window and watched a satellite feed and waited for a decision that was not his to make.

Then:

*"Agreed."*

Lee exhaled — quietly, so the handset would not catch it.

"Agreed," he repeated. "I will have the exclusion zone orders transmitted to KCG 506 and forwarded through your North Sea Bureau to Haijing 2306 within thirty minutes. The language will be unilateral in form — each ship receives orders from its own command — but identical in substance. Five nautical miles. No approach. Mutual enforcement."

*"And the scientific framework?"*

"Dr. [[Ahn Jae-hyun|Ahn]] will contact Dr. [[Wang Lijuan|Wang]] within the hour. They will draft terms of reference for a joint committee. KIOST and SOA as co-chairs. First virtual session within seventy-two hours. Observers from Japan, the United States, and relevant international bodies — nominated by mutual agreement."

*"And what do we tell the world?"*

"We tell the world," Lee said, "that two responsible governments are cooperating to understand an unprecedented phenomenon. We tell them nothing else until we have something to report. We control the tempo. We control the narrative. We do not let the warships write the story for us."

A pause. And then, unexpectedly, a sound that Lee had not anticipated: a quiet laugh.

*"Senior Secretary Lee. You and I are going to have a very difficult relationship. But it will be less difficult than the alternative."*

"I will take that as a compliment, Deputy Director."

*"It was intended as one. I will speak to you within the day."*

The line went dead.

Lee set the handset back in its cradle. His hand, he noticed, was trembling. He had not felt it during the call. He felt it now.

Admiral [[Yoon Seok-jin|Yoon]] stepped forward from the wall. "It is done?"

"It is done," Lee said. "A handshake. Nothing more. But a handshake is more than we had an hour ago."

"The Chinese will honour it?"

"Zheng Weimin will honour it. Whether [[Zhang Haifeng|Zhang]] will honour it — " Lee shrugged. "That is why we keep the American destroyer coming. If Beijing breaks the handshake, Washington sees it. The transparency is the enforcement mechanism."

"You just negotiated the first international agreement of the Gate era," [[Yoon Seok-jin|Yoon]] said. "Without presidential authorisation. Without cabinet consultation. On a phone call that lasted six minutes."

Lee looked at him.

"The President," he said, "is still asleep. I will brief him when he wakes. If he fires me, I will have earned it. If he does not — " he stood, " — we have a framework to build."

### Scene 5: The Central Foreign Affairs Commission — Beijing — 08:40 CST (09:40 KST)

Zheng returned to the conference room. The same three people were waiting.

[[Zhang Haifeng|Zhang]] had moved from the window to the table. His posture had shifted — less aggressive, more attentive. The satellite feed on the wall now showed both coast guard vessels and the approaching Chinese cruiser, their positions marked by colour-coded icons on the thermal overlay.

"Well?" [[Zhang Haifeng|Zhang]] asked.

"They agreed," Zheng said. "A five-nautical-mile exclusion zone. Enforcement by the coast guard vessels on scene. No warships enter the perimeter. Joint scientific oversight — KIOST and SOA as co-chairs. First committee meeting within seventy-two hours."

[[Zhang Haifeng|Zhang]] absorbed this without visible reaction. "And the Americans?"

"Are coming regardless. Lee does not control them. I made the point of asking. He made the point of admitting he cannot stop them. The exclusion zone is a rule the American destroyer captain will choose to observe — or not. If he observes it, the framework holds. If he breaches it, the framework was always a fiction."

"And you are comfortable with a framework that depends on American restraint?"

"I am comfortable," Zheng said, "with a framework that gives us six hours of breathing room. The Americans are forty-two hours away. By the time they arrive, the exclusion zone will be a fact on the water. The joint committee will be convened. Our scientists will be working alongside the Koreans. Washington will face a choice: respect the existing arrangement, or break it. Breaking it costs them diplomatically — with Seoul, with the UN, with every middle power watching to see whether the post-Gate world belongs to cooperation or coercion. I believe they will choose cooperation. They are Americans — they prefer to be seen as benevolent."

[[Guo Yong]] nodded. "The narrative is already shifting. The Asian Affairs director at State just messaged my counterpart — they are asking whether Beijing and Seoul are coordinating. The fact that they are asking means the framework is working before it is even announced."

[[Wang Lijuan|Wang]] was already typing on her laptop. "I am drafting a preliminary email to Dr. [[Ahn Jae-hyun|Ahn]]. I propose we begin with a shared water-column profile — temperature, salinity, dissolved oxygen, isotopic ratios. Both vessels have been collecting data since last night. If we pool it, we can build a three-dimensional model of the mixing zone within forty-eight hours. That gives us a baseline before the warships arrive and their wake disturbs the measurements."

"Send it," Zheng said. "And copy Assistant Minister [[Guo Yong|Guo]] for the record. I want a paper trail."

[[Zhang Haifeng|Zhang]] leaned back in his chair. "Deputy Director. A question."

"Ask."

"You called Lee because you believed a deal was better than a confrontation. But you also called him because you believed he was a man you could do business with. Is that correct?"

Zheng considered the question. "I called him because I have dealt with him before. He is competent. He is not ideological. He understands that a crisis of this magnitude is not a zero-sum game — that Seoul and Beijing both lose if they turn the Gate into a shooting match. But you are also correct in your implication, Admiral. I called him because I trust him more than I trust the American destroyer captain I have never met. And I called him because the first intergovernmental call of the Gate era should not be between two militaries — it should be between two men who understand that politics is the art of preventing wars, not the art of winning them."

[[Zhang Haifeng|Zhang]] said nothing. But he did not argue.

"Admiral," Zheng said. "Your cruiser. The orders are firm. She holds outside the perimeter. Observation only."

"I have already transmitted the order."

"Good." Zheng stood. "Then we wait. We watch the water. We prepare the science. And we let Seoul issue the first press statement. They found the Gate. Let them own the discovery. We will own the cooperation."

He walked to the door. This time, no one stopped him.

### Scene 6: The Water — Yellow Sea — 12:00 KST

The morning had aged into noon. The June sun was high over the Yellow Sea, burning off the last of the mist, and the Gate stood in the full glare of day — a silver seam on blue water, visible now for miles, impossible to unsee.

On KCG 506, Lieutenant [[Park Do-hyun|Park]] had just received the transmission he had been waiting for since 01:14. It came through on the encrypted channel, voice only, the operator in Incheon reading from a script that had been approved at a level far above Park's pay grade.

*"KCG 506. Effective immediately. You are designated co-enforcer of a five-nautical-mile exclusion zone centred on the anomaly. No vessel of any nationality is to enter the zone without joint authorisation from Republic of Korea and People's Republic of China chain of command. You will coordinate enforcement with Haijing 2306 via Channel 16 VHF. Incoming Republic of Korea Navy and allied vessels will hold outside the perimeter. Acknowledge."*

Park acknowledged.

He had not slept in fourteen hours. His coffee was cold. His eyes burned. And he had just been told, in the bureaucratic language of his service, that he was now half-responsible for guarding the most important geographical feature on the planet — in partnership with the Chinese captain he had nearly faced off against eight hours earlier.

He keyed the radio on Channel 16.

"Haijing 2306. KCG 506. Lieutenant Park. Do you read?"

A pause. Then the voice of Captain [[Chen Shaoqiang|Chen]], speaking English with a Qingdao accent that Park had already learned to parse:

*"KCG 506. Captain Chen. I read you."*

"I have just received orders establishing a five-nautical-mile exclusion zone around the anomaly. My government requests coordination with yours. Do your orders match?"

A longer pause. Park could picture Chen on his own bridge — the same exhaustion, the same disbelief, the same dawning recognition that the world had changed and they were the men left holding the perimeter.

*"My orders match."*

"Then we are partners," Park said.

*"We are partners,"* Chen said. And then, with something that might have been exhaustion or might have been humour: *"Do not make me regret this."*

Park allowed himself a single laugh — short, surprised, the first laugh he had produced in fourteen hours.

"I will try."

He set the handset down and walked to the bridge wing. Across the water, the Chinese cutter was visible in the distance — a grey silhouette against a grey sea, her running lights dimmed in the daylight. She had not moved. Neither had KCG 506.

In the sky above the Gate, a single bird wheeled — too far to identify, too large to ignore. Park watched it for a moment. Then he turned back to his bridge.

The morning was over. The world had not ended. And somewhere, in conference rooms in Seoul and Beijing, men who had never seen the Gate with their own eyes were already planning what to do with it.

Park did not envy them. He had the easier job. He only had to stand here and watch.
