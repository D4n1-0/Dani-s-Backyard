# Chapter 07 – The Pilgrims

## Summary

The morning after the fleet's assembly, [[James Hawthorne|Captain Hawthorne]] of *[[HMS Dragon]]* sends a signal through the portal to *[[HMS Resolute]]*: a formal parley at the midpoint buoy. The [[Crown of England|Faithful Crown]] delegation crosses in a small launch — surrounded by the hulls of the modern armada, dwarfed and terrified, their tone a single repeated message: *we do not seek conflict.* A third [[Rite of Discernment]], performed by a priest of the Faithful delegation on the modern officers present, confirms what the first two castings established — no infernal taint, no divine mark. Only blankness. A diplomatic exchange is proposed and accepted: [[Malcolm Renfield|Sir Malcolm Renfield]] of the [[United Kingdom (Modern)|Foreign Office]] will cross into Trench Crusade London, while [[Bishop Aldric|Bishop Aldric of Southwark]] will enter the modern world. Transport is provided by modern helicopters crossing the portal — a one-time concession the Faithful delegation accepts reluctantly, still reeling from the scale of what they have seen. As the exchange is arranged, [[Inquisitor Paulus|an inquisitor from New Antioch]] arrives at the portal and demands immediate audience with the modern delegation. [[Malcolm Renfield|Renfield]] flies into a London that has spent eight centuries at war and has normalised cruelty as a form of order — public penance at every crossroads, the [[Office of the Propagation of Virtue]]'s fortress looming over the skyline, and the quiet acceptance of suffering as a price of righteousness. He finds [[Rebecca Osei|Osei]] still there, seven days into her crossing — shaken but steady — and files a report that reads the Crown's self-image against its own streets and finds them irreconcilable. He rejects the offer to stay. In modern London, escorted by a [[Monsignor Franco Ricci|Vatican representative]] and a Foreign Office liaison, [[Bishop Aldric]] walks through a city that has never known war — a secular cathedral of consumerism, a Parliament where no one prays for guidance — and finds the silence more terrifying than any [[Heretic Legion|Heretic]] trench. He accepts the offer to stay. Alone that night in a modern hotel room, staring at a television that speaks without ceasing and without meaning, he begins to wonder if [[The Ark|the ark]] is not a vessel at all. Perhaps it is a test.

## Scenes

### Scene 1: [[The Portal Threshold]] — The Parley

**[TIME]: Day 11 — 2026-06-28, 08:00 Zulu / 1914-09-24, 20:00 TC**

**[POV]: Third Person — [[James Hawthorne|Hawthorne]]*

The signal had gone out at dawn.

[[James Hawthorne|Hawthorne]] had drafted it himself, standing at the communications console on *[[HMS Dragon]]*'s bridge, a cup of tea cooling at his elbow and the grey Atlantic dawn spreading across the portal in layers of pewter and silver. The message was brief — four lines of lamp-coded text that the signals officer had transmitted through the shimmer toward the dark silhouette of *[[HMS Resolute]]* on the other side:

*To Captain, HMS Resolute. Propose formal parley at midpoint of the threshold. Designated location: marker buoy Alpha, coordinates on your side of the distortion. 08:00 your time. We come unarmed. Acknowledge.*

The response had come within the hour — a single lamp flash from the *[[HMS Resolute|Resolute]]*'s bridge wing, the signal officer reading it aloud to the bridge crew:

*Parley accepted. We will attend. We also come unarmed.*

Hawthorne had acknowledged the response, ordered the marker buoy deployed, and spent the remaining hours preparing for a meeting that had no precedent in the long history of naval diplomacy. He would attend personally. A small party — himself, his executive officer, a marine bugler who would never blow his instrument, and a signals officer who had been chosen because he spoke with the patience of a man who had learned that meaning was more important than words. The modern fleet around him had been notified — the [[USS Harry S. Truman (CVN-75)|Truman]], the [[HMS Queen Elizabeth (R08)|Queen Elizabeth]], the French division, the Dutch and Norwegian and Spanish vessels — and they had been instructed to maintain station, to hold emissions, to do nothing that could be read as a threat.

The fleet did not need to do anything. Its existence was the threat.

Hawthorne stood on the bridge wing as his launch was lowered — a rigid inflatable boat (RIB) with a Royal Navy ensign at the stern and a white flag lashed to a short mast at the bow. The launch crew were chosen volunteers: a coxswain who had served in the Gulf, a marine who had served in Helmand, and a signals officer who had served nowhere interesting and was about to change that. They carried no sidearms. The Rules of Engagement, such as they were, stipulated that the parley party was to carry nothing that could be mistaken for a weapon. The gesture was necessary. The logic was thin. If the Faithful delegation chose to attack, Hawthorne's launch crew would defend themselves with their hands, and that would be the end of it.

He climbed down the ladder and took his place at the bow of the RIB. The coxswain throttled up, and the launch pulled away from *[[HMS Dragon]]*'s side, its wake a white thread across the grey water.

The portal loomed ahead — a vertical plane of silvered light, fifty feet high, a mile wide, humming at the edge of hearing. The water beneath it was cold. Hawthorne could feel the temperature drop as they approached, a chill that had nothing to do with the Atlantic wind and everything to do with the fact that they were about to cross into a place that existed on no chart in any admiralty.

The RIB crossed the threshold.

The sensation was subtle — a pressure change in the ears, a flicker of distortion at the edges of vision, and then nothing. They were through. The water was the same grey Atlantic, the sky was the same grey clouds, but the sun had shifted twelve hours in the sky — it was evening on this side, the light slanting from the west with the orange weight of a world that had been fighting Hell for eight centuries and had learned to dread the dark.

And ahead of them, the launch from *[[HMS Resolute]]* was already approaching.

She was a small wooden boat — a cutter, Hawthorne's briefing had called it, a term from a naval lexicon that had been obsolete for a century — rowed by eight oarsmen in the dark blue uniforms of the Faithful Crown's Royal Fleet. At her bow stood a figure in the black-and-silver vestments of the [[Sacred Order of the Dragon]] — a priest whose face Hawthorne did not recognise, younger than [[Father Beauchamp|Beauchamp]], with the hollow cheeks of a man who had been fasting and the careful stillness of someone who was holding himself together by force of will. Behind him, in the stern of the cutter, stood [[Alistair Pembroke|Captain Pembroke]] — his uniform immaculate, his bearing rigid, his eyes fixed on the modern fleet that surrounded them like a wall of grey steel.

Hawthorne had seen fear before. He had seen it in the faces of junior officers before their first deployment, in the eyes of merchant sailors they had rescued from sinking vessels, in the voices of men who had looked at the sea and understood their own insignificance. But he had never seen fear like this. The men in the cutter were not afraid of him. They were afraid of what stood behind him — the two immense carriers, the twelve destroyers and frigates, the aircraft that orbited overhead, the sheer industrial weight of a world that had never known war and had built machines that could end the Faithful Crown's entire civilisation in a single afternoon.

They were afraid, and they were still here. Hawthorne respected that more than he could say.

The two launches drew alongside the marker buoy — a bright orange sphere with a radar reflector, bobbing gently in the grey swell. Hawthorne stood at the bow of his RIB. [[Alistair Pembroke|Pembroke]] stood in the stern of his cutter. The distance between them was ten feet of cold Atlantic water and eight hundred years of theological divergence.

Hawthorne spoke first. He raised his hand in a gesture that he hoped was universal — palm open, unarmed, greeting. "Captain Pembroke. I am Captain James Hawthorne of His Majesty's Ship *[[HMS Dragon|Dragon]]*. Thank you for accepting our invitation."

[[Alistair Pembroke|Pembroke]] inclined his head. His voice was level, but Hawthorne could hear the effort behind the control. "Captain Hawthorne. I am Captain [[Alistair Pembroke]] of His Majesty's Ship *[[HMS Resolute|Resolute]]*, in service to the [[Crown of England]] and the [[Faithful]] Church. We thank you for your —" He paused. The word he wanted was *restraint*, or perhaps *mercy*, and neither word was adequate. "For your forbearance."

Hawthorne understood. The Faithful captain was thanking him for not attacking.

"We have not come to threaten you, Captain Pembroke. We have come to understand. Our governments have authorised us to establish a formal diplomatic channel — a framework for communication that will prevent misunderstandings and reduce the risk of —"

"We do not misunderstand you, Captain Hawthorne." Pembroke's voice was quiet, but it carried across the water with the clarity of a man who had spent forty years giving orders on a bridge wing. "You could destroy us. You could have destroyed us at any moment since your fleet assembled. You have not. That is not a misunderstanding. That is a choice. We wish only to understand why."

Hawthorne considered the question. The easy answer was political — the United Kingdom had not authorised offensive action, the international community had not reached consensus, the legal framework had not been established. But that was not the answer Pembroke was asking for. Pembroke was asking why they had chosen not to destroy the Faithful fleet when they could have. And the true answer was that neither Hawthorne, nor Brennan, nor any captain in the modern cordon had ever considered it an option. To destroy the *[[HMS Resolute|Resolute]]* would be murder dressed as doctrine, and every officer in the fleet knew it.

"Because you are not our enemy, Captain Pembroke. You have never been our enemy. You are a civilisation that has survived eight centuries of a war we cannot imagine, and you have survived it with your faith and your courage and your humanity intact. That is not a threat. That is a miracle."

Pembroke was silent for a long moment. The priest in the cutter's bow studied Hawthorne with eyes that had seen something terrible and were trying to see more. The oarsmen held their strokes, the cutter bobbing gently in the grey swell, the *[[HMS Resolute|Resolute]]*'s crucifix visible in the distance through the shimmer — a dark shape against a different sky.

Finally, Pembroke spoke again. "We have brought a priest. We wish to perform the [[Rite of Discernment]] once more — on you, and on any of your officers who will consent. It is not a test of faith. It is a test of nature. We must know, before we proceed further, whether the soul of your world has changed since our first contact."

"Then perform it," Hawthorne said.

The priest stepped forward in the cutter. He was younger than [[Father Beauchamp|Beauchamp]] — perhaps forty, with the dark hair and pale skin of the Faithful clergy and the thread of silver prayers stitched into the sleeves of his vestments. He carried a small reliquary in one hand and a vial of blessed chrism in the other. He did not cross into the RIB. He knelt at the bow of the cutter, facing Hawthorne across the ten feet of water, and began to pray.

The words were Latin — old Latin, the kind that had not been spoken in Hawthorne's world since the Counter-Reformation. The priest's voice was low and steady, the cadences ancient and precise, each syllable placed with the care of a man who believed that words had weight and that the wrong word could break the world. He sprinkled chrism into the water between the two launches — the salt and the holy water and whatever other consecrated substances the Faithful Church used — and the chrism left a silver residue on the grey surface, spreading in slow spirals like oil.

The prayer continued. Hawthorne felt nothing — no warmth, no cold beyond the chill of the portal, no pressure in his mind beyond the pressure of the moment. The priest's voice rose, fell, rose again. The prayer reached its climax. The priest opened his eyes and looked at Hawthorne.

"Nothing," he said. His voice was not frightened. It was wondering. "As before. No taint. No mark. The soul is empty."

He turned to [[Alistair Pembroke|Pembroke]]. "It is as [[Father Beauchamp|Beauchamp]] reported, Captain. The [[Rite of Discernment]] finds nothing. These people are neither touched by the Adversary nor blessed by the Divine. They are unmarked — as if the war between Heaven and Hell had never reached them."

Pembroke absorbed this. Hawthorne could see him processing it — the relief that the modern world was not infernal, the confusion that it was not divine either, the uncertainty about what that meant for everything the Faithful Crown believed about the nature of creation.

"A blank page," Pembroke said. He was quoting [[Father Beauchamp|Beauchamp]], Hawthorne realised. A phrase that had been passed from the first contact to the second, a description that had become a doctrine. "Every soul bears one mark or the other. That was the teaching of the Synods for eight hundred years. And now we have met a world where that teaching is wrong."

"Or incomplete," the priest said. He was still kneeling at the bow, the chrism drying on his hands, the silver residue catching the evening light. "The Synods knew only our world. They could not have known there was another."

Pembroke said nothing. The implication was too large for the moment — a theological earthquake that would take decades to assess, generations to absorb, and here they were, in a small wooden boat in the middle of the Atlantic, trying to conduct diplomacy while the foundations of their faith cracked beneath them.

Hawthorne brought them back to the practical. "Captain Pembroke. Our governments have proposed a diplomatic exchange. We wish to send an envoy to your London — a senior diplomat from our Foreign Office, Sir [[Malcolm Renfield]], who has been authorised to represent the United Kingdom. In return, we invite a representative of the [[Crown of England]] to visit our London — to see our world as we will see yours. The transport will be provided by our helicopters. They are faster than any ship, and they can cross the threshold."

Pembroke looked at the sky behind Hawthorne — at the modern fleet, at the monstrous silhouette of the *[[USS Harry S. Truman (CVN-75)|Truman]]*, at the aircraft orbiting overhead. The helicopter was only one more impossibility in a week that had been filled with them.

"Your machines. They can fly through the distortion."

"They can fly through anything, Captain Pembroke. We have tested it. The portal is permeable to matter — air, water, steel, human beings. It does not distinguish between the materials of our world and the materials of yours. A helicopter that crosses the threshold will operate in your airspace exactly as it operates in ours."

"And you ask us to board this machine. To trust ourselves to a vessel that flies without sails, without oars, without prayer."

"We ask you to accept our help. You will reach London in two hours instead of eighteen. The decision is yours."

Pembroke looked at the priest. The priest looked at the fleet. The oarsmen held their strokes. The evening light was fading, and the *[[HMS Resolute|Resolute]]*'s signal lanterns were beginning to glow in the gathering dark — stars in a sky that did not belong to Hawthorne's world.

"Captain Hawthorne," Pembroke said. "We have watched your fleet assemble for four days. We have counted your ships — two capital vessels of a type we have never seen, twelve smaller warships, an air wing that could darken the sky. We have understood, slowly and with great difficulty, that we are not your equals in war. We are not your equals in industry. We are not even your equals in the simple arithmetic of force projection. And yet you have come to us — not as conquerors, but as partners. Not with demands, but with an invitation." He paused. "Why?"

Hawthorne had been expecting the question since the parley began. He had prepared an answer. He had rehearsed it in his cabin, in the dark hours before dawn, when the words had seemed adequate.

He discarded it.

"Because we are afraid," he said. "We have a theory about what your world is — an ark, preserved by God against a flood of holy war. And our Archbishop has asked us a question that we cannot answer: what if we are not the cargo but the crew? What if we were hidden not to be saved but to arrive as a rescue? We do not know the answer. But we know that if we are the crew, then we have a duty to you. And if we are not — if we are only the cargo — then we have no idea what we are carrying, or where, or why. Either way, we need to understand. And understanding begins with trust."

Pembroke was silent. The priest crossed himself. The oarsmen shifted in their seats, the cutter's hull creaking with the motion of the sea.

"You speak of trust," Pembroke said. "You ask us to board your flying machine. You offer us your city and ask for ours in return. Very well. We will accept. But I ask one thing in return — one condition, before the exchange proceeds."

"Name it."

"The man you met at the first parley. The priest — Father [[Father Beauchamp|Beauchamp]]. He is on your side of the threshold, aboard one of your warships. I ask that he be returned to us. He has given us the theology that frames this contact. He should be present when that theology is tested."

"He will be returned," Hawthorne said. "I will arrange it personally."

Pembroke nodded once. "Then the exchange will proceed. Your envoy will come to our London. Our bishop — [[Bishop Aldric|Aldric of Southwark]] — will go to yours."

The two captains looked at each other across the ten feet of cold water. The agreement was made. The terms were set. The exchange would begin.

Hawthorne raised his hand once more — palm open, unarmed, farewell. "Until we meet again, Captain Pembroke."

"Until then, Captain Hawthorne. God be with you."

The RIB turned and throttled back toward *[[HMS Dragon]]*. The cutter turned and began the long row back to the *[[HMS Resolute|Resolute]]*. Behind them, the portal shimmered in the grey light, a wall of silver between two worlds, and the weight of everything that had just been agreed settled over the water like a change in the weather.

### Scene 2: *[[HMS Resolute]]* — The Inquisitor

**[TIME]: Day 11 — 1914-09-24, 21:00 TC**

**[POV]: Third Person — [[Alistair Pembroke|Pembroke]]*

The cutter had barely returned to the *[[HMS Resolute|Resolute]]* when the signal came: a vessel approaching from the south, running under the flag of the [[Principality of New Antioch]] — the crossed keys of the Patriarch on a field of white and gold. She was a fast courier, a steam sloop with the lean lines of a blockade runner, and she was making directly for the *[[HMS Resolute|Resolute]]* at a speed that suggested her captain had been ordered not to spare the boilers.

[[Alistair Pembroke|Pembroke]] watched her approach from the bridge wing, the evening wind cold on his face, the taste of the parley still bitter in his mouth. He had expected this. The cable from the [[Office of the Propagation of Virtue]] had been explicit — *We will send our own inquisitor* — and the inquisitor had arrived sooner than Pembroke had hoped. The timing was not accidental. The inquisitor had timed his arrival to coincide with the parley, or immediately after, so that he could insert himself into the diplomatic process before the Crown's representatives had fully established their position.

The sloop pulled alongside the *[[HMS Resolute|Resolute]]* with the precision of a vessel whose crew had been drilled in combat landings. A gangway was lowered. A figure climbed onto the dreadnought's deck — a man in the grey robes of the Office of the Propagation of Virtue, the scholar's robe worn over a naval tunic, the seal of the Office visible on his breast: a book and a sword crossed behind a crowned flame.

He was not what Pembroke had expected. The senior inquisitors of the Office were said to be terrifying — ascetics who had spent decades in the interrogation chambers beneath New Antioch's Cathedral-Patriarchal, men whose faith had been tempered in the crucible of the war against Hell until it was harder than [[Orichalcum]]. This man was not terrifying. He was quiet. He was patient. He carried an Orichalcum-tipped staff and a small reliquary on a chain around his neck, and his eyes — grey as the Atlantic, grey as the harbour at Dover in winter — studied the world with the detached curiosity of a scholar who had learned that truth was more dangerous than any blade.

"Inquisitor [[Inquisitor Paulus|Paulus]]." Pembroke inclined his head. "You are welcome aboard the *[[HMS Resolute|Resolute]]*."

"Captain [[Alistair Pembroke|Pembroke]]." The inquisitor's voice was soft, almost gentle. It was the voice of a man who had spent years asking questions and had learned that the quietest voice often produced the truest answers. "You have just concluded a parley with the other world. I would hear your report."

Pembroke led him to the captain's cabin — the same small room where [[Father Beauchamp|Beauchamp]] had first described the ark, where [[Caius Blackwood|Inquisitor-Admiral Blackwood]] had warned him to watch for when reverence became a cage. He offered the inquisitor a chair, a cup of water, the courtesy of a host who understood that the guest held more authority than the host.

The inquisitor accepted the water. He did not sit. He stood at the cabin's small window, looking out at the darkness beyond — the modern fleet visible through the shimmer, its running lights glowing like a constellation that had fallen into the sea.

"The parley was peaceful," Pembroke said. "The modern captain — a man named [[James Hawthorne|Hawthorne]] — proposed a diplomatic exchange. A modern envoy will visit our London. A Faithful bishop will visit theirs. Transport will be provided by their flying machines."

"Flying machines. You have seen these machines?"

"I have seen their aircraft. They are —" Pembroke paused. The word *impossible* was wrong. He had seen them fly. "They are different from anything we possess. They move with a speed that I cannot describe. They cross the sky like darts across a page."

"And you have agreed to let one of these machines carry a bishop of the Faithful Church into the unknown."

"I have agreed to a mutual exchange. They give us their diplomat. We give them our bishop. The risk is shared."

The inquisitor turned from the window. His grey eyes were unreadable. "The risk is not shared. The risk is asymmetrical. The modern world knows everything about us that they have learned from [[Father Beauchamp|Beauchamp]], from [[Edmund Lassiter|Doctor-Abbot Lassiter]], from their own observations of our fleet. They know our capabilities, our limitations, our theology. We know nothing of theirs. We have seen their ships — but we do not know their intentions. We have heard their words — but we do not know if they speak truth. We have watched their fleet for four days — and we do not know why they have not attacked."

"Beauchamp performed the Rite. He found no taint."

"Beauchamp performed the Rite on two souls — a lieutenant commander and a chaplain — and found blankness. Today, a new priest performed the Rite on the same souls and found the same blankness. The finding is consistent. But a blank page can be written on. The absence of infernal taint is not the same as the presence of divine grace."

Pembroke said nothing. The inquisitor was not wrong. He was simply applying the same rigorous skepticism that the Office of the Propagation of Virtue applied to every theological question — including questions that the [[Crown of England|Crown]] had already answered in its own way. That was the Office's function. That was why the cable had been sent. *Verify before you venerate.*

"What do you intend to do?" Pembroke asked.

"I intend to cross the threshold," the inquisitor said. "I wish to see this other world for myself. Not through the reports of priests who have been overwhelmed by the strangeness of what they witnessed. Not through the theological frameworks of a Synod that has already decided what it believes. I wish to stand on the other side of the portal and see what is there with my own eyes."

"The modern delegation will not refuse you. They have been open to —"

"I do not require their permission, Captain. I am an inquisitor of the Office of the Propagation of Virtue. I answer to the Patriarch of New Antioch, not to the Crown of England, and not to the modern world's governments. I will cross the threshold. I will see what is there. And then I will decide what the Office believes."

Pembroke understood. This was not a request. It was a notification. The inquisitor was asserting the Office's independence — the same independence that [[Caius Blackwood|Blackwood]] had warned him about, the same independence that had prompted the cable, the same independence that would either fracture the Faithful front or strengthen it, depending on what the inquisitor found.

"Very well," Pembroke said. "I will inform Captain [[James Hawthorne|Hawthorne]] that you will be crossing with the next exchange. He will not object."

"I do not expect him to object." The inquisitor turned back to the window. The modern fleet glowed in the darkness, a constellation of running lights and radar arrays and floodlit flight decks, the sheer industrial scale of it pressing against the night like a second sky. "The modern world has been generous with its trust, Captain Pembroke. That makes me suspicious. Generosity without currency is either innocence or calculation. I do not yet know which this is."

"Beauchamp believes they are the ark."

"Beauchamp believes many things." The inquisitor's voice was not dismissive. It was clinical. "Father Beauchamp is a good priest. He performed the first Rite with courage and skill. He formulated the ark theology in good faith — in both senses of the phrase. But he is also a man who has been changed by contact with the modern world. He has given his crucifix to a modern officer. He has spoken at length with a modern chaplain. He has begun to doubt teachings that he accepted without question for forty years. A changed man is not an unreliable witness — but he is a changed witness, and his testimony must be tested."

Pembroke said nothing. The inquisitor was making the same argument that [[Caius Blackwood|Blackwood]] had made in his private warning — *watch for the moment when reverence becomes a cage* — and Pembroke could not decide whether he agreed or whether the warning itself was a cage.

"You will test it yourself, then," Pembroke said.

"I will." The inquisitor turned from the window one final time. His grey eyes met Pembroke's, and for a moment the scholar's detachment slipped and Pembroke saw something else beneath — something older, harder, the thing that had been forged in the interrogation chambers beneath New Antioch and had learned to recognise truth the way a blade recognises whetstone.

"And if what I find contradicts what you have found, Captain Pembroke — if the modern world is not what it appears to be — then the Crown's theology, the Synod's declaration, the Royal Decree of Hospitality, and everything [[Father Beauchamp|Beauchamp]] has told you will be re-examined. Coldly. Methodically. Without sentiment." He paused. "Do you understand what that means?"

Pembroke understood. It meant that the Office of the Propagation of Virtue was preparing for the possibility that the modern world was not the ark. That the blank souls were a deception. That the trust the modern delegation had extended was a weapon, and that the Faithful Church had been invited to walk into an ambush with open arms and a hymn on its lips.

"I understand," Pembroke said.

"Good." The inquisitor inclined his head — a gesture that was almost a bow, almost a blessing, almost a threat. "Then I will prepare to cross."

He left the cabin. Pembroke remained alone, the sound of the inquisitor's footsteps fading down the companionway, the weight of the conversation settling over him like the cold that leaked from the portal. Outside, the modern fleet glowed in the darkness — patient, silent, waiting.

Pembroke did not know what the inquisitor would find on the other side. He knew only that the Faithful front was no longer united, that the Crown and the Office were moving in different directions, and that the diplomatic framework he had just helped to build was already beginning to crack.

### Scene 3: [[London (Trench Crusade)]] — The Modern Envoy

**[TIME]: Day 11 — 1914-09-25, 02:00 TC / 2026-06-28, 14:00 Zulu**

**[POV]: Third Person — [[Malcolm Renfield|Renfield]]*

The Merlin crossed the threshold at fourteen hundred hours Zulu, and for the first time in the history of either world, a rotary-wing aircraft flew in the skies of Trench Crusade Earth.

[[Malcolm Renfield|Sir Malcolm Renfield]] sat in the troop compartment, his back against the vibration-dampened bulkhead, his diplomatic bag on his lap, his eyes on the small window beside him. He was fifty-seven years old, a career diplomat who had served in five embassies, three war zones, and one constitutional crisis before the [[National Unity Government]] had elevated [[William Ashford|Ashford]] to the Foreign Office and [[William Ashford|Ashford]] had elevated him to the role he now occupied: Deputy Under-Secretary for Unprecedented Situations — a title that had been invented forty-eight hours earlier to give him the authority to do what he was now doing, which was flying through a hole in the universe to negotiate with a theocratic monarchy from a parallel dimension.

He had accepted the assignment without hesitation. Not because he was brave, but because the alternative was sending someone younger, someone less experienced, someone who might mistake the strangeness of the Faithful Crown for a reason to be afraid. Renfield had been afraid before — in the bombed-out embassy in Baghdad, in the freezing consulate in Kiev during the blackouts, in the conference rooms where he had watched treaties collapse and wars begin. He had learned, in those rooms, that fear was a tool like any other — useful for sharpening the attention, useless for making decisions. He had stopped being afraid of being afraid decades ago.

The helicopter banked, and he looked out the window.

Below him, Trench Crusade England unrolled like a map in a medieval manuscript. The landscape was green and grey and brown — fields divided into strips by hedgerows that had been there since before the Conquest, villages clustered around stone churches whose spires rose like fingers pointing at an absent God. The roads were unpaved, the rivers undammed, the forests dark and dense and unbroken by the scars of motorways and power lines. It was England, Renfield thought. It was England as it might have been if the Industrial Revolution had never happened, if the Enlightenment had never dawned, if the Reformation had never split the Church and the Renaissance had never taught anyone to paint a Madonna without a halo.

But there were differences. The fields were worked, but the workers were armed — every man and woman in the fields carried a pike or a crossbow or a musket slung across their back, the tools of labour and the tools of war indistinguishable after eight centuries of continuous conflict. England was beautiful. England was terrifying. England had been at war for longer than any civilisation on modern Earth had existed, and the war had shaped everything — the architecture, the agriculture, the way people walked down the road with their heads down and their weapons ready.

The Merlin followed the Thames toward London.

The city rose from the river like a fortress carved from the bones of a cathedral. The skyline was dominated by spires — not the delicate Gothic spires of the churches Renfield had studied in architectural history, but massive, brutal, functional structures that combined the sacred and the martial in a way that made no distinction between a pulpit and a gun platform. The Tower of London was visible in the distance — not a tourist attraction, not a relic of a vanished past, but a working fortress bristling with cannon and prayer-pennants. St. Paul's stood on Ludgate Hill — but it was not the St. Paul's of Wren's dome and the Blitz photographs. It was a different cathedral entirely, a dark stone mass with defensive walls and arrow-slit windows and a tower that mounted twelve-pounder guns blessed by the Synod.

And everywhere, everywhere, the crosses. Not the bare wooden crosses of modern churches. These were Orichalcum-tipped, silver-threaded, the prayers of warding visible on their surfaces like the veins in a leaf. They stood at every intersection, every bridge, every gate — a grid of divine protection laid over a city that had learned, over eight hundred years, that God was not a metaphor.

The Merlin descended toward a landing field south of the river — a cleared area surrounded by soldiers in the blue-and-gold, their muskets ready but not raised, their faces turned upward toward the machine that was descending from a sky that had never held anything like it.

Renfield straightened his tie, checked his diplomatic credentials, and stepped out of the helicopter into a world that had been fighting Hell for eight centuries.

The first person he saw was [[Rebecca Osei|Lieutenant Commander Rebecca Osei]].

She was standing at the edge of the landing field, flanked by two Crown soldiers, wearing the same uniform she had worn when she crossed the threshold seven days ago. "Sir [[Malcolm Renfield|Malcolm]]." She greeted him with a salute that was more instinct than protocol. "Welcome to London. The Faithful Crown's London, I should say."

"Lieutenant Commander [[Rebecca Osei|Osei]]. It is good to see you in one piece." He returned the salute, then extended his hand. She shook it. Her grip was firm, but her fingers were cold. "I take it you have not been mistreated."

As a fact. As certain as the sun."

"Everything." Osei's voice was flat, controlled, the voice of an officer who had been trained to deliver intelligence reports and was delivering one now. "The Crown is not hostile. They are not our enemy. But they are not what we think they are. They are a theocracy — a genuine theocracy, not a metaphor, not a political arrangement. Every aspect of their society is organised around the war against the [[Heretic Legion]]. The war shapes their laws, their customs, their understanding of right and wrong. "And the Office of the Propagation of Virtue?"

"The Office is the worst of it." Osei lowered her voice. The soldiers flanking her did not move, but she clearly did not want them to hear. "They are the doctrinal police. "It is not our standard, sir. A woman was brought before the Office yesterday for speaking out of turn during a theological debate in a public square. She was placed in the stocks for six hours. No food. No water. No shelter. And no one in the square stopped to look at her. Not because they were afraid of the Office. Because they agreed with it. This is normal here, sir. This is Tuesday."

Renfield absorbed this. He had read the intelligence briefings — the summaries of the Faithful Crown's theological doctrines, the descriptions of the Office's functions, the warnings from the analysts about the gap between modern and Faithful conceptions of human rights. But reading a briefing was not the same as hearing it from a woman who had spent a week watching it happen.

"I need to see it," he said. "Not a curated tour. I need to see what you have seen. Can you arrange that?"

"I can try. The Crown will want to show you the Palace, the Cathedral, the Academy — the things they are proud of. But if you insist on seeing the streets —" She looked at the soldiers. "If you insist, they will not refuse. They do not think there is anything to hide."

Renfield understood. The Crown did not think it was hiding anything because the Crown did not think it was doing anything wrong. The public penance, the interrogation chambers, the casual cruelty — these were not secrets. They were institutions. They were the normal machinery of a society that had spent eight hundred years fighting a war for the survival of the human soul and had decided, somewhere along the way, that mercy was a luxury it could not afford.

"Show me," he said.

They walked through the streets of London — the TC London, the London of cobblestones and prayer-pennants and the constant, distant rumble of artillery from the coastal batteries to the east. The city was crowded. But they moved with purpose. They moved with faith. Every few minutes, a bell would toll from one of the watchtower-spires, and the crowd would pause — not in fear, but in acknowledgment. A prayer had been said somewhere. A saint had been invoked. A battle had been won or lost. The bells kept the rhythm of the city, and the rhythm was sacred.

Osei led Renfield through the main thoroughfare — the Strand, she called it, the same name as the modern street, the same geography, the same bend in the river. But the buildings were different. The Savoy Palace was not a hotel. It was a barracks — the headquarters of a regiment of the Sacred Order of the Dragon, their banners hanging from the battlements, their chaplains visible on the balconies, their drakes — real drakes, Renfield realised with a shock that he did not have time to process — stabled in the courtyard, their wings folded, their breath steaming in the cold air.

"They ride those things," Osei said, following his gaze. "Into battle. Against the Heretic Legion. The drakes are blessed by the Order. They can breathe consecrated fire. They passed a public square — a wide cobblestone space dominated by a cross and a set of wooden stocks. A man was in the stocks — his crime, Osei explained, was blasphemy. The Office of the Propagation of Virtue had assessed his case, determined that his questioning was "insufficiently grounded in established doctrine," and sentenced him to twelve hours in the stocks and a public recantation. The man's head hung forward. His hands were bruised from the weight of the stock. No one was looking at him. People walked past — mothers with children, soldiers with muskets, priests with reliquaries — and no one stopped.

Renfield stopped.

He stood in the square and looked at the man in the stocks — the bruised hands, the bowed head, the silence of a crowd that had learned not to see what was in front of them. The man was not a heretic in the modern sense — a dissenter, a freethinker, a journalist who had written something the government did not like. He was a heretic in the medieval sense — a person whose beliefs were a threat to the social order, a contagion that could spread, a weakness that the Adversary could exploit. The Crown was not punishing him because it was cruel. The Crown was punishing him because it believed — genuinely, sincerely, with the full weight of eight centuries of continuous holy war — that a single unpunished heresy could cost the lives of a thousand Faithful soldiers.

That was the horror of it. The Crown was not evil. The Crown was righteous — and its righteousness was a machine that ground up everything it touched.

"Do you understand now?" Osei asked quietly.

Renfield did not answer. He was still looking at the man in the stocks, and he was thinking about the report he would have to write — the report that would go to [[William Ashford|Ashford]], to [[Stephen Davenport|Davenport]], to the National Unity Government and the international coalition and the world. The report that would say: *The Crown of England is a theocracy that believes itself to be the righteous sword of God, and it has confused mercy with weakness, and it has normalised cruelty as a form of order, and it literally does not see what it is doing — because eight centuries of war have made it unable to see.*

He followed Osei through the city. They walked past the Office of the Propagation of Virtue's London fortress — a dark stone building with the crowned-flame seal above its doors, the interrogation chambers visible through the lower windows, the sound of chanting drifting from the upper floors. Renfield did not ask to go inside. He had seen enough.

They walked past a market square where a woman was being marched away by Office agents — her crime, Osei said, was speaking during a theological debate without the approval of the presiding priest. The punishment was public admonition and a week of penance.

They walked past a church where a congregation was kneeling in the rain — not because it was raining, but because the priest had declared a day of collective penance for the sins of the city, and the rain was a blessing, and kneeling in it was a privilege.

They walked past the Palace of Whitehall — King Robert's seat — and Renfield caught a glimpse of the warrior-king through a window, standing in a council chamber with his advisors, his hand on the hilt of a sword that had seen a thousand battles, his face lit by the glow of a reliquary that held the finger-bone of a saint.

And everywhere, everywhere, the crosses.

Renfield filed his report that evening.

He sat at a small table in the quarters the Crown had provided — a room in the Palace of Whitehall, comfortable by Faithful standards, austere by modern ones — and wrote in longhand on a legal pad that he would later dictate into a secure channel. The report was twelve pages. It spared nothing.

He described the public penance — the stocks, the admonishments, the woman marched to correction for speaking without approval. He described the Office of the Propagation of Virtue — its fortress, its interrogation chambers, its agents who policed the boundaries of acceptable thought with the casual efficiency of a traffic warden. He described the theology — the ark, the divine mandate, the belief that every soul bore one mark or the other and that no neutral ground existed. He described the king — devout, calculating, a man whose faith was genuine and whose use of faith was deliberate.

And then he wrote the paragraph that would become the report's defining statement:

*The Crown of England is not hostile. It is not malicious. It does not seek to deceive us or to impose its theology upon us. By its own standards, it is righteous — the last line of defence against a force that has spent eight centuries trying to destroy everything the Faithful value. But its righteousness is a machine that has been running for eight hundred years, and it has ground certain concepts — mercy, dissent, the dignity of the individual — into a fine powder that the Crown no longer recognises as having ever existed. The Crown does not see what it is doing. It cannot see. The war has taken more from them than land and lives. It has taken their ability to distinguish between justice and cruelty, between order and oppression, between faith and tyranny. They are not evil. They are blind. And they will remain blind until someone from the outside shows them what they have become.*

He sealed the report, handed it to the signals officer who would transmit it through the portal, and then he did the thing that surprised everyone — the Crown officials who had prepared quarters for a week-long stay, the soldiers who had been assigned to escort him, the king who had expected a longer diplomatic engagement.

He rejected the offer to stay.

"Tell His Majesty that I am grateful for his hospitality," Renfield said to the Crown official who had been assigned as his liaison. "But I have seen what I needed to see. My report has been filed. I will return to my world at the earliest opportunity."

The official looked confused. The concept of a diplomat who wanted to leave as quickly as possible — who had crossed an interdimensional portal to visit an alien civilisation and had chosen to spend less than a day — was outside his frame of reference. Diplomacy, in the Faithful Crown's world, was a slow process. It involved liturgy, ceremony, the careful cultivation of trust over months and years.

Renfield was not interested in months and years. He was interested in the twelve pages of his report, and in the question of what the modern world was going to do about a theocracy that it could not fight, could not trust, and could not look away from.

He returned to the landing field, boarded the Merlin, and left Trench Crusade London behind him as the sun rose over a city that had learned to dread the dark.

### Scene 4: [[London (Modern)]] — The Faithful Bishop

**[TIME]: Day 11–12 — 2026-06-28, 16:00 Zulu to 2026-06-29, 02:00 Zulu / 1914-09-25, 04:00 to 14:00 TC**

**[POV]: Third Person — [[Bishop Aldric]]*

The helicopter was a sin against nature.

[[Bishop Aldric|Bishop Aldric of Southwark]] knew this with the certainty of a man who had spent fifty-three years studying the created order of the universe — the hierarchies of angels, the taxonomy of demons, the meticulous architecture of a world in which every object had a purpose and every purpose was ordained by God. The machine in which he was now seated violated every principle of that order. It flew without wings. It burned without fire. It made a noise like the scream of a dying animal, and yet the men who operated it — the modern sailors in their grey uniforms, their faces calm, their hands moving across panels of glass and light — treated it as casually as a drake-knight treated his saddle.

The bishop gripped the edge of his seat and prayed.

Beside him, [[Monsignor Franco Ricci]] was watching him with the careful neutrality of a man who had spent his career observing other people's discomfort without contributing to it. The monsignor was a small man — compact, precise, his black cassock immaculate, his Roman collar white as a flag of truce. He was a diplomat of the [[Holy See|Vatican]]'s Secretariat of State, dispatched to the portal zone three days earlier on the personal instruction of the Cardinal Secretary of State, with a mandate that was as vague as it was urgent: *assess the theological implications of interdimensional contact and report directly to the Holy Father.* He had been waiting at [[HMS Queen Elizabeth (R08)|HMS Queen Elizabeth]] when the bishop crossed the threshold, and he had introduced himself with a handshake and a phrase in Latin that the bishop had not heard since his ordination.

*Dominus vobiscum.*

*Et cum spiritu tuo.*

The monsignor spoke the old tongue with a Roman accent that the bishop found disorienting — not because it was wrong, but because it was right. The monsignor was a Catholic. A true Catholic, in the sense that the bishop understood the term — a priest of the One True Church, ordained in apostolic succession, bearing the authority of the See of Peter. And yet the Catholic Church that the monsignor served was not the Catholic Church that the bishop knew. The bishop's Church was the Church Militant — the Church that had fought the Adversary for eight centuries, that had blessed the blades of the Faithful armies and consecrated the graves of the martyrs, that had held the line against Hell when every other institution had crumbled. The monsignor's Church was — something else. Something the bishop did not yet have words for.

"Your Grace is uncomfortable," the monsignor said. His English was excellent, flavoured with the soft music of the Roman Curia. "The helicopter. It is disorienting, I know. I felt the same way the first time I flew in one."

"You have flown in one of these before." The bishop's voice was steadier than he felt.

"Many times. The Holy See maintains a small fleet of them — for pastoral visits, for disaster relief, for the logistical demands of a modern papacy. They are quite safe, I assure you. Statistically safer than walking across a street in Rome."

The bishop did not know what a statistic was. He knew only that he was seated in a machine that defied the natural order, flying through the sky above a world that had never known the Adversary's touch, and that the man beside him — a priest of the same Church, ordained by the same apostolic authority — spoke of helicopters and statistics as if they were ordinary things. As if the world had not been shattered by the opening of a portal between two realities. As if the bishop's entire understanding of creation had not been called into question by the simple fact that this world existed at all.

The helicopter banked, and the bishop looked out the window.

England was green. Not the green of his own England — the green of fields marked by shell craters and trench lines and the scorched earth of centuries of holy war — but a green so deep and so peaceful that it hurt to look at it. The fields were divided by hedgerows, the same hedgerows that marked the boundaries of the bishop's own diocese, but there were no fortifications at the road junctions. No watchtowers on the church spires. No workers in the fields carrying pikes against the possibility of a Heretic raid.

The roads were black ribbons, smooth as glass, and on them moved — things. Carriages without horses, hundreds of them, thousands, flowing along the black ribbons in streams of colour and light. The bishop had been told about automobiles. The intelligence briefings that [[Alistair Pembroke|Pembroke]] had compiled from [[Father Beauchamp|Beauchamp]]'s reports and [[Edmund Lassiter|Lassiter]]'s observations had described them in detail — *self-propelled carriages, powered by the combustion of refined petroleum, capable of speeds in excess of seventy miles per hour* — but reading a description and seeing them were different things.

They were everywhere. They filled the roads, the spaces between the roads, the cities that sprawled across the landscape like grey fungi consuming the green. The bishop had expected London to be large — the modern London, the London of the other world — but he had not expected it to be this large. It stretched from horizon to horizon, a sea of buildings that swallowed the countryside and spat it out as streets and squares and parks that were manicured rather than fortified, gardens rather than killing grounds.

The helicopter descended toward a landing pad on the bank of the Thames.

London resolved beneath them — modern London, the London of glass towers and stone monuments and bridges that arched across the river like bows drawn against the sky. The bishop saw St. Paul's and felt his heart lurch. The dome was familiar — a church of the same name, the same river, the same hill — but it was not his St. Paul's. His St. Paul's was a fortress. This was —

"It is a museum," the monsignor said, as if reading his thoughts. "Or partly. It is still a functioning church, of course — the Church of England holds services there on Sundays and holy days. But it is also a heritage site. A tourist attraction. People pay an entry fee to walk through it and take photographs."

The bishop did not know what a tourist was. He did not ask.

The helicopter landed on a pad beside the river — a flat rectangle of concrete surrounded by buildings of glass and steel that reflected the grey sky in a thousand shifting panels. A delegation was waiting for them: officials from the Foreign Office in dark suits, a representative from the [[Peter Chisholm|Archbishop of Canterbury]]'s office, and a security detail that the bishop recognised as soldiers only by the way they stood — alert, watchful, their eyes scanning the surroundings with the professional paranoia of men who had been trained to expect attack.

The monsignor stepped out of the helicopter first. The bishop followed. His legs were unsteady — from the flight, from the strangeness, from the weight of everything he had seen and everything he was about to see — but his back was straight. He was a bishop of the Faithful Church. He had faced heresy, schism, and the shadow of the Adversary itself. He would not show fear to a delegation of diplomats.

"Your Grace. I am [[United Kingdom (Modern)|Eleanor Walsh]], from the Foreign Office. On behalf of His Majesty's Government, I welcome you to London. We have prepared a programme for your visit — Parliament, St. Paul's, the Cabinet Office, a meeting with the [[Peter Chisholm|Archbishop of Canterbury]] if you wish — but we have also been instructed to accommodate any requests you may have. Your time is your own. We are here to facilitate."

The bishop looked at her — at the dark suit, the crisp diction, the complete absence of any reference to God, to the portal, to the fact that they were standing in a world that should not exist. The woman was welcoming him as if he were a visiting dignitary from a foreign country, not a pilgrim from a parallel reality. The formality was meant to be reassuring. It was not.

"I wish to see your Parliament," the bishop said. "I wish to see where your leaders make their decisions. And I wish to see your churches — the ones that are still churches, not museums. And I wish to speak to your Archbishop. And I wish to walk through your city and see what your people do when they are not being watched by priests."

The woman — Eleanor Walsh — nodded without expression. Whether she was offended, amused, or simply efficient, the bishop could not tell. "Very good, Your Grace. The cars are this way."

The cars. More self-propelled carriages, these ones black and polished and driven by men in uniforms who did not speak. The bishop climbed into the rear seat, the monsignor beside him, and the car pulled away from the landing pad and into the streets of modern London.

The city was a cathedral of noise.

That was the bishop's first thought — not a judgment, not a theological assessment, but a simple observation. Modern London was louder than any city he had ever entered. The rumble of the automobiles, the grind of the construction machinery, the endless chatter of voices from the devices that people carried in their hands and spoke into as they walked — it was a barrage of sound, a constant assault on the senses that the bishop had been trained to keep still and receptive.

And the light. Electric light — not the warm yellow glow of prayer-candles and oil lamps and the consecrated flame of the altar, but a cold, white, unwavering light that came from glass bulbs set into ceilings and walls and street-corner poles. The light did not flicker. It did not warm. It simply existed — a fact of the modern world, as ordinary as the air, as common as the ground.

The car stopped outside Parliament.

The Palace of Westminster was not a palace. That was the bishop's second thought. It was a palace in the sense that the Faithful Crown's Palace of Whitehall was a palace — a building of government — but it had none of the sacred weight that the bishop associated with governance. There were no crosses on the doors. No reliquaries in the entrance hall. No priests blessing the threshold as the legislators entered. The building was a machine for making decisions, and the machine was secular.

They entered the gallery overlooking the Commons chamber.

The bishop sat in the front row of the gallery, his hands folded in his lap, his eyes fixed on the scene below. The chamber was smaller than he had expected — smaller than the Synod's assembly hall in the Cathedral-Patriarchal, smaller than King Robert's council chamber in Whitehall — and the men and women who occupied its green benches were not warriors or saints or even particularly impressive specimens of humanity. They were ordinary people — tired, bored, shuffling papers and checking their devices and waiting for their turn to speak.

And no one had prayed.

The session had begun without an invocation. It had begun without a blessing. It had begun with a procedural motion and a point of order and the dull administrative machinery of a legislature that had forgotten — or had never learned — to ask for God's guidance before it made the decisions that shaped millions of lives.

The bishop watched the debate for an hour. The topic was trade policy — tariffs, import quotas, the regulation of agricultural subsidies — and the language was technical and dry and utterly devoid of any reference to the divine. The legislators argued about costs and benefits, about economic projections and political consequences, about the interests of their constituents and the demands of their parties. They did not argue about what God wanted. They did not argue about what was righteous. They did not even seem to consider that the question might have a theological dimension.

The bishop turned to the monsignor. "Do they ever pray?"

The monsignor considered the question. "There are prayers before the sitting — I believe the Speaker's Chaplain leads them each morning. But they are private. The public does not attend. And the prayers are —" He searched for the right word. "Ecumenical. Inclusive. Designed not to offend."

"Designed not to offend." The bishop repeated the phrase as if it were a foreign language. "Prayer is not designed not to offend. Prayer is designed to reach God. What does it matter if it offends men?"

The monsignor said nothing. His silence was an answer in itself.

They left Parliament and walked through the city. The bishop had asked to walk — no car, no escort beyond the monsignor and the Foreign Office liaison and the security detail that followed at a discreet distance — and they walked.

They walked through streets filled with shops — glass-fronted emporiums that displayed goods the bishop had never seen in quantities he could not comprehend. Clothing, food, furniture, books, machines, luxuries and necessities jumbled together in a cacophony of colour and light and noise. The people who walked through these streets were not warriors. They were not priests. They were not even particularly attentive to their surroundings. They walked with their eyes on their devices or their ears filled with music or their attention absorbed by conversations that were too quiet and too fast for the bishop to follow.

They were consumers. That was the word the monsignor used — a word the bishop had heard in the intelligence briefings but had not fully understood. They consumed. They bought things, used things, discarded things, and bought more things. The economy of modern London was built on consumption, and the consumption was endless, and the people who participated in it did not seem to notice that they were worshipping at an altar that had no name and no god and no purpose beyond itself.

"It is a cathedral," the bishop said. He was standing in the middle of a shopping street — Oxford Street, the monsignor had called it — and the crowds flowed around him like water around a stone. "A secular cathedral. They come here to worship things they can hold, and they mistake the holding for the meaning."

The monsignor looked at him. "You are not wrong. But I would ask Your Grace to consider that these people are not evil. They are — unaware. They have been raised in a world where the divine is private, optional, a matter of personal preference rather than public reality. They are not rejecting God. They have simply never been taught that God is something that can be known."

The bishop turned to look at him. The monsignor's face was kind — genuinely kind, not the diplomatic kindness of a man who was being paid to be polite — and the bishop felt something crack inside him.

"You are a priest," the bishop said. "You serve the same Church. The same apostolic succession. The same See of Peter. And you speak of God as if He were a preference — something that people can choose or not choose, like a coat or a pair of shoes."

"God is not a preference," the monsignor said carefully. "But belief in God — the expression of that belief, the public acknowledgment of His authority — is a choice. A choice that must be made freely, or it is not faith. It is coercion. That is what the Church has learned, Your Grace. Over the centuries. That forced faith is no faith at all."

The bishop stared at him. The monsignor had spoken of centuries — centuries of learning, centuries of change, centuries in which the Church had evolved from the Church Militant that the bishop knew into the Church that stood before him now, gentle and diplomatic and utterly convinced that the greatest threat to faith was not heresy but coercion.

"You have learned to be gentle," the bishop said. "My Church learned to be strong. I do not know which of us is right."

"Perhaps neither," the monsignor said. "Perhaps both. The portal has opened for a reason. Perhaps the reason is that we have something to teach each other."

The bishop did not answer. He was looking at the crowd on Oxford Street — the hundreds of people, the thousands of people, all of them moving through their lives without any awareness that they were souls in a cosmic drama, that they were being watched by God and the Adversary and every angel in the hierarchies of Heaven. They were blank. They were all blank — unmarked, untouched, untainted by the war that had consumed the bishop's world for eight centuries. And they were not grateful. They did not even notice.

That was the horror of it. The modern world had been given a gift that the bishop's world could not imagine — a world without the Adversary, a world where faith was a choice rather than a necessity, a world where no one had to fight for their soul because no one was trying to take it — and they had responded by forgetting that the gift existed.

They were not evil. They were not damned. They were asleep.

And the sleep was more terrifying than any Heretic trench, because in the trench the enemy wore its own face and the danger was honest and the fight was real. Here, in the bright-lit streets of modern London, the danger was invisible. It was the slow erosion of a soul that no one had told needed saving. It was the peace of a world that had forgotten that peace had a price.

The bishop turned to the monsignor. "I will stay. I was offered a few days. I accept. I need to understand more."

The monsignor nodded. "The Foreign Office will be informed. Your quarters have already been prepared — a hotel near Westminster. It is comfortable. You will have everything you need."

The bishop did not care about comfort. He cared about understanding. He had crossed a portal into a world that was supposed to be the ark — the preserved remnant of uncorrupted faith, the vessel that God had hidden from the Adversary to survive the flood of holy war. And what he had found was not an ark. It was a question. A question that the ark theology could not answer.

If the modern world was the ark, then why had it forgotten God? And if it had forgotten God, then what was it preserving? And if it was preserving nothing — if the faith that the ark was supposed to carry was already dead — then what were the Faithful fighting for? What was the war for? What was the suffering for?

The questions chased each other through his mind as the monsignor led him to the hotel — a tall glass building near Westminster, its lobby gleaming with polished stone and electric light, its staff efficient and smiling and utterly without fear. The room they gave him was on the twenty-first floor — a small apartment with a bed, a desk, a window that looked out over the city, and a small flat-screen television mounted on the wall.

The bishop stood at the window for a long time, watching the city. The sun had set — the long summer evening fading into a darkness that was not dark, a darkness lit by the electric glow of a million windows and a million streetlamps and a million headlights moving along the black ribbons of the roads. The city did not sleep. It did not pray. It simply continued, a machine of light and noise and consumption, humming through the night without any awareness that the night had once been a time for prayer.

He turned to the television.

The device was a rectangle of glass and plastic, mounted on the wall opposite the bed. The monsignor had shown him how to operate it — a small black rectangle with buttons, a remote control, a device that spoke to the television without wires or prayers — and the bishop had been fascinated despite himself. He pressed the button.

The screen came to life.

Moving pictures. Voices. Music. A woman in a red dress was selling something — perfume, the bishop thought, or perhaps a promise — and her voice was a stream of words that meant nothing and everything at once. The image changed. A man in a dark suit was talking about money — stocks and bonds and interest rates and inflation — and the bishop understood none of it and all of it. The image changed again. A war — somewhere, a war, with explosions and rubble and soldiers in uniforms that the bishop did not recognise — and the voice-over was calm, detached, as if the war were a story rather than a reality, a narrative rather than a wound.

The bishop watched for an hour. He watched advertisements for things he did not need, news reports about events that did not matter in the cosmic scale of the war between Heaven and Hell, entertainments designed to fill the silence that God had left behind. The television spoke without ceasing and without meaning, and yet the people in the hotel room next to his — he could hear them through the wall, laughing, talking, living — had probably left their television on all night and thought nothing of it.

He turned it off.

The silence was worse than the noise. The noise had been a distraction — a barrage of meaninglessness that blocked out the deeper silence beneath. But with the noise gone, the silence was absolute. The hotel room was quiet. The city was quiet — quieter than he had expected, quieter than a city of millions had any right to be. And in the quiet, the bishop waited.

He waited for the presence of God. He had felt it his entire life — the warmth, the order, the sense of being known by something that loved him and judged him and guided him through the darkness. It was always there, on the other side of the portal, in the cathedral at Southwark, in the prayers of the congregation, in the chrism on his hands and the blessing on his tongue. It was the foundation of everything he believed, everything he had dedicated his life to, everything that made the war against the Adversary bearable.

He felt nothing.

The bishop knelt beside the bed — a habit so old it was a reflex — and prayed. He prayed in the old Latin, the Latin of the Synods and the saints, the Latin that had been spoken in the cathedrals of his world since the Gate of Hell opened in Jerusalem and the war began. He prayed for guidance. He prayed for understanding. He prayed for the strength to look at the modern world and see what God wanted him to see rather than what his own fear wanted him to see.

And in the silence that followed the prayer, he heard nothing. No voice. No warmth. No presence. Only the distant hum of the city and the beating of his own heart and the quiet that was more terrifying than any Heretic trench because it meant — it might mean — that God had never been here either. That the ark was not a refuge. That the preserved remnant was not preserved at all.

That the ark was not a vessel.

That it was a test.

A test of whether the Faithful, faced with a world that had forgotten God, would remember why they believed. A test of whether mercy could survive the discovery that mercy was not necessary. A test of whether faith could endure the silence of Heaven — not the silence of a God who was absent, but the silence of a God who was waiting to see what His children would do when left alone with nothing but themselves.

The bishop remained on his knees for a long time. Then he rose, sat on the edge of the bed, and stared at the dark screen of the television — the rectangle of glass that had poured an hour of meaninglessness into the room and left nothing behind.

Outside, the city hummed. The machines kept running. The people kept sleeping. The world kept turning, indifferent to the question that the bishop had brought with him through the portal and would carry with him until he found an answer.

*If this is what God was saving — what were we?*
