# Chapter 04 – The Audit

## Summary

The Martinaise installation concluded without incident. The cameras are live. The door actuators seal every bay in under eight seconds. The control room monitors cycle through their feeds — four out of six, the last two installed the day before [[Lukas Delacroix|Lukas]] packed his bag and took the company launch back to Grand Couron. Moreau filed his final report and left without a word. The terminal is operational. The cage is complete. Lukas returns to Wild Pines headquarters and settles back into the engineering division — desk, workshop bench, meetings, reports, the fluorescent hum of corporate life. But his detection system will not leave him alone. He begins collecting parts: a microphone from the salvage bin, a relay rack moved during lunch, a CRT monitor scheduled for disposal. Each acquisition is logged against a work order that does not exist. Within a week the audit catches up. Joyce spends the morning defending him — miscommunication, her authority, her responsibility. The warning letter is a formality. The real punishment takes place that night on her bathroom floor: leather straps, cold tile, a cloth around his face, the cage locked and unlocked and relocked in a rhythm that teaches exactly what happens when you embarrass the woman who owns you.

## Scenes

### Scene 1: The Completion — Martinaise Dock Terminal, Morning, One Week After Chapter 03

The last camera went live at ten-fifteen on a Tuesday.

It was mounted above the south bay loading dock — the bay that had been the hardest to wire, its old conduit rusted shut, its ceiling too low for Moreau's team to work upright — and when the technician powered it on, the feed resolved on monitor five with a small flicker and then a clean, steady image of concrete and water and the grey curve of the bay. Lukas stood in the control room with his coffee and his clipboard and the quiet satisfaction of a man who had built something that worked. The satisfaction was genuine. It was also, he had learned over the past two weeks, not quite his own — borrowed satisfaction, the kind that came from delivering what someone else had paid for. But it held. For now, it held.

The door actuators had passed their final test the previous afternoon. Every bay sealed in under the eight-second specification. The control console showed six green lights in a row — EAST, WEST, NORTH, SOUTH, MAIN, GENERATOR — each representing a section of the terminal that could be locked from a single room, by a single operator, with a single switch. The terminal was a panopticon with a very simple interface. Anyone could operate it. Lukas had designed it that way. Joyce had specified the requirement during their second year together: *A security system is only as good as the person who can use it without training.* She had not been talking about the terminal at the time. She had been talking about something else entirely. He had applied the principle anyway. He applied everything she taught him. That was the arrangement.

The remaining technicians packed their equipment. Vinet — the radar technician, the one who had said *this thing is useless* and meant it — shook his hand on the loading bay floor. She had not asked about his "idea" again. She was a professional. She knew when an engineer was working on something that was not yet ready to be discussed. She also knew when an engineer was working on something without authorisation. The two categories overlapped more often than management liked to admit.

Moreau did not shake his hand. He dropped his final power-diagnostic report on the control room desk — a single sheet of paper, typed, the margins perfect, the data columns aligned with the precision of a man who had been filing reports for fifteen years and had long since stopped caring whether anyone read them. He met Lukas's eyes for perhaps two seconds. Then he picked up his tool bag and walked out of the terminal without a word. The silence was the most generous thing he had given Lukas in two weeks. It was also the most damning.

The Coalition commander Roux — younger / Varga, more eager, the kind of officer who said *operational coordination* and meant *tell me why my soldiers are not in charge* — met Lukas at the gate for the official handover. The meeting took twelve minutes. The commander asked questions that Lukas answered. The answers were satisfactory. The paperwork was signed. The terminal was Wild Pines property again, its cameras watching, its doors sealing, its control room humming with the quiet electricity of a system that had been designed to outlast the occupation. The Coalition soldiers withdrew from the perimeter by noon. By evening the only people in the terminal were the security guards Joyce had hired — eight men, ex-INSURCOM, vetted, bonded, expensive, reporting to her and not to him. They patrolled the bays in pairs. They checked the cameras on the hour. They did not speak to Lukas when he walked past them. They had been told who the real chain of command was. They had been told correctly.

The company launch left the fishing village jetty at six in the evening. Lukas stood at the bow, the same position he had occupied two weeks earlier when he had arrived with Joyce and her cigarette and her silence and the Polaroid camera in her cabin. The bay was flat and grey. The terminal receded behind him — a dark shape on the shore, its windows lit, its cameras rotating, its doors sealed. The cage was finished. He had built it in eleven days. He would not see it again for some time, but it would continue to exist without him, which was the mark of a well-designed system. A well-designed system did not need its designer. Joyce had taught him that too.

### Scene 2: The Bench — Wild Pines Engineering Division, Grand Couron

The engineering division occupied the fourth floor of the Wild Pines headquarters building in Grand Couron — a long, low-ceilinged room divided into cubicles and workbenches, lit by fluorescent tubes that buzzed at a frequency slightly below conscious hearing but high enough to produce, by the end of a shift, a low-grade headache that everyone in the division had learned to ignore. The windows looked out over the Grand Couron skyline — the Moralintern spire, the old Suzerainty palace converted to government offices, the row of corporate towers that had risen during the Thirties and never quite filled. Lukas's desk was near the east window, a view he had earned by outlasting three senior engineers who had retired or been transferred or simply stopped coming in. He was twenty-five. He had the desk of a man twice his age. No one in the division commented on this. No one in the division commented on anything that might require them to form an opinion about Joyce Messier's protégé.

The work was routine. Wild Pines had terminals in four districts, each with its own security requirements, each generating a stream of maintenance requests and upgrade proposals and incident reports that flowed into the engineering division like a slow river of paperwork. Lukas processed his share. He attended meetings where men in suits discussed budgets he was not allowed to see. He filed reports. He signed forms. He drank coffee from the machine in the break room that produced a liquid that was technically coffee in the sense that it was brown and hot and contained caffeine, but that tasted of burnt plastic and institutional despair. He did his job. He did it well. He was an excellent employee.

After hours, he built his detection system.

The workbench at the back of the division — the one nearest the supply closet, the one no one used because it was too far from the windows and too close to the ventilation shaft that rattled every time the heating system cycled on — had become, over the past week, a small private workshop. Lukas had claimed it without asking. The other engineers had noticed. They had not objected. The desk near the window came with certain privileges, and one of those privileges was the right to claim the bad bench for projects that were not on the official schedule.

The prototype was taking shape in pieces, each piece acquired through methods that ranged from "technically within regulations" to "would require a very creative explanation if anyone looked too closely."

The directional microphone had come from the salvage bin — a surplus unit from a decommissioned terminal in the Samaran Isolas, its housing dented, its diaphragm still functional. He had signed it out under a work order for "acoustic testing — Building 4 maintenance assessment," a project that did not exist but that was vague enough and boring enough that no one in procurement would follow up. The relay rack — twelve slots, each capable of holding a signal-comparator module — had been moved from the decommissioned-equipment storage room to his bench during the lunch hour, when the division was empty and the security cameras were pointed at the doors, not the benches. He had logged it as "reassignment of surplus assets, pending disposal review," which was the Wild Pines internal code for "this machine is too heavy for anyone to steal and too obsolete for anyone to care about."

The CRT monitor had been the hardest acquisition. Not because it was expensive — it was not; Wild Pines threw away better monitors than the one he had salvaged — but because CRT monitors were tracked, their serial numbers logged in a central inventory database that was updated quarterly and audited annually and that was, at present, the only thing standing between Lukas and a complete prototype. He had solved the problem by ordering a replacement monitor for the accounting department — a genuine request, their existing unit had a flickering phosphor layer that gave the accountants headaches — and keeping the old one, which was scheduled for disposal, instead of sending it to the salvage depot. The paperwork showed a disposal. The monitor was on his bench, connected to the relay rack, displaying a green test pattern that did not yet mean anything but that *would* mean something, eventually, when the signal-comparator modules were properly calibrated and the acoustic sensors were feeding real data.

The rest of the components were small enough to fit in his coat pockets: vacuum tubes, resistors, capacitors, a spool of copper wire, a soldering iron he had bought with his own money from an electronics shop in the old city. The vacuum tubes had come from the engineering stores, signed out one at a time, each against a different legitimate project that had a small surplus in its materials budget and that would not notice one tube missing from a box of twenty. The resistors had come from the same source. The copper wire had been on his desk when he arrived one morning — a spool, unlabelled, origin unknown — and he had decided, after a moment's hesitation, that asking where it had come from would be less useful than using it.

The detection system was not complete. It was not close to complete. But the architecture was there — the relay rack, the signal-comparator modules (two of them, wired in parallel, their relays clicking in a rhythm that was almost musical), the microphone housing, the RF antenna mount, the CRT display — and every evening, after the other engineers had gone home and the fluorescent lights had been dimmed to their night-time setting, Lukas sat at the bad bench with his soldering iron and his test equipment and his coffee that tasted of burnt plastic, and built the machine that did not yet exist, one component at a time, in the hour between the end of his shift and the moment when the night security guard made his first patrol and Lukas had to pack everything into a storage crate and slide it under the bench where no one would look.

He told himself he would put everything back before anyone noticed. He told himself this every evening, while he was soldering, and every morning, while he was processing maintenance requests, and every afternoon, while he was sitting in meetings that had nothing to do with acoustic triangulation or radio-frequency direction-finding or the machine that was growing, piece by piece, on the bad bench at the back of the division that no one used.

He knew, somewhere underneath the engineering satisfaction and the coffee and the fluorescent buzz, that this was a lie. He had been telling himself the same lie for three years, about different things, in different contexts, with different stakes, and the lie had never become true. The lie was not supposed to become true. The lie was supposed to be comfortable.

### Scene 3: The Audit — Wild Pines Headquarters, Grand Couron, Five Days Later

The audit came on a Thursday.

It came in the form of a supply clerk named Harald, a man in his late fifties with glasses that were slightly too thick for his prescription and a manner that suggested he had been doing the same job for thirty years and had developed, over those three decades, a deep and abiding suspicion of anyone who signed out equipment without filling in the correct form. Harald's job was to cross-reference the engineering division's equipment sign-outs against the central project database — a quarterly audit that no one paid attention to because the discrepancies were usually small and the consequences were usually nonexistent and the auditors were usually men like Harald, who filed their reports and went home and did not, as a rule, make trouble.

Harald made trouble.

He had found three sign-outs from the engineering stores — a directional microphone, a relay rack, a CRT monitor — that were not associated with any active project in the central database. The microphone was logged against "acoustic testing — Building 4 maintenance assessment," a project that, upon review, did not appear in any of the three databases Harald had checked. The relay rack was logged as "reassignment of surplus assets, pending disposal review," a designation that should have been accompanied by a disposal-authorisation form that did not exist. The CRT monitor was logged against the accounting department's replacement request — a legitimate procurement — but the old unit had never arrived at the salvage depot, and its serial number did not appear in the disposal log.

The pattern was clear. Harald, who had been doing this job for thirty years and had seen patterns of every conceivable shape, recognised it immediately. An engineer with access to the stores was signing out equipment without proper authorisation. The equipment was not being used for any approved project. The equipment was, in all likelihood, being used for something the engineer did not want management to know about.

Harald wrote the report. He sent it to the engineering division manager, a man named Petrov who had been promoted into management because he was good at engineering and who had remained in management because he was good at avoiding responsibility. Petrov read the report. Petrov recognised the name on the sign-out forms. Petrov, who had spent five years learning to avoid responsibility, recognised that a report about Joyce Messier's protégé was not a report he wanted to handle personally. He forwarded the report to Joyce's office with a memo that said, in the careful language of a man who had been promoted for his engineering skills and had survived for his bureaucratic instincts: *Flagged for your attention. Routine audit discrepancy. No immediate action required.*

The report arrived on Joyce's desk at nine-fifteen in the morning. At nine-thirty, her assistant called Lukas and told him to report to her office at ten o'clock.

---

Joyce's office was on the seventh floor, the executive floor, where the carpets were thicker and the windows were larger and the fluorescent lights had been replaced with lamps that emitted a warm, genteel glow that made everyone look slightly more trustworthy than they were. The door was oak. The nameplate said J. MESSIER — SENIOR NEGOTIATOR in brass letters that had been polished recently. Everything on the seventh floor had been polished recently. The seventh floor was a place where things were polished before they had time to become dirty.

Lukas knocked. The door opened. Joyce was behind her desk, a cigarette burning in the ashtray, a cup of coffee — the good coffee, the Moralintern export — cooling beside her telephone. The report from Harald was on her desk, face-up, the seal of the internal-audit office bright red in the upper-right corner. She did not ask him to sit. She let him stand in front of her desk for perhaps fifteen seconds while she finished reading a paragraph that she had, he suspected, already read before he entered.

"You've been busy," she said. Not a question. She did not look up.

"I can explain."

"You will explain." She turned the page. The sound of the paper was louder than it needed to be. "But first I am going to tell you what has happened this morning, so that you understand the position you have put me in."

She looked up. The cigarette rose to her lips. She did not blink.

"At nine-fifteen I received a report from the internal-audit office. The report identifies three equipment sign-outs — a microphone, a relay rack, a cathode-ray-tube monitor — that were made by you, under your name, against either nonexistent project numbers or disposal authorisations that were never filed. The report does not use the word 'theft.' It uses the phrase 'unauthorised equipment reassignment,' which is the internal-audit office's preferred euphemism and which will, if the report is escalated, be translated into 'theft' by the first board member who reads it."

She set the cigarette down. The ash was long. She did not flick it.

"I have spent the past forty-five minutes on the telephone. I have spoken to the audit office. I have spoken to Petrov. I have spoken to the board's legal counsel, who called me because Petrov forwarded the report with a memo that was designed to protect Petrov and not you, and the legal counsel wanted to know if this was a disciplinary matter or a criminal matter. I told them it was a miscommunication. I told them the equipment was being used for a preliminary feasibility study that I had authorised verbally, without paperwork, because the project was in an early stage and I did not want to burden the procurement process with a formal request before the concept was proven."

She paused. The cigarette was still burning.

"They believed me. They believed me because I am Joyce Messier and because my word is worth more than a supply clerk's audit on the seventh floor of this building, where the carpets are thick and the trust is thin and everyone is watching everyone else for the first sign of weakness." She leaned forward. "You made me look weak, Lukas. You made me look like a woman who cannot control her own engineer. And I cannot afford to look weak — not to this board, not to this company, not two weeks before Le Retour and the Coalition's mandate expires and the entire Zone of Control becomes a power vacuum that every faction in Revachol is going to try to fill. Do you understand what you did?"

He understood. He had understood since the moment Harald's name had been mentioned in the assistant's phone call. The understanding did not make the next sentence easier to say.

"I'm sorry."

"I know you are. Apologies are not currency on the seventh floor. Favours are. I spent a favour this morning — a large one, the kind of favour you call in once and cannot call in again — to protect you from the consequences of your own carelessness. The audit report will be amended to reflect a verbal authorisation from me. Your sign-out forms will be backdated. The equipment will be logged as an authorised pilot project. The warning letter in your personnel file will say 'procedural oversight — equipment requisition' and nothing else. That letter is the public consequence. It is the price of making it look like the system works."

She stubbed out the cigarette. The movement was precise, controlled, the same economy of motion she applied to everything — the same economy she applied to him.

"The private consequence," she said, "is this evening. My apartment. Eight o'clock. You will bring nothing. You will wear nothing that you cannot remove in under thirty seconds. You will not be late."

He nodded. He did not ask what the private consequence would involve. He had been her engineer for three years. He knew.

### Scene 4: The Apartment — Joyce's Apartment, Grand Couron, Evening

Joyce's apartment occupied the top floor of a building in the old city — a converted Suzerainty townhouse, its facade kept original, its interior stripped and rebuilt to the specifications of a woman who knew exactly what she wanted from every room. The floors were dark wood. The windows were tall and narrow, the glass old, the light filtering through in the amber tones of the streetlamps below. The furniture was expensive and sparse — a leather chair, a low table, a bookshelf that contained more memoranda than books. The apartment was not a home. It was a stage. Joyce had told him this during their first year together, when he had made the mistake of asking if she liked living there. *A home is a place you return to because you want to. A stage is a place you occupy because it serves the performance.* He had not asked about the apartment again.

The bathroom was at the end of the hall, past the bedroom, through a door that was heavier than it looked. The floor was white tile — large squares, cold even in summer, the grout pristine. There was a clawfoot bathtub, a pedestal sink, a mirror that covered the entire wall above the sink, and a narrow window that looked out over the rooftops of Grand Couron, the Moralintern spire visible in the distance like a finger pointing at nothing. The bathroom was the one room in the apartment that did not feel staged. Joyce used it for the same things everyone used bathrooms for, and also for things that were not typical, and the tiles had borne witness to all of them.

Lukas stood in the centre of the bathroom at eight o'clock, naked, his clothes folded on the closed lid of the toilet. The cage was locked. It had been locked all day. It would be locked for most of the evening. The key was around Joyce's ankle, beneath the hem of the silk robe she was wearing — a dark blue robe, the one she wore on nights when the session was going to be long.

"On the floor," she said. "Face down. Arms behind your back."

He lowered himself to the tile. The cold was immediate — a shock that travelled up through his knees, his palms, his chest as he lay flat, his cheek against the porcelain-white surface. The tile smelled faintly of the cleaning solution the building's staff used, a floral chemical that was meant to be pleasant and was instead unnerving in its artificiality. He could see the base of the clawfoot tub from this angle, the curve of its iron foot, the shadow beneath it where the tile was slightly darker.

Joyce knelt beside him. The robe rustled. The key on the chain around her ankle made a small sound — a tiny metallic click against the tile — and he felt his body respond to it the way it always responded, a Pavlovian tightening in the cage, a surge of awareness that was not quite arousal and not quite fear and was, like most things Joyce had conditioned him to feel, somewhere in the gap between the two.

"Calves to thighs," she said. "You know the position."

He bent his legs at the knee, folding them upward, the soles of his feet facing the ceiling. Joyce took the first leather strap — a wide band, soft from use, the buckle worn smooth — and wrapped it around his right leg, cinching the calf against the back of his thigh. The motion was practiced, efficient, the strap tightening in three small increments until his leg was locked in place, folded under him, immobile. She repeated the process on the left leg. When she was finished, both his legs were folded, his knees pressing into the tile, his weight distributed across a surface area that was now smaller than his torso.

"Hands to ankles."

She had to move him — roll him slightly onto his side, which was awkward with his legs bound, his body a collection of angles that no longer functioned the way bodies were supposed to — to bring his wrists down to his ankles. She wrapped a narrower strap around each wrist and the corresponding ankle, left to left, right to right, pulling the strap until his arms were bent behind him at a angle that was just short of uncomfortable. The position was a modified hogtie — his body folded, his legs bound to themselves, his arms bound to his legs, his face-down posture the only orientation the bindings permitted.

She stood. Her bare feet were level with his eyes — the arch of her instep, the delicate bones of her ankle, the chain with the key resting against her skin. He could see it from here. He could always see it.

"The cloth," she said, and he felt the fabric settle over his face — a strip of black silk, not the blindfold from the boat (that was on the boat; this was the apartment's version), thick enough to block light but thin enough to breathe through. She tied it at the back of his head, knotting it once, then again — the second knot a small pull that pressed the silk tighter against his eyes and mouth. He could not see. He could speak, through the fabric, but speaking would be difficult and would, in all likelihood, not be desired.

He lay on the bathroom floor — naked, bound, blindfolded, caged, a man folded into a shape that was not his own — and waited for the lesson to begin.

---

Joyce did not speak for a long time.

He heard her move — the pad of bare feet on tile, the rustle of the robe, the small sounds of preparation that he had learned to interpret during three years of sessions in rooms like this one. A drawer opening. A faucet running briefly and then stopping. The clink of something being set on the edge of the bathtub. She was preparing the curriculum. He was not permitted to know what the curriculum contained. The not-knowing was part of it.

Her feet reappeared in his narrowed field of awareness — the only part of the room he could perceive, through the gap between the blindfold and the tile, was a sliver of floor directly in front of his face. He could see her toes, the pale polish on her nails, the chain with the key draped across the arch of her foot. She was standing over him. She had been standing over him for perhaps a minute. She was waiting.

"Three years," she said. Her voice came from above — not angry, not warm, the neutral register she used for briefings and depositions and the early stages of a session when the tone was still being established. "Three years I have been training you. Three years of instruction, correction, reward, and discipline. Three years of building you into an engineer who could lead a team, manage a terminal, command the respect of a room. And in five days, you threw a piece of that away. Not the engineering — the engineering was good, the engineering was yours, I did not help you with the prototype. That is not the problem. The problem is that you did it without asking. You made a unilateral decision that affected my standing, my reputation, my ability to protect you from the consequences of your own choices. You did not consult me. You did not inform me. You moved equipment, you falsified paperwork, you acted as if you were the only person whose career was at stake."

She knelt. Her face came into view — not completely, but enough. The edge of her jaw. The corner of her mouth. The dark of her hair falling forward as she leaned close to his ear.

"You are not the only person whose career is at stake, Lukas. You are my engineer. My protégé. My responsibility. When you fail, I fail. When you are caught, I am caught. And tonight I am going to make sure you understand exactly what that means."

She stood. The robe rustled. He heard her step into the bathtub — the ceramic creak of her weight settling — and then the water began to run. Not the faucet. Something else. A smaller stream. A pitcher, maybe, or a basin. The sound was close and deliberate and deeply, fundamentally alarming.

The first pour hit the cloth over his face.

The water was cold — not freezing, but cold enough to shock, cold enough that his whole body convulsed against the straps. The cloth absorbed it instantly, became heavy, became a wet membrane pressed against his mouth and nose and eyes, and for a moment there was no air — there was only water and silk and the sudden animal certainty that he was drowning. He sucked for breath. The cloth resisted. His lungs pulled against the vacuum. A small, wet gasp — not enough, not nearly enough — and then the cloth lifted, Joyce's hand pulling it away from his mouth for three seconds, four, the air rushing in like a reprieve he had not earned.

"Good," she said. "Again."

The water came a second time. And a third. And a fourth. Each pour was a small drowning — the cloth sealing against his face, the air gone, the body screaming for oxygen while the mind counted the seconds (one, two, three, four — how long could a man hold his breath? longer than he thought, longer than was comfortable, longer than the animal in his chest wanted to permit). Between each pour she lifted the cloth and let him breathe — just enough, just barely enough — before the water came again. The lesson was not about drowning. The lesson was about mercy. She was teaching him that mercy was hers to give, and that she gave it on her own schedule, and that the interval between drowning and reprieve was not a guarantee. It was a gift.

The fifth pour was warmer — she had adjusted the temperature, a small kindness that was also a small manipulation, because the warmth made the water feel less like waterboarding and more like something intimate, something shared, and the contrast was more disorienting than the cold had been. He was crying. He did not know when the crying had started. The tears mixed with the water and the cloth absorbed all of it, and the tile beneath his face was wet with a combination of substances that he could not separate and did not try to.

"Enough," she said.

The cloth lifted. The water stopped. He lay on the tile, gasping, his chest heaving against the straps, his body a single raw nerve wrapped in leather and silk and the lingering sensation of almost having drowned five times in a row.

She was not finished.

---

"Turn over," she said.

She had to help him — his limbs were still bound, his legs folded, his hands attached to his ankles, the whole assembly of leather and flesh designed for a single orientation and resistant to any other. She rolled him onto his back, her hands firm on his shoulder and hip, the motion practiced and unhurried. His bound legs rose into the air above him — the position was absurd, a man folded and inverted, his calves strapped to his thighs and his hands cuffed to his ankles like a package that had been tied too tightly. The cage was visible from every angle now, a blunt steel geometry at the centre of his body, the key still dancing at her ankle, still out of reach.

The tile was cold against his back — a different cold from the face-down cold, sharper, more exposed. He could not see her face — the blindfold was still in place, the wet silk clinging to his eyes — but he could feel her presence above him, the shift of air as she moved, the heat of her body somewhere beyond the edge of his awareness.

"You were clever," she said. "Harald is not clever. The supply clerks are not clever. The audit department is not clever. You counted on their lack of cleverness to keep you safe, and you were almost right. Almost. But you forgot one thing." Her hand touched his chest — light, clinical, the same pressure she had used on his jaw in the cabin of the boat, two weeks and a hundred years ago. "You forgot that they report to me. Everything in this building reports to me eventually. Every report, every audit, every piece of paper with your name on it. You cannot hide from me, Lukas. You can only try. And trying is what tonight is about."

The first slap came without warning.

Her open palm across his face — not a full strike, not the kind of blow that would leave a mark, but sharp enough to sting, sharp enough that his head turned to the side and the blindfold shifted and for a moment he could see the edge of the bathtub and the gleam of the faucet and the pitcher of water still standing on the rim. Then the blindfold settled back and the world was silk again, and the second slap landed on his chest — the flat of her palm over his sternum, a red bloom of heat that spread outward toward his ribs.

The third landed on his thigh — the exposed inner surface, the tender skin just below the cage's lower ring, a spot she had mapped during the first year and returned to whenever she wanted to remind him that she knew his body better than he did. He gasped. The sound was swallowed by the cloth.

"Count," she said.

"One," he said. The word came out muffled, the silk pressing into his mouth, the damp fabric making the syllables soft and indistinct. "Two." The slap on his chest. "Three." The slap on his thigh. She did not stop at three. She continued — a rhythm of strikes distributed across his body in a pattern only she could follow, the palms of her hands leaving heat-prints on his face, his chest, his stomach, the undersides of his arms where the straps held his wrists to his ankles. None of the strikes were hard enough to bruise. All of them were hard enough to remember.

"Seven," he said. His voice was hoarse. The water had done something to his throat. "Eight. Nine. Ten."

She paused. Her breathing was slightly elevated — the only sign that the exertion had affected her. When she spoke, her voice was still calm, still instructional, the pedagogue in the bathroom with her curriculum and her leather straps and her pupil folded on the tile beneath her.

"You are still hard," she said. "Inside the cage. I can see it pressing against the bars. You're leaking onto the tile. Your body does not know the difference between punishment and reward anymore.
