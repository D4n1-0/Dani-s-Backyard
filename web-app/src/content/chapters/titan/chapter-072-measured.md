# Chapter 07.2 – Measured

## Summary

[[Levi Ackerman]] has been watched before. He is used to being watched. But no one has ever measured him. [[Hange Zoë|Hange]] brings the request — [[Ahn Jae-hyun|Dr. Ahn]] and the CATF science division want access to the biological signatures of the Shifters and the Ackerman bloodline. Armin volunteers immediately. [[Eren Yeager|Eren]] says nothing and submits. Levi watches both blood draws from the corner of the liaison office, cataloguing the procedure the way he catalogues a threat, and when he is satisfied that it is safe he says yes to his own full battery — not because he trusts, exactly, but because someone has to go first, and he has already watched the people he is responsible for go first, and that means it is his turn. What follows is a day inside his body — blood drawn, sensors placed, strength measured, scars catalogued, brain scanned, and, finally, the hardest yes: a germline sample, self-collected, in a private room where no one watches. By evening he is sitting in a CATF conference room at [[Grande Terre Logistics Hub|Grande Terre]] listening to a Korean scientist tell him what his body is.

## Scenes

### Scene 1: The Request

The list was on the desk between them.

[[Hange Zoë|Hange]] had placed it there at the beginning of the conversation — a single sheet of paper, printed on the office printer that Grande Terre had sent as part of the second technology transfer shipment, the paper whiter than anything Paradis produced, the text in clean Korean script with an Eldian translation in the margin. [[Levi Ackerman|Levi]] had read it once. He had read it twice. He had not sat down.

The room was [[Hange Zoë|Hange]]'s office in the Trost headquarters — the same office she had been drowning in, the desk still stacked with three piles of paper, now two piles and a cleared corner where the list had been placed. The window faced the training yard. The yard was empty in the late afternoon light.

"It's a request," [[Hange Zoë|Hange]] said. "Not an order. I told them I wouldn't give it to you if you didn't want it."

"What did they ask for."

"Three subjects. Armin. Eren. And — " she paused. She did not pause often. This pause was a full sentence. "You."

"What kind of samples."

"Blood, for the Shifters. [[Ahn Jae-hyun|Dr. Ahn]] wants to compare baseline Eldian biology to the Titan-linked markers. Armin's blood before and after transformation. Eren's spinal fluid traces. The science part is — " she waved her hand in the general direction of the science part. "They want to understand the mechanism. They've had a live Titan in a cage for months and they still don't know how it works. They think the Shifters' blood might tell them something the Titan's blood can't."

Levi looked at the list. The list was longer than blood. "And for me."

"Extensive." She said the word the way a commander delivered a casualty report — flat, factual, no softening. "They believe the Ackerman bloodline is a product of engineered modification. Not Titan science. Something older. Something that was deliberately built into the bloodline by Eldian Empire scientists. The abilities — the speed, the strength, the memory transfer through the Paths — are not random. They are designed. They want to understand the design."

The list, in order, was:

1. Venous blood draw — 30cc
2. Physical examination — anthropometric, cardiovascular, muscular
3. Full body examination — dermatologic, orthopaedic, surface-trauma inventory
4. Magnetic resonance imaging — brain, with focus on cerebellum and motor cortex
5. Germline sample

Levi had read the list twice. He had not asked what germline meant. He knew. He had been in the Underground. He knew what a man could sell and what he couldn't, and he had never sold that.

"No," he said.

[[Hange Zoë|Hange]] did not argue. She waited.

He looked at the list a third time. Item five was written in Korean script. In the Eldian translation, the word was *reproductive tissue sample*. It was the most clinical possible rendering of the concept. It was not clinical enough.

"Why do they need the last one."

"Because the modification is heritable." [[Hange Zoë|Hange]]'s voice was the voice she used for explaining Titan biology — detached, scientific, the mask of a woman who was talking about a man's reproductive material as though it were a specimen in a jar. "The Ackerman abilities pass through generations. Your parents had them — or one of them did. Your uncle had them. Any child you had would have them. The modification is in the germline. The part of your biology that carries forward. They can't see it in your blood. They need to see it in the cells that transmit inheritance."

"I don't have children."

"They know. The sample is not about your children. It's about the modification itself — the part that stays the same across generations. They call it — " she checked the paper, " — a stable epigenetic construct. Something that was built once and persists. They want to understand how it was built."

Levi looked at the paper. The paper was flat on the desk. The desk was wood. The wood had been Erwin's desk before it was Hange's. He had cleaned blood off this desk. He had cleaned blood off almost every surface in this building.

"There is a condition," he said.

"Name it."

"Armin and Eren go first. I watch. If the procedure hurts them — if it is anything other than a needle in an arm and a bandage — I withdraw my consent. If Ahn objects to being watched, I withdraw my consent. If the room is not clean, I withdraw my consent. If I decide, at any point, that the risk is unacceptable, I withdraw my consent. Do they accept these terms."

[[Hange Zoë|Hange]] looked at him. Her expression was the expression of a woman who had just watched a door open that she had assumed was locked.

"I'll tell them. I expect they will say yes."

"Then I will do the list. All of it."

She nodded. She folded the paper. She tucked it into her jacket — the waterproof jacket that CATF had sent, the one with the sealed seams, the one Levi had examined and kept.

"Thank you, Levi."

"Don't thank me. I haven't done anything yet."

"You said yes."

"I said yes with conditions. The conditions have not been met. If the conditions are met — yes. If they are not met — no. There is no thank you until the conditions are met."

[[Hange Zoë|Hange]] smiled. The smile was small and tired and genuine. "You just negotiated a medical consent protocol with more clauses than the iceburst stone treaty. I'm thanking you for the clauses."

He had no answer to that. The clauses were common sense. The clauses were the minimum. The clauses were what he would have insisted on for any soldier under his command, and he was, in this one instance, the soldier under his command, and he was going to insist on them for himself.

"When," he said.

"Armin and Eren tomorrow. You the day after. [[Park Sung-ho|Captain Park]] will accompany you to Grande Terre. Dr. [[Choi Min-ji|Choi]] — she's their chief medical officer — will conduct the physical examination. Dr. [[Ahn Jae-hyun|Ahn]] will oversee the full battery. Park will translate. The room will have three people besides yourself. No more."

"Good." He turned toward the door. Then he stopped.

"Hange."

"Levi."

"If the germline sample — " he did not turn around. "If the sample is used for anything other than what they said. If I find out. What happens."

The silence that followed was the silence of a Commander understanding that a subordinate was asking her to endorse violence.

"Then you will handle it," she said. "And I will not ask what handling it means."

He left the office. The corridor was dark. The training yard was quiet. The list was in [[Hange Zoë|Hange]]'s pocket, folded and accepted, and the conditions had been named, and tomorrow he would watch his soldiers give blood to strangers from another world, and the day after he would give more than blood. The list had five items. He had said yes to four of them without hesitation. The fifth — he had not said yes to the fifth. He had said yes with conditions, and one of the conditions was that he could still say no. He was holding that condition in reserve, and he knew it, and he suspected [[Hange Zoë|Hange]] knew it, and that was fine. A reserve was a kind of control. He was going to need it.

### Scene 2: Armin

[[Armin Arlert|Armin]] came to the liaison office at eight in the morning, ten minutes early, carrying a notebook and a ballpoint pen. He was wearing the same Survey Corps jacket he always wore, the same boots, the same expression of someone who had six questions prepared and would ask them in order of priority. He shook [[Ahn Jae-hyun|Dr. Ahn Jae-hyun]]'s hand. He said thank you for the books. He sat down in the examination chair that had been set up in the corner of the liaison office — a folding chair, metal-framed, with a padded seat, the kind of chair that CATF had shipped to Paradis two weeks ago for exactly this purpose.

[[Ahn Jae-hyun|Dr. Ahn]] was a biologist. He had glasses and the specific exhaustion of a man who had been running a laboratory on a subantarctic island for six months and was now doing fieldwork on an alien planet. He spoke Eldian with an accent that had improved noticeably since the first rendezvous — he could form sentences now, ask questions, give instructions. He was not fluent. He did not need to be. The work was mostly silent.

"This will take approximately three minutes," [[Ahn Jae-hyun|Dr. Ahn]] said. He was setting up the blood-draw kit on a small table beside the chair. The kit was a standard CATF medical tray: antiseptic swabs, tourniquet, vacutainer tubes, a butterfly needle. It was the same kit he had used a hundred times on the Eldian engineers at Grande Terre, the same kit the medics had used during the morphine training at the port. "There will be a small needle. It will feel like a sharp pinch. Then it is over. You have given blood before?"

"No," [[Armin Arlert|Armin]] said. "Paradisian medicine doesn't do this. We — " he thought about how to explain it. "We don't take blood from living people. We let it out with leeches, if there's swelling. Or we bleed someone who has a fever. But we don't extract and study it."

"Then this is your first time. Are you nervous?"

"A little. Not about the needle. About what the blood will tell you."

"Good nervous. Scientific nervous. I like scientific nervous." [[Ahn Jae-hyun|Dr. Ahn]] smiled — a brief, warm smile, the smile of a man who had spent his career explaining science to nervous people and had learned that warmth was more effective than reassurance. "The blood will tell me whether your biology has been altered by the Titan inheritance. The Colossal Titan — your predecessor, [[Bertolt Hoover|Bertolt]] — had a unique spinal fluid signature. If that signature has transferred to you, we will see it in your blood. If it has not transferred — " he paused, " — then the inheritance mechanism works differently than we believe. Either finding is useful."

[[Armin Arlert|Armin]] nodded. He was still holding his notebook. He had written five questions in it already — about the inheritance mechanism, about the Path connection, about whether the spinal fluid signature changed over time. He was going to ask them after the blood draw. He had prioritised them in order of importance. He was, [[Levi Ackerman|Levi]] thought from his position in the corner of the room, the most predictable human being on either Earth.

[[Levi Ackerman|Levi]] stood against the wall. His arms were crossed. He had been in the room for twenty minutes — he had arrived before [[Ahn Jae-hyun|Dr. Ahn]], had inspected the examination chair, had watched [[Ahn Jae-hyun|Dr. Ahn]] unpack the medical kit and swab the table with antiseptic. The kit was clean. The needle was sealed in sterile packaging. The vacutainer tubes were factory-sealed. The procedure was simple and safe and he had identified no points of failure. He continued to watch anyway.

The blood draw took two minutes. [[Ahn Jae-hyun|Dr. Ahn]] tied the tourniquet, found the vein, swabbed the site, inserted the needle with the practiced efficiency of a man who had done this ten thousand times. [[Armin Arlert|Armin]] watched the blood fill the tube. His expression was not surprise or fear. It was interest. The expression of a boy who had spent his childhood reading about a world he could not see, and was now watching his own biology leave his body and enter a glass vial, and was thinking about what the biology would tell the man who studied it.

"There," [[Ahn Jae-hyun|Dr. Ahn]] said. He removed the needle, pressed a cotton swab to the puncture site, applied a small adhesive bandage. "Two minutes. That is all. You have been very brave."

"I don't think I was brave. I just sat still."

"Most people cannot sit still during their first blood draw. You can. That is a form of bravery."

[[Armin Arlert|Armin]] looked at [[Levi Ackerman|Levi]]. Levi's expression had not changed. His posture had not changed. But there was something in the quality of his stillness — a relaxation so slight it was almost invisible, the settling of a weight that had been held in reserve.

"Captain," [[Armin Arlert|Armin]] said. "Was it acceptable?"

"Yes."

"All of it? Even the bandage?"

"The bandage is standard. The needle was sterile. The procedure was clean. It was acceptable."

"Good." [[Armin Arlert|Armin]] stood. He picked up his notebook. He opened it to the page with the five questions. "Dr. Ahn. I have some questions about the inheritance mechanism."

[[Ahn Jae-hyun|Dr. Ahn]] looked at the notebook. He looked at [[Armin Arlert|Armin]]. His expression shifted — the specific shift of a scientist who had just realised that his most valuable test subject was also his most important scientific collaborator.

"I will answer all of them," he said. "But first — let me put the blood in the cooler. It is less useful if it gets warm."

[[Levi Ackerman|Levi]] uncrossed his arms. He walked to the door. He did not need to watch any more of this. Armin was fine. The procedure was clean. The scientist was competent and the subject was curious and the blood was in the cooler and the first condition had been met. One down. Two to go.

### Scene 3: Eren

[[Eren Yeager|Eren]] arrived at the liaison office at ten in the morning. He was not ten minutes early. He was not carrying a notebook. He had been told to be there, and he was there, and the space between being told and being there was empty of any visible emotion.

[[Levi Ackerman|Levi]] watched him enter. Watched him register the examination chair. Watched him register the medical kit on the table. Watched him register [[Ahn Jae-hyun|Dr. Ahn]], who was standing beside the table with a fresh needle and a new set of vacutainer tubes, his expression the same calm professional warmth he had offered [[Armin Arlert|Armin]]. [[Eren Yeager|Eren]] did not return the warmth. He sat down in the chair. He rolled up his sleeve. He did not speak.

[[Ahn Jae-hyun|Dr. Ahn]] had been briefed on [[Eren Yeager|Eren]]. Not the classified assessment — the briefing that [[Park Sung-ho|Captain Park]] had given him, off the record, in the corridor before the blood draw began. *He has been silent for months. He is not hostile. He is just — elsewhere. If he does not speak, do not push. If he does not meet your eyes, do not force it. If he leaves without a word, let him leave.* The briefing had been concise. The briefing had been accurate.

"Good morning, [[Eren Yeager|Eren]]," [[Ahn Jae-hyun|Dr. Ahn]] said. "The procedure is the same as Corporal Arlert's. Two minutes. A small needle. A bandage. You have given blood before?"

No answer. [[Eren Yeager|Eren]] extended his arm. His forearm was bare. The veins were visible, close to the surface. He had rolled up the sleeve before he sat down. He had known what was coming and he had prepared for it and he was not going to make conversation while he waited.

[[Ahn Jae-hyun|Dr. Ahn]] said nothing more. He tied the tourniquet. He found the vein. He swabbed the site. He inserted the needle — the same practiced motion, the same steady hand. [[Eren Yeager|Eren]] did not flinch. Did not blink. His eyes were fixed on the far wall, a point of nothing in particular, and his face was the face of a person who had been somewhere else for a long time and was not coming back today.

The blood filled the tube. Dark red, almost black in the low light of the liaison office. [[Eren Yeager|Eren]] watched it without expression.

"Two tubes," [[Ahn Jae-hyun|Dr. Ahn]] said. "One for the baseline. One for the spinal fluid comparison. Your blood is — " he hesitated, " — it is the most important sample I have ever drawn. The Founding Titan. The Attack Titan. Two of the Nine. In one body. The biological signature — "

"Are you finished." [[Eren Yeager|Eren]]'s voice was flat. It was not a question.

[[Ahn Jae-hyun|Dr. Ahn]] paused. The needle was still in Eren's arm. The tube was full. He removed the needle, pressed the cotton swab to the puncture site, applied the bandage. "Yes. I am finished."

[[Eren Yeager|Eren]] stood. He did not acknowledge the bandage. He did not acknowledge [[Ahn Jae-hyun|Dr. Ahn]]. He walked to the door. At the threshold, he stopped.

He looked at [[Levi Ackerman|Levi]].

[[Levi Ackerman|Levi]] was standing against the wall. His position was the same as it had been for Armin's draw — arms crossed, posture still, eyes on the procedure. He had watched everything. He had catalogued everything. The needle. The blood. The silence. The way [[Eren Yeager|Eren]] had looked at nothing in particular.

[[Eren Yeager|Eren]] held his gaze. The gaze lasted three seconds. Then he turned and walked out.

The door closed behind him. The room was silent. [[Ahn Jae-hyun|Dr. Ahn]] was placing the tubes in the cooler with hands that were slightly less steady than they had been for [[Armin Arlert|Armin]].

"Captain [[Levi Ackerman|Levi]]," he said. "That man — is he well?"

"No," [[Levi Ackerman|Levi]] said. "He is not well. He has not been well for months. He is the Founding Titan and he has not been well for months, and his condition is neither your fault nor your concern. You have the blood. That is what you came for."

"I understand." [[Ahn Jae-hyun|Dr. Ahn]] closed the cooler. "I will not ask about him again."

"Good."

[[Levi Ackerman|Levi]] pushed off the wall. The second condition had been met — the draw was clean, the needle was sterile, the subject had walked out under his own power. [[Eren Yeager|Eren]] was not fine. That was not a condition. [[Eren Yeager|Eren]] had not been fine for months, and the only person who could fix him was [[Eren Yeager|Eren]], and the only evidence that [[Eren Yeager|Eren]] was trying to fix himself was that he had shown up.

That was enough. It had to be.

"Doctor," [[Levi Ackerman|Levi]] said.

"Yes?"

"Tomorrow. Grande Terre. The full battery. I will be there at dawn."

[[Ahn Jae-hyun|Dr. Ahn]] looked at him. The scientist's expression was a mixture of relief and apprehension — the specific emotion of a man who had just been told that his most valuable test subject had volunteered, and who was now calculating whether the test would break the subject.

"I will be ready," he said.

### Scene 4: The Crossing

The helicopter lifted off the Paradisian grassland at dawn. It was an H125 — the same model that had carried [[Connie Springer|Connie]] to his first ocean crossing, the same model that had landed in the dark grassland at the first rendezvous. The rotors beat against the cold air. The coast fell away. The Wall — Wall Maria, the outer ring, fifty metres of stone sleeping full of Colossal Titans — receded into the grey horizon, and then it was gone, and [[Levi Ackerman|Levi]] was looking at the ocean for the first time in his life.

He did not react. Reacting was for people who had not trained themselves out of reacting.

But he recorded it. The colour — grey-green at the coast, dark blue at the depth. The light — the sun rising over the water, a sheet of white fire spreading across the waves. The silence — the helicopter was loud, the rotors were loud, the vibration was a constant pressure against his chest, but the ocean itself was silent, and the silence was the largest thing he had ever heard. He had spent his whole life inside Walls. There had always been something between him and the horizon. Now there was nothing. The water stretched to the edge of the world, and the world was larger than any Wall, and he was sitting in a metal cabin in the sky watching it happen.

The flight was six hours with a refuelling stop on an auxiliary ship — a CATF logistics vessel that was anchored at the midpoint between Paradis and Grande Terre, a grey shape on the grey water that the helicopter settled onto like a bird on a floating stone. The crew refuelled the helicopter. The crew did not speak to him. He did not speak to them. He stood on the deck of the auxiliary ship, his hands in the pockets of the waterproof jacket he had been given, and watched the ocean move.

The second leg was three hours. The helicopter crossed water that changed from blue to grey to a darker grey, and the air grew colder, and the sky clouded over, and then — through the window — the island.

Grande Terre was a volcanic ridge rising out of the subantarctic ocean, grey rock and sparse tussock grass and seabird colonies that scattered at the sound of the rotors. The harbour was a deep-water fjord, sheltered on three sides, studded with grey warships and modular piers and fuel farms that spread across the flat ground like civilisation's first attempt to domesticate a wilderness. The base was larger than any city Levi had ever seen — not taller, not denser, but more intentional, the grid of buildings and roads and fuel tanks a geometry that had been designed by people who thought in systems rather than defences. He catalogued it. The sightlines. The exits. The distribution of armed personnel. The location of the medical facility — a prefab complex at the base's eastern edge, marked with a red cross on a white field, the same symbol that the Survey Corps had painted on their field hospital tents for a century.

The helicopter landed. The rotors slowed. The hatch opened. [[Park Sung-ho|Park]] was waiting on the landing pad, his liaison officer's uniform neat and clean, his expression the careful blankness of a man who had been briefed on who was arriving and was treating the arrival with the appropriate gravity.

"Captain," [[Park Sung-ho|Park]] said. "Welcome to Grande Terre."

"Don't welcome me. I'm not a guest. I'm a subject."

[[Park Sung-ho|Park]] absorbed this without reaction. The liaison officer had learned, across six months on Paradis, that [[Levi Ackerman|Levi]] did not do pleasantries. "The medical facility is this way. Dr. [[Choi Min-ji|Choi]] is waiting. Dr. [[Ahn Jae-hyun|Ahn]] is preparing the lab. The schedule is — "

"I know the schedule. I read the list."

"Then I will walk you there. We can talk or not talk. Your choice."

"Not."

They walked. The path was crushed volcanic gravel. The air was cold — subantarctic autumn, the wind off the harbour cutting through the waterproof jacket in a way that made Levi aware of every seam. The base smelled different from Paradis. Diesel, not coal. Salt, not horse. The hum of generators, not the silence of walls. His boots made a different sound on this gravel than they did on Paradisian stone. He catalogued the difference.

Ahead, the medical facility resolved — a cluster of prefab modules connected by covered walkways, the red cross visible on the largest building. The door was steel. The knob was plastic. He opened it with the hand that was not cataloguing, and stepped inside.

### Scene 5: The Lab

The medical facility was clean.

[[Levi Ackerman|Levi]] had been in clean rooms before — the medical bay at Trost, the field hospital at Shiganshina, the surgical tent where he had been stitched up after more fights than he could count. None of them had been this clean. The floors were linoleum, seamless, white. The walls were panelled with something that looked like paper but was not paper. The lights were embedded in the ceiling and produced a steady white glow without flicker. The air smelled of antiseptic — not the harsh alcohol of Paradisian field dressings, but something softer, more clinical, a smell that was designed to be inoffensive and succeeded in being unsettling.

A woman in a white coat was standing in the corridor. She was Korean — the same height as Levi, maybe slightly taller, with dark hair pulled back and glasses that were thinner than any glasses he had ever seen. Her expression was calm. Her posture was the posture of a person who had spent her career in rooms like this and was no longer capable of being surprised by the bodies that entered them.

"Captain [[Levi Ackerman|Levi]]," she said. Her Eldian was functional, accented, the accent of someone who had learned the language precisely for this assignment. "I am Dr. Choi Min-ji. I will be conducting your physical examination. Dr. [[Ahn Jae-hyun|Ahn]] will supervise the MRI and the biological analysis. [[Park Sung-ho|Captain Park]] will translate if you need translation. Do you need translation?"

"No. Your Eldian is acceptable."

"Thank you. I have been practising." She gestured down the corridor. "The examination rooms are this way. I will explain each procedure before we begin it. You may ask questions at any time. You may stop at any time. Your consent is continuous — you are not committed to anything you have not yet agreed to. Do you understand?"

"Yes."

"Good. Before we begin — do you have any questions?"

Levi looked at her. He looked at the corridor. He looked at the white walls and the linoleum floor and the lights that did not flicker. He had questions. The questions were not about the procedures. The questions were about what happened to the samples after he left — who read them, who stored them, who decided what they meant. But those were not questions for the doctor. Those were questions for the scientist, and the scientist was not in the room yet.

"No."

"Then let us begin."

### Scene 6: Blood and Sensors

The first examination room was small. A bed. A cabinet. A machine on a cart — a monitor with screens and wires and sensors, the kind of machine that had been built to read a body without touching it. [[Ahn Jae-hyun|Dr. Ahn]] was waiting beside the machine. His glasses were pushed up on his forehead. His hands were steady. His expression was the expression of a man who was about to do the most important work of his career and was terrified of getting it wrong.

"The first procedure is the blood draw," he said. "The same as [[Armin Arlert|Armin]] and [[Eren Yeager|Eren]]. Thirty cubic centimetres. Four tubes. May I proceed?"

Levi sat on the edge of the bed and extended his arm. The tourniquet went on. The vein was found. The needle went in — the same pinprick, the same familiar sting. The tubes filled. [[Ahn Jae-hyun|Dr. Ahn]] worked with the same practiced precision he had shown on Paradis, and [[Levi Ackerman|Levi]] watched him the same way he had watched him on Paradis — assessing, cataloguing, finding no fault.

"Done," [[Ahn Jae-hyun|Dr. Ahn]] said. The cotton swab. The bandage. "The blood will be analysed for the Ackerman protein signature. The preliminary results should be available within four hours."

"What is the Ackerman protein signature."

"We don't know yet. That is what we are looking for. The hypothesis — " he paused, switched to Eldian, " — the guess — is that Ackerman biology contains a unique cellular marker. A protein structure that blocks Path-mediated manipulation. Something that prevents the Founding Titan from affecting your mind, and prevents Titan spinal fluid from triggering transformation. If the marker exists, your blood will show it."

Levi absorbed this. The guess. They were looking for something and they did not know if it existed. That was honest. He preferred honest.

"The second procedure," [[Ahn Jae-hyun|Dr. Ahn]] continued, "is the physical examination. Dr. [[Choi Min-ji|Choi]] will conduct it. You will need to remove your jacket and shirt. The sensors — " he gestured at the machine, " — will be attached to your chest, your arms, and your torso. They measure your heart rate, your muscle activity, your oxygen consumption. They are not painful. They are adhesive pads. They pull off like bandages."

"I know what adhesive pads are. We have bandages."

"Of course. My apologies." [[Ahn Jae-hyun|Dr. Ahn]] backed away from the bed. "Dr. [[Choi Min-ji|Choi]] will take over from here. I will return for the MRI."

He left. The door closed. [[Choi Min-ji|Dr. Choi]] picked up a small cloth bundle from the cabinet.

"There is a changing screen in the corner," she said. "You will undress to your undergarments. And your shoes and socks. The sensors need skin contact to read accurately. You will also need to wear these." She handed him the bundle. "Shorts. Athletic shorts. They are standard CATF medical issue. They will allow the movement tests to be conducted without restriction. If you are not comfortable — "

"They are shorts. I am comfortable."

He went behind the screen. The screen was a white fabric panel on a metal frame. He removed his jacket. His boots. His trousers. His shirt. He folded each item and placed it on the chair beside the screen — the same way he had folded his gear in the Survey Corps barracks for fifteen years, each item aligned, each crease intentional. He put on the shorts. They were blue. They were lighter than any garment he had ever worn. They fit.

He stepped out from behind the screen.

[[Choi Min-ji|Dr. Choi]] was waiting beside the bed. She was holding a set of adhesive pads — small white squares with wires attached to them, the wires trailing back to the monitor on the cart. She looked at him. She looked at his torso. And she stopped.

[[Levi Ackerman|Levi]]'s torso was a map.

The scar across his left shoulder — from the Titan's claw that had nearly taken his arm during the 57th Expedition, the blade that had been broken in his hand while he was still swinging, the tendon that had been severed and then stitched with a needle and silk thread. The scar across his ribs — from the Underground fight where a man with a broken bottle had gotten lucky, the bottle edge finding the gap between his ribs before his knee found the man's throat. The scar on his hip — from the training accident in the early years of the Survey Corps, a rig malfunction that had dropped him fifteen metres onto a rooftop, the impact that had cracked his pelvis in two places and left him walking with a limp for three months. The scar on his back — from the moment he had turned to shield [[Eren Yeager|Eren]] from a Titan's hand, the hand that had been the size of a cart and had opened his skin from shoulder to waist, the wound that had taken forty-seven stitches and four hours and all of Hange's emergency morphine supply. The scar on his abdomen — from the surgery after Shiganshina, the incision where they had repaired internal bleeding from a blow he had not felt in the moment and had felt for every day since.

There were seventeen scars. He had counted them. He had reasons for all of them.

[[Choi Min-ji|Dr. Choi]] was looking at him. Her expression had shifted — the professional calm was still there, but underneath it, visible if you knew how to read people, was something else. Not shock. Not pity. Assessment. The specific assessment of a physician who had just seen a body that should not have survived and was recalibrating her baseline for what survival looked like.

"Captain," she said. Her voice was steady. "I am going to place the sensors now. Some of them will go over scar tissue. The adhesive may pull on the skin. It should not be painful. If it is, tell me."

"Do it."

She placed the sensors. One on his chest. One on each shoulder. Two on his abdomen. One on each inner arm. She worked with the practiced efficiency of a physician who had done this a thousand times, and she did not comment on his scars, and she did not hesitate, and her hands were warm and dry and impersonal in a way that was almost respectful. She was reading his body the way a surveyor read a landscape — marking the features without judgement, recording the data without reaction.

When the sensors were placed, she gestured at the treadmill. "Three minutes at a walking pace. Then three minutes at a running pace. Then there is a strength test — a grip strength meter, a vertical jump measurement, a core stability assessment. The sensors will record everything. Do you understand?"

"Yes."

"Before we begin on the treadmill — " She picked up a second device from the cabinet — a clear plastic mask with a rubber seal and a flexible tube that connected to a sensor module on the cart. The mask was shaped to cover the mouth and nose, held in place with elastic straps. "This is a metabolic mask. It measures your oxygen consumption and carbon dioxide output. It will tell us how efficiently your body uses energy during exertion. You will wear it during all exercises. It will be — uncomfortable."

Levi looked at the mask. The mask was the kind of thing he had seen in illustrations in the history of science book that Armin had been carrying around — a device that trapped breath and measured what the breath contained. The mask was clear. The mask had a rubber seal that would press against his skin. The mask was a machine for quantifying the air he pushed in and out of his body.

"Put it on."

She fitted the mask over his face. The rubber seal pressed against his cheeks, his nose, his chin. The straps went behind his head and tightened with a soft Velcro pull. The plastic was cool against his skin. The tube extended from the front of the mask like a snout, connecting to the sensor module with a click. The first breath through the mask was strange — the air tasted of rubber and plastic and the sterile chemical of the cleaning solution. The second breath was the same. The third breath was the same. He stopped noticing the taste. He could not stop noticing the presence of the mask itself — the way every inhalation was now measured, the way every exhalation passed through the tube into the machine, the way his own breathing had become a signal that someone was watching.

"How is the seal," Dr. Choi asked.

"Adequate."

"The mask will not restrict your breathing. If you feel lightheaded, tell me."

"I will not feel lightheaded."

She accepted this without argument. She gestured toward the treadmill. "Stand on the belt. The test will begin at a walking pace. After three minutes, I will increase the speed to a moderate run. After three minutes at that pace, I will increase the speed further. The increases will continue until you reach your limit, or until the sensor data plateaus, or until you ask to stop. Do you understand?"

Yes, he understood. She was going to push him until he broke. That was the point of the test — find the edge, measure the failure, record the number. He had been pushed to his limit before. His limit had moved.

He stepped onto the treadmill. The belt was black rubber. The machine hummed. The mask was sealed against his face. The sensors were taped to his chest and arms and back. The room was cold, but the cold would not last.

"Begin."

The belt moved. He walked. The pace was slow — a comfortable stride, the kind of pace a soldier used on a road march with full gear. He measured his breath through the mask. Inhale, four counts. Exhale, four counts. The machine on the cart produced a soft beep every ten seconds — the sensor module logging his oxygen consumption, his carbon dioxide production, his heart rate, his respiratory rate. The data accumulated in columns on the screen. The numbers built a curve that would show, when the test was complete, exactly what his engine was capable of.

Three minutes at the walking pace were easy. His heart rate settled at 88. His breath was steady.

"Speed increasing," Dr. Choi said. The belt accelerated. He shifted into a light jog, then a run.

The running pace was not hard. He ran every morning — three kilometres before breakfast, five kilometres on a good day, eight kilometres when he was training new recruits into the shape of soldiers. The treadmill was easier than Paradisian terrain — no uneven cobblestones, no mud, no horse manure to avoid. His heart rate climbed to 122 and held steady. His oxygen consumption was, according to the monitor, 15 percent below the expected baseline for a man his age and weight. The mask amplified each breath, the plastic fogging and clearing with every cycle. Sweat began to gather at his hairline.

Three minutes. The belt slowed. He thought the running phase was complete.

The belt did not slow. The belt accelerated.

"Speed increasing," Dr. Choi said. Her voice was level. "Maintain pace as long as you can."

He had not been told there would be a third phase. The list had said three minutes walking, three minutes running. The list had not said what came after. The list was incomplete, and the incompleteness was intentional — she was testing something that the list could not capture. His willingness to continue past the expected limit.

He continued.

The new pace was fast — faster than a standard drill, faster than he would run in training unless he was pushing for speed. His strides lengthened. His arms pumped. The mask pulled air into his lungs, and his lungs demanded more, and the mask delivered what they demanded but the demand was rising faster than the supply. He had been running for nine minutes. His heart rate had climbed to 148. The sweat was running down his temples now, collecting at the rubber seal of the mask, bleeding into the plastic tube.

"Speed increasing."

The belt accelerated again. This pace was a sprint. He had not sprinted for nine minutes. He was sprinting now. His legs burned. The breath through the mask was not a breath anymore — it was a gasp, a seizure, a cycle of air that came in hot and went out hot and left the plastic of the mask slick with condensation from the inside. Sweat ran into his eyes. He blinked it out. Sweat ran down his chest, pooling against the adhesive pads of the sensors. Sweat ran off his chin and onto the belt, a dark stain spreading across the rubber.

"Your oxygen saturation is dropping," Dr. Choi said. Her voice was clinical, detached — the voice of a physician reading a dial. "Your heart rate is 171. Your respiratory rate is 48 breaths per minute. These numbers are approaching anaerobic threshold for a normal subject. Do you want to stop."

"No."

The belt did not accelerate, but it did not slow. The pace held. The sprint continued. He had been running for twelve minutes. His legs were not burning anymore — they were beyond burning, in a state where the nerves had given up reporting the damage because the damage was continuous and reporting it was pointless. The mask was sealed to his face. Every breath fogged the plastic and cleared it and fogged it again. The sweat was everywhere — pooling in the hollow of his collarbone, dripping from his ear-lobes, soaking the waistband of the shorts.

He did not stop. He could not stop. Stopping was not a decision he had ever made while his body could still move. The question of stopping had not arisen during the Titan fights, during the ODM training, during the years in the Underground when a stopped foot meant a closing door and a closing door meant a knife. Stopping was not in the architecture. The architecture was designed to continue.

"Your oxygen consumption has plateaued," Dr. Choi said. "VO2 max reached. The data is stable. You may stop."

He did not stop. The belt was moving. His legs were moving. The mask was sealed and the sensors were reading and the machine was beeping and he was not finished.

"Captain. You may stop. The test is complete. You have passed the threshold."

He stopped. His hands hit the handrails of the treadmill. His chest heaved. The mask amplified every breath into a hollow, animal sound — the sound of a bellows working at full stroke. Sweat dripped from his nose. Sweat dripped from his chin. Sweat pooled on the black rubber of the belt and ran into the gap between the belt and the frame. His heart was beating in his ears. The sensors were still transmitting. The data — his VO2 max, his anaerobic threshold, the shape of his oxygen consumption curve under maximal exertion — was being recorded.

He pulled the mask off. The Velcro tore. The rubber seal left a red impression around his mouth and nose. The cold air of the room hit his face like a shock. He breathed — without the tube, without the plastic, without the machine measuring every molecule — and the breath was the sweetest thing he had tasted in months.

Dr. Choi was watching him. Her face was composed. Her hands were still on the tablet.

"Captain. That was — " She stopped. "That was a maximal exertion test. You ran for fourteen minutes and twenty seconds, including seven minutes above your estimated anaerobic threshold. The average subject terminates the test at three to four minutes above threshold. You doubled that."

"There was no reason to stop."

"Most people need a reason to stop. You do not seem to be most people."

He did not answer. He was still breathing through his mouth, the air cold and clean and free. The sweat was cooling on his skin, the sensors pulling at his skin as the adhesive softened with moisture. He was done. He had passed the test. The numbers would go in the report. The numbers would be higher than any normal baseline.

The grip strength meter came next. Dr. Choi handed it to him — a black cylinder with a digital readout.

"Squeeze as hard as you can."

He squeezed. The meter gave a reading that made her look at the screen and then back at him and then back at the screen. She did not say the number. He did not ask. The number was a number. The number was his.

The vertical jump measurement was next. A mat on the floor with sensors. He jumped from a crouch, his calves screaming from the treadmill, and the mat recorded a height that made her pause and recalibrate the device and then record the measurement without comment. His legs were shaking — the exhaustion in the muscles visible, the fine tremor of fibres that had been pushed past their normal range. The jump was still higher than any baseline.

The core stability assessment was the longest. Dr. Choi set a timer and instructed him to hold a plank position on the mat — elbows on the floor, body straight, weight on his forearms. The position was simple. The position was brutal after twenty minutes of maximum cardiovascular exertion.

His body began to shake within thirty seconds. The tremor started in his shoulders — the muscles that had been supporting him in the sprint — and spread through his torso to his legs and down to his feet. The shaking was visible. The shaking was violent. The sweat from his forehead dripped onto the mat beneath his face, a dark circle spreading on the grey fabric. He held the position. The timer counted.

At one minute, Dr. Choi said: "You may stop."

He held the position.

At one minute thirty seconds, the shaking was so intense that the sensors on his chest were rattling against his skin, the adhesive pulling with each tremor. The sweat was pooling under his face, forming a small lake on the mat. His arms were burning. His core was burning. His body was a muscle trying to tear itself apart through sheer refusal to stop.

At two minutes, Dr. Choi stepped forward and placed a hand on his back.

"Captain. The data is collected. You do not need to hold any longer. You can stop."

He stopped. His body collapsed onto the mat — not a controlled descent, but a release, the exhaustion taking him from straight to sprawled in the space of a second. He lay on his back on the wet mat, the sensors pulling at his skin, the ceiling lights too bright, his chest still heaving, his legs still trembling, the sweat still running down his temples.

He lay there for ten seconds. Then he sat up. Then he stood.

Dr. Choi was looking at the tablet. The readings from the core stability test — the duration, the muscle activation curves, the heart rate variability during sustained exertion — were being compiled. She had been a military physician for twelve years. She had run this protocol on hundreds of service members — Korean special operations, American SEALs, Japanese infantry, Chinese reconnaissance. The protocol was designed to produce failure. The protocol produced failure in every subject, eventually. The only variable was time.

This subject had held for two minutes and fourteen seconds of a maximal-exertion plank. The record among the special operations cohort was one minute forty-eight seconds. The record had been set by a Korean UDT/SEAL operator who had trained for the test for three months.

This subject had never seen a treadmill until today. He had never worn a metabolic mask. He had never performed a plank on a sensor mat. He had simply been told to hold, and he had held, and the data had been collected, and the data said something that did not fit the baseline.

She removed the sensors one by one — the same practiced efficiency, the same dry, impersonal touch. The adhesive pulled at his skin, lifting small beads of sweat with it. She placed the sensors in a plastic tray for cleaning. She made a final note on her tablet.

"The physical examination is complete. The data will be transmitted to Dr. [[Ahn Jae-hyun|Ahn]] for analysis. Do you have any questions?"

"No."

"The next procedure is the full body examination. You will need to remove the shorts. The examination is dermatologic — skin, surface, scar tissue. It is not invasive. You may stop at any time. Do you understand?"

He understood. It was item three on the list. He had read the list and said yes with conditions, and the conditions had been met, and the treadmill had been a calculation he had not anticipated, and he had survived it the way he survived everything — by continuing when continuing was the only option.

He removed the shorts.

### Scene 7: The Full Body Examination

[[Choi Min-ji|Dr. Choi]] examined him for twenty-one minutes.

She started with the scars. There were seventeen on his torso and back — she counted them, documented them, measured each one in centimetres and recorded the measurements on her tablet. The scar on his left shoulder: 14.2 centimetres, hypertrophic, consistent with a slashing injury from a serrated edge. The scar on his back: 41 centimetres, diagonal, from the seventh thoracic vertebra to the iliac crest, consistent with a deep laceration from an irregular cutting surface — a Titan's claw, she noted in her log, though she did not say this aloud. The scar on his hip: 9.8 centimetres, surgical, the entry point for a pin that had been inserted through the iliac wing and then removed. The scar on his abdomen: 17 centimetres, midline, the signature of an emergency laparotomy — the surgeon who had performed it had been skilled, the closure was clean, the scar was thin and had healed without infection. A Paradisian surgeon, she noted. Working without modern antiseptic.

She did not ask how he had received any of the scars. She did not need to. The scars answered their own questions. The scarring pattern was the pattern of a man who had been fighting for his entire life and had survived by being faster and stronger and luckier than the things that were trying to kill him. The scar on his back said *this was the one that should have killed you*. The scar on his abdomen said *this was the one where someone else saved you*. The scar on his shoulder said *you kept fighting with one arm*. The scars collectively said *you are not a normal human*, and [[Choi Min-ji|Dr. Choi]] was a physician, and physicians learned to listen to what bodies said.

She documented the surgical scars. The orthopaedic repairs. The fractures that had healed without modern intervention — a wrist fracture that had set at a suboptimal angle but still functioned; a metacarpal fracture on his right hand that had healed with minimal displacement despite no evidence of professional reduction; a rib fracture that had calcified cleanly. She documented the burn scar on his left calf — a childhood injury, she noted, chemical rather than thermal, consistent with a spill of industrial solvent. The Underground, she knew. She had read his file.

She documented his muscle structure. The density of his deltoids, his trapezius, his latissimus dorsi — the muscle groups that powered ODM gear operation, developed to a degree that matched no athletic profile in any medical literature. His body had been shaped by a single activity across ten years, and the shaping was visible on every surface.

She documented his height and weight. 160 centimetres, 65 kilograms. The numbers were below-average for a human male. The strength readings from the physical examination were above-average for any human of any size. The contradiction would go in the report. The report would not resolve the contradiction.

At the end of the twenty-one minutes, she stepped back from the examination table. She made a final note on her tablet. She looked at him.

"The examination is complete. You may dress."

She walked toward the door. At the door, she paused. She looked at him. Her expression was the same calm professional mask, but underneath it, for the first time, there was something personal.

"Captain," she said. "I have been a military physician for twelve years. I have treated combat injuries on three continents. I have never seen a body with this many survived traumas. Whatever your biology is — it is working."

He did not answer. She had not asked a question. She had delivered an assessment. An assessment was information. Information was useful. He filed it.

He got dressed.

### Scene 8: The MRI

The MRI machine was in a separate building at the eastern edge of the medical complex — a prefab structure with reinforced walls and a power line that ran directly from the base generator. [[Ahn Jae-hyun|Dr. Ahn]] was waiting at the door. He had changed into a clean lab coat. His glasses were on his nose now, pushed down, the lenses catching the white light from the ceiling panels.

"The machine scans your brain," he said. He was holding a tablet. The tablet showed a diagram of the MRI — a long white tube with a table that slid into it, a cross-section of a human head inside the tube, the magnetic coils rendered in blue and red. "Specifically, the cerebellum and the motor cortex. The areas that control movement, coordination, and procedural memory. We are looking for structural differences. The machine is loud — it produces a rhythmic banging sound, approximately 80 decibels. You will be given ear protection. You will need to remain completely still for the duration of the scan — approximately thirty-five minutes. Do you understand?"

Levi looked at the diagram. The tube. The table. The cross-section of the human head. The machine was a machine. The machine was a tube that made noise and expected him to be still inside it. He had been still in worse places.

"I understand."

"There is one additional requirement. The MRI uses a powerful magnetic field. You cannot have any metal on your body during the scan. The shorts you are wearing — they have a metal drawstring buckle. It must be removed."

Levi looked down at his shorts. The buckle.

"The alternative," [[Ahn Jae-hyun|Dr. Ahn]] said, "is a medical gown. It opens at the back and has no metal components. I have one prepared."

"Give me the gown."

He changed in the preparation room. The gown was thin and white and open at the back. He folded his shorts and placed them on the chair. He walked into the MRI room.

The machine was larger than the diagram. The tube was white and perfectly circular, and the table was sliding toward it, and the opening was narrow — narrower than the diagram had suggested. The radiologist was a Japanese man in scrubs who spoke English and did not speak Eldian. He had been told to expect a test subject who spoke no English, and he had prepared by writing his instructions on a laminated card in Eldian script, with diagrams. The card said:

1. Lie on the table.
2. Do not move your head.
3. Do not move your body.
4. Do not move at all.
5. The machine will make noise. Do not react.
6. It will take 35 minutes.
7. You may speak. We will hear you.
8. If you need to stop, say STOP.

Levi lay on the table. The table was cold. The gown was thin. The radiologist placed headphones over his ears — padded, sound-dampening, the same kind of headphones that the pilots wore during helicopter flights. The headphones played a steady tone. The radiologist placed a rubber bulb in Levi's right hand. "Squeeze this if you need to stop," he said, in accented Eldian. "Squeeze it and the machine stops. Do you understand?"

"Yes."

The table slid into the tube.

The first sensation was claustrophobia. The tube was narrow — narrower than he had expected, the white plastic walls perhaps fifteen centimetres from his face. He could not see the room. He could not see the radiologist. He could see only the white curve of the tube, the white ceiling of the tube, the white plastic shell that enclosed him like a sarcophagus. His instinct — the instinct that had kept him alive for thirty-three years, the instinct that had killed thirty-seven Titans and twenty-two humans and one man in the Underground who had been too slow to draw his knife — told him to move. To be still in an enclosed space was to be dead. To be still in an enclosed space with no exit was to be buried. He was not buried. He was inside a machine. The machine was not a grave. But his body did not know the difference between a grave and a machine, and his body wanted to move.

He did not move.

The machine began. The first bang was a hammer on the inside of his skull — a sharp, percussive *crack* that reverberated through the headphones and into his teeth. The second bang was the same. The third was the same. The noise continued — a rhythmic pounding, a sequence of bangs and whirs and clicks and hammers, the magnetic coils cycling through their acquisition sequences, the machine taking slices of his brain in two-millimetre increments, constructing a three-dimensional model of the structure that made him what he was.

He did not move. He counted the bangs. He counted one hundred and twenty-seven bangs in the first sequence, then a pause, then eighty-four bangs in the second. The counting was a strategy. The counting was a way of turning the noise into data, the data into control, the control into the kind of stillness that was not surrender.

At the twenty-minute mark, the machine paused. A voice came through the headphones — the radiologist, his accented Eldian distant and tinny.

"Halfway. How are you feeling."

"Fine. Continue."

The machine continued. The third sequence was the longest — two hundred and fifteen bangs, then a whirring sound that lasted forty seconds, then a final series of shorter pulses. At the thirty-five-minute mark, the noise stopped. The table slid out of the tube. The radiologist removed the headphones. [[Levi Ackerman|Levi]] opened his eyes. The room was white and clean and quiet. He had not moved for thirty-five minutes. He had counted six hundred and forty-two bangs. He had not squeezed the rubber bulb.

"The scan is complete," the radiologist said. "The images will be ready within the hour. Do you need anything?"

Levi sat up. The gown was damp with sweat. His hands were steady. His heart rate — he could feel it in his wrists — was 68. It had been 122 during the running test. It was 68 during the stillness test. The stillness test had been harder.

"No," he said. "I need to get dressed."

### Scene 9: The Hardest Yes

The final procedure was in a small room at the western end of the medical facility — a private room, windowless, with a sink and a counter and a plastic cup sealed in sterile packaging. There was a chair. There was a lock on the door.

[[Ahn Jae-hyun|Dr. Ahn]] had asked to speak to him before the procedure. They were standing in the corridor — [[Levi Ackerman|Levi]], [[Ahn Jae-hyun|Dr. Ahn]], and [[Park Sung-ho|Park]], who was translating not because Levi needed translation but because the subject required precision and [[Ahn Jae-hyun|Dr. Ahn]]'s Eldian was not precise enough for this.

"The germline sample," [[Ahn Jae-hyun|Dr. Ahn]] said. He was holding a tablet. The tablet showed a diagram of a cell — the nucleus, the chromosomes, the double helix of DNA rendered in blue and green. "The Ackerman modification is not a Titan-derived trait. It is something older. Something that was built into the bloodline by Eldian Empire scientists — possibly before the Titans existed. The modification is stable across generations. Your uncle — [[Kenny Ackerman|Kenny]] — had the same abilities. Your parents had them. Any distant Ackerman relative, across a dozen generations, would show the same markers. The modification is in the germline. The cells that transmit inheritance."

He paused. The diagram on the tablet shifted — a new image, a sperm cell, magnified a thousand times.

"The germline sample allows us to examine the DNA in your reproductive cells. The DNA in your blood has been diluted by a lifetime of mutation and environmental exposure. The DNA in your germline is the original — the template. If the Ackerman modification has a genetic signature, we will find it there. We will not use the sample for anything else. We will not store the sample beyond the analysis window. We will not share the sample with any other institution. These are the terms of your consent, and they are binding. Do you understand?"

Levi looked at the diagram. The cell. The chromosomes. The double helix. The thing that carried him forward — or would have, if he had ever been the kind of person who had children. He was not. He had decided this a long time ago, in the Underground, when he watched a mother sell her son for a piece of bread and realised that bringing a child into the world was the cruelest thing a person could do unless the world was worth being born into. His world had never been worth being born into. He had never regretted the decision.

But the cells were there regardless. The germline was there regardless. The modification was there regardless — built into him by scientists who had been dead for a thousand years, passed down through generations of murderers and survivors and soldiers and one small man standing in a corridor in an alien base being asked to give the template of himself to science.

"Does it hurt."

"No. The collection is self-administered. You will be given the sample container and a private room. There are no observers. The door locks. The procedure is — " [[Ahn Jae-hyun|Dr. Ahn]] hesitated. The word embarrassed was not in his Eldian vocabulary. [[Park Sung-ho|Park]] leaned in and translated a single word. [[Ahn Jae-hyun|Dr. Ahn]] nodded. "Private. Completely private."

Levi looked at the door. The door was grey. It had a lock. The lock was a metal bolt that slid into a metal plate, the kind of lock that could not be picked from the outside. The room beyond the door was empty except for a chair and a counter and a plastic cup in sterile packaging.

He had said no when he first read the list. He had said yes with conditions. The conditions had been met. The blood draw had been clean. The physical examination had been professional. The MRI had been tolerable. The list had four items and he had completed all of them and the fifth item was waiting behind this door.

He had never done this for anyone. Not for a doctor. Not for a scientist. Not for himself — he had donated blood to the Survey Corps medical supply, he had submitted to physical examinations for duty readiness, he had been stitched and splinted and drugged and operated on more times than he had eaten meals in the past year. But this — this was different. This was the part of him that was not for strategy, not for survival, not for the war. This was the part of him that was merely biological, and he had spent his whole life pretending that part did not exist, and now he was being asked to provide evidence that it existed.

"Captain," [[Park Sung-ho|Park]] said. His voice was quiet. "You do not have to do this. The battery is complete without it. The blood work, the physical, the MRI — those are already more data than we had this morning. The germline sample is useful but not essential. If you decline, the science will continue."

Levi looked at him. The liaison officer — the Korean Marine who had been handed to an alien military as a hostage and had spent six months learning to be a bridge. He was not lying. He was not negotiating. He was offering a door, and the door was real, and walking through it would not cost anyone anything.

"I know," Levi said. "Give me the cup."

[[Ahn Jae-hyun|Dr. Ahn]] handed him the cup. The cup was sealed in sterile packaging. The packaging was white. The cup was clear. The cup was the container for the thing that would tell them what his body was.

Levi opened the door. He walked into the room. He closed the door behind him. He slid the lock. The bolt moved with a clean, metallic sound — the sound of something final, something that could not be undone from the outside.

The room was small. The chair was against one wall. The counter was against the other. The sink was clean and the mirror was clean and there was a small paper towel dispenser and a bin for medical waste. There were no windows. There was no one else.

He stood in the centre of the room. The cup was in his hand. The door was locked. The building was quiet. The machine that had scanned his brain was in the building at the eastern end of the medical complex. The scientist who would analyse his germline was in the corridor outside. The war was still being fought on Paradis, on Marley, in the minds of everyone who had seen too much and slept too little and waited for the Rumbling to begin. And he was standing in a room with a cup, about to provide the most intimate evidence of his existence to a man he had met ninety minutes ago.

He thought about [[Eren Yeager|Eren]]. About the silence on the dock. About the blankness in his eyes during the blood draw. About the three-second gaze that had passed between them as Eren walked out of the liaison office. Eren had not said a word, but the gaze had said something. The gaze had said: *you are going where I cannot go. You are giving what I cannot give. You are choosing what I can no longer choose.*

[[Levi Ackerman|Levi]] had chosen his whole life. He had chosen to follow Erwin. He had chosen to give the injection to Armin instead of Erwin. He had chosen to serve Hange. He had chosen to go to this base, to submit to this battery, to give his body to science. Every choice had been his, and every choice had cost something, and every choice had been worth the cost.

This choice was his too. The germline — the part of him that carried forward, the part that had been designed a thousand years ago and passed down through thirty generations of murderers and survivors and soldiers — was his to give. And he was giving it. Not because he trusted the scientists. Because he trusted the choice.

He opened the sterile packaging. He did what needed to be done. He placed the sealed cup on the counter. He washed his hands in the sink. The water was cold. The soap smelled like nothing in particular. He dried his hands on a paper towel. He looked at himself in the mirror. The mirror showed a man in his early thirties, short, scarred, wearing a thin white medical gown that was open at the back. The man did not look any different.

He unlocked the door. He stepped into the corridor. [[Ahn Jae-hyun|Dr. Ahn]] and [[Park Sung-ho|Park]] were waiting. [[Park Sung-ho|Park]]'s expression was neutral. [[Ahn Jae-hyun|Dr. Ahn]]'s expression was the expression of a scientist who had just been handed the most valuable biological sample in his career and was trying not to show it.

"The cup is on the counter," Levi said. "The room is as I found it. Do you need anything else."

"No," [[Ahn Jae-hyun|Dr. Ahn]] said. "No. Thank you. The results — the preliminary results — will be ready within the hour. The complete analysis will take longer. But the preliminary results — you will know what your body is, Captain. You will be the first Ackerman to know."

Levi nodded. He walked back down the corridor, toward the waiting room, toward the chair where he would sit for the next hour while the machines read his blood and his scans and the template of himself that he had left in a plastic cup in a private room. He had given everything. He had given more than everything — he had given the part of himself that he had never acknowledged existed. The part that would have been a father's legacy, if he had been a father.

He was not a father. He was a soldier. And soldiers gave what they had.

### Scene 10: The Findings

[[Ahn Jae-hyun|Dr. Ahn]] came to the waiting room at four in the afternoon. He was carrying a tablet. His glasses were pushed up on his forehead. His hands were slightly unsteady — the specific unsteadiness of a scientist who had just seen something that did not fit any framework he had been given.

"Captain [[Levi Ackerman|Levi]]," he said. "I have the preliminary results."

Levi was sitting in a chair. He had been sitting in the chair for an hour and forty minutes. He had not moved. He had counted the ceiling panels. There were fourteen panels. He had counted the floor tiles. There were sixty-two tiles. He had counted the minutes. There had been one hundred of them. He had not asked for water. He had not asked for food. He had sat in the chair and waited.

"Tell me."

[[Ahn Jae-hyun|Dr. Ahn]] sat down across from him. He placed the tablet on the table between them. The tablet showed a brain scan — a cross-section of the cerebellum, the region at the base of the brain that controlled motor coordination, procedural memory, and balance. The cerebellum was rendered in blue and orange. The scan was annotated with measurements — density readings, volume comparisons, a percentile ranking against a normative database.

"Your cerebellum," [[Ahn Jae-hyun|Dr. Ahn]] said, "is not normal."

"I know."

"It is — how do I say this — not merely abnormal. It is categorically different. The motor-coordination region — the part of the cerebellum that controls movement and balance — is hyperdeveloped to a degree that does not appear in any medical literature. Not in elite athletes. Not in concert pianists. Not in any human whose brain has ever been scanned. The density of the neural tissue is approximately thirty-seven percent above the ninety-ninth percentile of the normative distribution. The volume of the cerebellar cortex is proportionally larger than any individual measurement on record. You have — " he paused. He was searching for an Eldian word that did not exist. He settled for English: "A superstructure. A secondary motor-processing layer. It sits on top of the normal cerebellum like an additional organ. It is not a mutation. It is an addition. Something was built onto your brain."

[[Levi Ackerman|Levi]] looked at the scan. The orange was the hyperdeveloped tissue. The blue was the normal brain. The orange was a second layer, a sheath of neural material draped over the cerebellum like a glove over a hand. It should not be there. It was there.

"This is why I can fight," he said. It was not a question.

"This is why you can do what you do. The secondary layer processes motor commands faster than the primary cortex. Your reaction time — the scan is consistent with a reaction time of approximately seventy milliseconds. The average human reaction time is two hundred. Elite athletes — one hundred forty. You are twice as fast as an elite athlete. The impulse travels from your eyes to your muscles through the secondary layer, bypassing the normal processing pathway. You do not think about moving. You move. The thought and the movement are simultaneous."

"Because the Ackermans were designed to protect the King."

"Yes. The secondary motor layer — what we call the superstructure — is consistent across this scan and the theoretical model of a bodyguard-enhanced nervous system. It is not an adaptation. It is a modification. It was built."

[[Ahn Jae-hyun|Dr. Ahn]] swiped the tablet. A new image appeared — a blood work analysis, the cellular markers rendered in graphs and percentages.

"The blood work confirms the cellular marker. Your cell membranes contain a protein structure that is not present in non-Ackerman Eldian blood — not in [[Armin Arlert|Armin]], not in [[Eren Yeager|Eren]], not in the Eldian engineers at Grande Terre. The protein binds to a receptor that the Founding Titan uses to access the mind of a Subject of Ymir. The binding blocks the receptor. The Founder tries to reach you through the Paths, and the protein stops it. The protein does not block the Paths entirely — you still receive information through them, which is how the Ackerman memory transfer works. But the protein prevents the Founder from altering your memories or controlling your body. You are connected to the Paths but immune to the Founder. You are inside the network but outside the command hierarchy. It is — " he paused. "It is the most elegant biological security architecture I have ever seen."

[[Levi Ackerman|Levi]] looked at the graph. The protein marker. The Founder immunity. The thing that had kept him from being controlled by the King for the past century — the thing that had gotten his family hunted and killed and driven into the shadows of the Underground. It was a protein. A protein in his blood. A protein that had been built by scientists a thousand years ago and passed down through thirty generations until it arrived inside his body and saved his mind from being erased.

"The protein is in my blood," he said. "It was in my mother's blood. My father's. [[Kenny Ackerman|Kenny]]'s. [[Mikasa Ackerman|Mikasa]]'s. Every Ackerman who ever lived had this protein."

"Yes."

"And if the Founder tried to use the Paths on me — this protein stops it."

"Yes."

"Show me the germline."

[[Ahn Jae-hyun|Dr. Ahn]] hesitated. The germline analysis was the part he had been most afraid to show. Not because the results were bad. Because the results were complete. Because the results confirmed something that no scientist had ever been able to confirm before.

He swiped the tablet. A new image appeared — a DNA sequence, the double helix rendered in coloured bands, with annotations in Korean and English. The sequence was messy — a genome was always messy, billions of base pairs of noise and signal and the accumulated debris of a million years of evolution. But in the centre of the sequence, in a region of chromosome four that corresponded to neural development, there was a block of code that was not noise. It was signal. Pure signal. A cluster of genes that had been inserted — not evolved, not mutated, inserted — into the Ackerman germline at some point in the distant past. The insertion was clean. The insertion was intentional. The insertion had been designed.

"This is the modification," [[Ahn Jae-hyun|Dr. Ahn]] said. His voice was quiet. "The proteins in your blood. The superstructure on your brain. The motor-processing enhancement. The Founder immunity. It is all in this block. The genes were spliced into the Ackerman germline by a technique that does not exist in modern biology — a technique that we cannot replicate, cannot fully explain, and can only observe. The modification was made once, approximately one thousand eight hundred years ago, and it has been copied faithfully into every generation of Ackermans since. It is — " he stopped. He removed his glasses. He rubbed his eyes. "Captain, I have been a biologist for twenty-three years. I have never seen a genetic construct this sophisticated. It makes our best gene-editing technology look like a child's drawing. It is the work of a civilisation that understood biology at a level we have not reached. And it was built into your ancestors. And it is in you. And it will be in your children, if you ever have them. And there is nothing in either of our worlds that can remove it."

[[Levi Ackerman|Levi]] looked at the sequence. The coloured bands. The block of code on chromosome four. The thing that made him what he was.

He had known he was different. He had known from the first time he picked up a knife in the Underground and felt his body move faster than his mind. He had known from the first time he killed a man and felt his muscles remember the movement — not learn it, remember it, as though he had done it before, a thousand times before, in the bodies of ancestors he had never met. He had known from the first time Kenny had looked at him and said *you have it too*, and the way Kenny had said it, the way Kenny had known — because Kenny had felt it, the same difference, the same speed, the same memory that was not memory.

He had always known. But knowing was not the same as seeing. Seeing was a diagram. A sequence. A block of code on chromosome four. Seeing was the protein in his blood and the superstructure on his brain and the germline modification that had been there since before the Walls, before the Titans, before the Empire, before anything that any history book remembered. Seeing was knowing that the thing he had always attributed to training or instinct or luck was actually architecture — a second brain layer, a protein shield, a block of alien code that his body had been running for thirty-three years without ever reading the manual.

He closed his eyes.

"Captain?"

He opened his eyes. [[Ahn Jae-hyun|Dr. Ahn]] was looking at him. His expression had shifted — the mask of the scientist cracking slightly, revealing something beneath. Not pity. Not horror. Respect. The specific respect of a man who had just been handed the genome of a living weapon and had watched the weapon process the information without flinching.

"The report," Levi said. "The full report. I want a copy."

"Of course. I will have it prepared before you leave."

"Good." He stood. The chair was plastic. The ceiling panels were still fourteen. The floor tiles were still sixty-two. The germline sample was still in a plastic cup in a laboratory somewhere in this building, being read by machines that had been built on another world. He had given everything. He had received the findings. The findings said: *you are not normal. You have never been normal. You were built to be what you are, and what you are is exactly what the scientists who built you intended.*

He walked toward the door. At the door, he paused. He looked at [[Ahn Jae-hyun|Dr. Ahn]].

"The blood," he said. "Armin's and Eren's. What did you find in their blood."

[[Ahn Jae-hyun|Dr. Ahn]] looked at the tablet. He had not planned to discuss [[Armin Arlert|Armin]] and [[Eren Yeager|Eren]]'s results with anyone except [[Hange Zoë|Hange]] and the Quadripartite Committee. But the man asking was the man who had just given him the most important biological sample in human history, and the man had conditions, and one of the conditions — implicit, unspoken, but real — was that he got to know what had been done to the soldiers under his command.

"Corporal [[Armin Arlert|Armin]]'s blood shows markers consistent with a Titan Shifter who has transformed at least once. The spinal fluid signature of the Colossal Titan is present — the same signature we have on record from the captured Titan in containment, but with differences that indicate a living Shifter rather than a transformed Pure Titan. The signature is stable. There are no anomalies."

"And Eren."

[[Ahn Jae-hyun|Dr. Ahn]] did not answer immediately. His hands, which had been steady through the entire battery, were now slightly unsteady.

"Private [[Eren Yeager|Eren]]'s blood contains the Founding Titan signature. It also contains the Attack Titan signature. And — " he paused. " — there is something else. A third signal. A biological trace that does not match either Titan. It is faint. We cannot identify it. It may be an artefact of the dual-Titan biology. It may be something else. I need more time."

"Tell me when you know."

"I will."

[[Levi Ackerman|Levi]] nodded. He walked out of the waiting room. The corridor was grey. The lights were steady. The door to the private room at the end of the hall was closed.

He walked past it without looking.

### Scene 11: Eren — Interlude

[[Eren Yeager|Eren]] was in his quarters at the Trost barracks. It had been three days since the blood draw. He had not spoken to anyone about it. He had not asked for results. He had resumed his duties — the patrol assignments, the intelligence briefings, the meetings he attended without contributing — and conducted himself in the way he had conducted himself for the past six months: present but absent, visible but unreachable.

The afternoon light was grey through the window. The room was spare — a cot, a desk, a washbasin, a single shelf of books that he had not opened in months. On the desk was the coffee brick that CATF had sent as part of the second rendezvous. The brick was unopened. The brick had been unopened for six months.

He was sitting on the edge of the cot. He was holding his right arm — the arm where the needle had entered. The puncture site had healed. The bandage had been removed. The blood was in a laboratory on Grande Terre, being read by machines that had been built on another world. The machines had found the Founding Titan signature. They had found the Attack Titan signature. They had found something else. They did not know what it was.

He did.

He had known since the day he kissed [[Historia Reiss|Historia]]'s hand in the medal ceremony after Shiganshina. The memories had begun then — fragmented, incomplete, images that belonged to his father and his father's predecessors and, further back, to the original Attack Titan, the first one, the one who had fought against the King and lost. The memories were not his. The memories belonged to the future. The Attack Titan was unique among the Nine — it did not inherit the memories of its predecessors. It inherited the memories of its successors. The future leaked backward through the Paths, and [[Eren Yeager|Eren]] had been receiving fragments of it for six months.

The fragments told him what he was going to do.

He was going to do it regardless. The blood in the lab did not matter. The CATF scientists did not matter. The gifts did not matter. The liaison office did not matter. The friendship did not matter. The only thing that mattered was the future — the future that was already written, the future that he had already seen, the future that was marching toward him through the Paths like a Wall Titan crossing the ocean.

He let go of his arm. The puncture site was clean. There was no scar. A blood draw did not leave a scar.

He had larger scars. He had scars no one could see — not [[Levi Ackerman|Levi]], not [[Hange Zoë|Hange]], not [[Mikasa Ackerman|Mikasa]], not [[Armin Arlert|Armin]]. Scars that had been carved into him by the future, by the memories of things that had not happened yet but would. The Rumbling. The trampling. The extinction. The freedom.

He was going to do it. He was going to do all of it.

The coffee brick sat on the desk. Unopened. It had been a gift from another world. He was not going to open it. He was going to leave it there, on the desk, in the room, as a reminder that gifts were temporary and leverage was permanent, and the only freedom that mattered was the freedom that no one could give you.

He lay down on the cot. He closed his eyes. The grey light faded into the grey dark, and the future waited.

### Scene 12: The Harbour

[[Levi Ackerman|Levi]] stood at the rail of the Grande Terre harbour, looking at the grey water and the grey fleet and the grey sky. The helicopter was being refuelled on the pad behind him. The flight back to Paradis would leave in forty minutes. He was holding a tablet — the tablet that [[Ahn Jae-hyun|Dr. Ahn]] had given him, the tablet with the full report on his biology.

He had read it once. He would read it again on the flight. He would give a copy to Hange. He would give nothing to anyone else. The report was his — he had earned it with the blood and the stillness and the germline in the plastic cup, and he would decide who saw it.

[[Park Sung-ho|Park]] approached from the medical facility. His footsteps were quiet on the gravel, the same quiet he had learned from Levi in the past six months — the quiet of a man who had been told that silence was a weapon and had taken the lesson seriously.

"Captain. The helicopter is ready. The flight time is six hours. You'll be back in Trost by midnight."

"Good."

They stood at the rail. The water moved beneath them — grey, cold, deep. The fleet rode at anchor. The generators hummed. The seabirds circled. The island was quiet in the late afternoon light.

"Park."

"Sir."

"When you write your report about today — about the battery, about the findings — what will you say."

[[Park Sung-ho|Park]] considered the question. He had been a liaison officer for six months. He had written a hundred reports. He had learned that the hardest thing to put in a report was not the fact. It was the truth.

"I will say that the Ackerman bloodline is a biological weapon designed to protect the sovereign. I will say that Captain [[Levi Ackerman|Levi]] submitted to comprehensive testing in the interest of strategic cooperation between Paradis and CATF. I will say that the findings confirm the generational persistence of the modification and its biological mechanism. And I will say — " he paused. "I will say that the testing was conducted with the full and continuous consent of the subject, and that no procedural violation occurred at any point."

"Good. That is what you should say."

"In my personal notes. The notes I do not submit. I will say something else."

"What."

"I will say that today I watched a man give his body to science. Not his strength, not his blood, not his duty. His body. The whole of it. Every scar, every cell, every inheritance. I will say that he did it alone — I was in the room, but he was alone. And when it was over, he asked me what I would write in my report. Not because he wanted to control it. Because he wanted to know if anyone had seen what he had done."

[[Levi Ackerman|Levi]] looked at him. The liaison officer — the Korean Marine who had been handed to an alien military as a hostage, who had spent six months learning to be a bridge, who had just watched a man give his body to science and had understood what he was watching.

"You saw it."

"Yes."

"Then the report will be accurate."

The helicopter rotors began to turn. The noise built — the familiar beating of blades, the vibration in the chest. [[Levi Ackerman|Levi]] turned away from the rail. He walked toward the landing pad. The tablet was in his hand. The findings were in the tablet. The findings said: *you are not normal, you have never been normal, you were built to be what you are, and what you are is exactly what the scientists who built you intended.*

He had always known. Now he had evidence.

He climbed into the helicopter. He strapped himself in. He watched Grande Terre recede through the window — the grey rock, the grey fleet, the grey water. The island grew smaller. The ocean grew larger. The world was larger than any Wall.

He opened the tablet. He read the report for the second time. The protein in his blood. The superstructure on his brain. The block of code on chromosome four. The germline modification that had been there for eighteen hundred years and would be there for eighteen hundred more. The thing that made him what he was — measured, quantified, confirmed.

He closed the tablet. He looked out the window. The ocean stretched to the horizon. The sky was grey and endless. The helicopter flew north, toward Paradis, toward the Walls, toward the war that was waiting.

He had given his body to science. He had received the findings. The findings said: *you are a weapon.*

He had known. He had always known. But now he had the proof — in his hand, on a tablet, printed in Korean and English and Eldian. The proof that he was what his ancestors had been. The proof that his body was not his. His body was architecture. His body was inheritance. His body was a gift from scientists who had been dead for eighteen hundred years, and he had been carrying it without knowing its weight.

Now he knew. The weight was measurable. The weight was 37 percent above baseline. The weight was a protein in his blood and a superstructure on his brain and a block of code on chromosome four. The weight was the thing he had always felt and never been able to name.

He had a name now. The name was *Ackerman*. The name was the report — fifty-three pages of measurements and graphs and analyses that confirmed what his hands had always known.

The helicopter flew north. The ocean moved beneath him. The report was in his hand. The future was ahead.

He had been measured. He had not broken.
