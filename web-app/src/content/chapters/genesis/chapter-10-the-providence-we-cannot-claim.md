# Chapter 10 – The Providence We Cannot Claim

## Summary

Three days after the collision, the Earth coalition convenes in London for an emergency assessment of the aftermath. [[Hugh Renwick|Renwick]] presents the intelligence: every intercepted Faithful transmission reads the *[[USS Stout (DDG-55)|USS Stout]]*'s performance not as a violation of trust but as divine confirmation — the submarine detected and destroyed in thirty-one seconds, the American hull intact, the Faithful enemy annihilated by a machine that needed no blessing. The room does not know what to do with a civilisation that refuses to be alienated. [[Marcus Webb|Webb]], a mid-level CIA analyst assigned to the strike group, speaks into the silence: *They want to believe we are Providence. We don't have to say we are. We just have to stop saying we aren't.* The [[The Ark Strategy|Ark Strategy]] is born in the quiet that follows — a calculated decision to let the Faithful's theology do the work of stabilisation, leverage, and diplomacy. [[Étienne Morel|Foreign Minister Morel]] objects on principle; [[James Hawthorne|Hawthorne]] argues the alternative is worse. [[Stephen Davenport|Davenport]] authorises the approach — not because he is convinced it is right, but because every other option leads somewhere darker. Within forty-eight hours, [[King Robert the Longsword|King Robert]] receives the modern invitation and sees it as the next command of Providence: a great council in the modern London, with the [[Iron Sultanate]] brought to the same table for the first time in history. He issues the summons. Couriers depart south and north. The manipulation succeeds — but the moral cost settles on [[Stephen Davenport|Davenport]] like cold water, and he will not be the only one to carry it.

## Scenes

### Scene 1: [[London (Modern)]] — The Cabinet Office Briefing Room

**[TIME]: Day 16 — 2026-07-03, 09:00 Zulu / 1914-09-29, 21:00 TC**

**[POV]: Third Person — [[Stephen Davenport|Davenport]]**

The Cabinet Office Briefing Room was full — fuller than it had been in the first days of the crisis, when the portal had been a novelty and the Faithful crown a curiosity and the whole business something that might still resolve itself into an orderly diplomatic exchange. That possibility had died with the collision, or with the torpedo, or with the moment the *[[USS Stout (DDG-55)|USS Stout]]*'s bow had torn through the armour plate of a dreadnought that had been fighting Hell for eight centuries and had never expected to be wounded by an ally. The room was full because the situation required, for the first time, a unified coalition decision — not a military posture, not a rules-of-engagement adjustment, but an answer to the question that had been waiting in the silence since the collision. *What do we do with a civilisation that refuses to be alienated?*

[[Stephen Davenport|Davenport]] sat at the head of the table — the same table where he had chaired COBR meetings on terrorism and pandemics and the slow, grinding dissolution of the post-Cold-War order, none of which had prepared him for the task of deciding how to respond to a theocratic kingdom that had mistaken a destroyer collision for a sign from God. [[William Ashford|Ashford]] from the Foreign Office was to his left, his expression the careful neutrality of a career diplomat who had learned to hide alarm behind a mask of professional attention. [[Hugh Renwick|Renwick]] — the DG of MI5, the grey man in the grey suit whose voice carried no inflection and whose conclusions admitted no ambiguity — sat opposite, a slim black folder open before him, his hands folded on the table as if the folder contained nothing more urgent than a quarterly budget review.

The video links occupied the far wall — four screens, four faces, the distributed command of a coalition that could not physically assemble at a single table. [[Terrence Kohler|Kohler]] from the *[[USS Gerald R. Ford (CVN-78)|USS Gerald R. Ford]]*, his flag cabin a steel box behind him, his coffee untouched, the permanent squint of a man who had spent thirty years staring at horizons and had never expected to stare at a portal to another dimension. [[James Hawthorne|Hawthorne]] from the *[[HMS Dragon|HMS Dragon]]*, his expression unreadable, the weight of the past two weeks visible only in the way he held his shoulders — tighter than before, a man who had been carrying something and had not yet been allowed to set it down. [[Étienne Morel|Morel]] from the Quai d'Orsay — the French Foreign Minister, a professor before politics, his spare frame and his intellectual precision making the video screen feel like a lecture hall. And at the far edge of the table, in the chair closest to the door, a man [[Stephen Davenport|Davenport]] had met only twice before — [[Marcus Webb|Webb]], the CIA analyst seconded to [[Terrence Kohler|Kohler]]'s strike group, here to present the intelligence assessment that had made this meeting necessary.

[[Stephen Davenport|Davenport]] had not intended to call a full coalition council. He had intended to read the intelligence summary in private, consult with [[William Ashford|Ashford]], and formulate a measured response that maintained the posture of cautious secular engagement without escalating the crisis. But the intelligence summary had not permitted privacy. The intelligence summary had been — he searched for the word as [[Hugh Renwick|Renwick]] opened his black folder and began to speak — *destabilising*.

"Three days," [[Hugh Renwick|Renwick]] said. His voice was grey — the same grey as his suit, his hair, the folder, the room. "Seventy-two hours since the *[[USS Stout (DDG-55)|USS Stout]]* struck the *[[HMS Resolute|HMS Resolute]]* and destroyed the Heretic submarine. In that period, the Faithful Crown has transmitted forty-seven separate signals — diplomatic, military, ecclesiastical — across the portal cordon and within its own fleet. We have intercepted all of them. The pattern is consistent. The Crown does not blame us. The Crown does not suspect us. The Crown does not request an investigation, an apology, or reparations. The Crown has concluded — with a unanimity that our analysts find difficult to explain — that the collision was not an accident. It was a demonstration." He paused. "Of divine favour."

The room absorbed this. [[William Ashford|Ashford]] shifted in his chair. [[Terrence Kohler|Kohler]]'s expression did not change. [[James Hawthorne|Hawthorne]] closed his eyes briefly — the gesture of a man who had been certain this was coming and had hoped he was wrong.

"A demonstration of what, precisely?" [[Étienne Morel|Morel]]'s voice was dry — the French of an academic who had learned English from books and deployed it with surgical precision. "That an Arleigh Burke-class destroyer can ram a dreadnought without sinking? That a Mk 54 torpedo can destroy a submarine built in 1912? These are facts. They are not theology."

"To them," [[Hugh Renwick|Renwick]] said, "they are indistinguishable. The Faithful Crown's theology does not separate the physical from the divine. A destroyer that detects a submarine the Faithful could not see — a submarine that has been listening to their transmissions for days — is not a machine. It is an instrument. The torpedo that killed the submarine in thirty-one seconds is not an engineering achievement. It is a miracle. The collision itself — the wound that did not sink the *[[HMS Resolute|HMS Resolute]]*, the damage that allowed the destroyer to continue the pursuit, the sequence of events that resulted in the destruction of the enemy and the survival of the flagship — is being read as a divine architecture. *God cleared the way.* That is the phrase in three of the intercepted transmissions. *God cleared the way.*"

The silence that followed was long. [[Stephen Davenport|Davenport]] had been a politician long enough to recognise the silence of a room that did not know what to do with the information it had just received. It was not the silence of agreement. It was not the silence of opposition. It was the silence of a room that had been asked to navigate a problem for which no existing framework had been designed — the silence of a coalition that had prepared for suspicion, for blame, for a diplomatic crisis, and was now being told that the diplomatic crisis was happening in the opposite direction. The Faithful did not trust them less. The Faithful trusted them *more*. And the trust was built on a mistake.

"What are our options?" [[Stephen Davenport|Davenport]] asked. He kept his voice even — the voice of a prime minister who had learned that a calm question asked in a room of experts often produced better answers than a sharp one.

[[William Ashford|Ashford]] spoke first. "We correct the record. Quietly. Through channels. A formal note — regret for the collision, commitment to continued cooperation, no mention of Providence or divine instruments or any of the theological framing. We don't endorse their interpretation. We don't attack it. We simply decline to participate in it. That maintains the integrity of the [[Cautious Secular Engagement|CSE framework]] while preserving the diplomatic relationship."

"It won't work," [[Hugh Renwick|Renwick]] said. "The intercepted transmissions are explicit on this point. The Crown is not waiting for us to confirm their interpretation. They have *already* confirmed it — internally, theologically, in the sermons and the councils and the decrees. A formal note that declines to participate will be read as humility. As proof that we are instruments who do not claim the title — which in their theology is the highest form of sanctity. If we try to correct the record, we will be interpreted as correcting ourselves in the wrong direction. We will deepen the belief."

[[Étienne Morel|Morel]] leaned toward his camera. "Then we deepen the belief. We issue a public statement. A clear, unambiguous statement that the modern world does not claim to be divine, does not believe in Providence as a political framework, and regards the collision as an accident — a tragic accident, for which we accept responsibility. We do not leave ambiguity for their theologians to fill. We fill it ourselves."

"And if that shatters the alliance?" [[James Hawthorne|Hawthorne]]'s voice was quiet — the voice of a man who had been holding his words and was releasing them one at a time, carefully, the way a sapper releases a fuse. "If the Faithful Crown, confronted with a public denial of the Providence they have invested everything in, retreats into suspicion — closes the cordon, withdraws the hospitality, treats the portal as a threat rather than a gift? We will have lost the only diplomatic channel we have into the TC world. We will have handed the [[Heretic Legion]] an advantage it has been trying to manufacture since the day the portal opened. And we will have done it in the name of honesty — a honesty that no one in that world is asking for, and that no one in this room would benefit from if it were given."

"Honour is not a cost-benefit calculation, [[James Hawthorne|Capitaine de frégate Hawthorne]]," [[Étienne Morel|Morel]] said. "The [[Cautious Secular Engagement|CSE]] doctrine was designed precisely for this situation. We do not participate in the theological frameworks of a foreign state. We do not endorse a divine mandate we do not believe in. We do not trade honesty for convenience. If the Faithful Crown cannot sustain its alliance with us without the fiction that we are instruments of Providence, then the alliance was never real. It was a transaction — and transactions, in international affairs, have a habit of reversing themselves when the price changes."

"With respect, Foreign Minister," [[Terrence Kohler|Kohler]] said, and the room shifted — the rear admiral's voice carrying the weight of the strike group behind it, the weight of the fleet that was holding station beside a portal to a world at war, the weight of a man who had spent two weeks staring at a problem that refused to be solved by the categories the coalition had brought to it. "The Faithful Crown does not have the luxury of a philosophical debate. They are fighting a war — a war they have been losing, slowly, for eight centuries. They have just watched one of our destroyers detect and destroy an enemy submarine that was sitting beneath their fleet for days without their knowing it. They are not going to retreat into suspicion because we refuse to endorse their theology. They are going to lean *harder* into the belief that we are what they need us to be — because the alternative, the possibility that we are simply a coincidence of physics and that the portal means nothing, is too cruel for a civilisation that has been praying for deliverance for eight hundred years. The question is not whether we correct their theology. The question is whether we let it work."

"And what does 'letting it work' mean in practice?" [[William Ashford|Ashford]] asked.

It was then that [[Marcus Webb|Webb]] spoke.

He had been silent for the entirety of the meeting — a mid-level analyst in a room of senior officials, present to present the intelligence and not to shape the policy. His face was thin, his posture awkward — the posture of a man who had spent fifteen years in the Directorate of Intelligence reading signals traffic and writing assessments that no one read until someone needed them, and who had never expected to be invited into a room where decisions were made. When he spoke, his voice was quieter than the room had been prepared for — quieter than [[Hugh Renwick|Renwick]]'s grey monotone, quieter than the hum of the air conditioning, quiet in the way a man was quiet when the thing he was about to say was something he had been turning over in his mind for days and had not yet decided whether it was brilliant or monstrous.

"They want to believe we're Providence," [[Marcus Webb|Webb]] said. "We don't have to say we are. We just have to stop saying we aren't. That's —" He paused. The room was looking at him. He had not expected the room to look at him. He had intended to make an observation, not a proposal, and the observation had become a proposal in the space between his mouth and the air, and now the room was looking at him and he was not certain he had the authority to finish the sentence. "That's the play."

He stopped. The silence that followed was different from the silences before — heavier, sharper, the silence of a room that had just heard something it was not certain it was allowed to think.

[[Marcus Webb|Webb]] looked around the room. [[William Ashford|Ashford]] was staring at the table. [[Hugh Renwick|Renwick]]'s face had not changed — the grey mask, the grey eyes, the grey silence — but something in the way his hands had stopped moving on the folder suggested he was considering. [[Terrence Kohler|Kohler]] on the video screen had leaned back in his chair — the squint shifting, the posture loosening, the posture of a man who had just been handed a tool he had not known he needed. [[James Hawthorne|Hawthorne]] was looking at [[Marcus Webb|Webb]] with an expression that was not quite approval and not quite horror and not quite either. [[Étienne Morel|Morel]] was very still — the stillness of a man who had just heard something he would argue against and was marshalling his arguments.

[[Marcus Webb|Webb]] felt the silence press against him. He was not a diplomat. He was not a commander. He was an analyst — a man who read patterns and wrote assessments — and he had just named a pattern that the room had been circling for an hour and had not been able to name. The silence was his reward. The silence was the cost of being right in a room full of people who were not ready to be told what they were thinking.

"Go on," [[Stephen Davenport|Davenport]] said. His voice gave nothing away.

[[Marcus Webb|Webb]] swallowed. The silence did not retreat — but it shifted, fractionally, the way a door shifts when someone puts a hand on the handle. He had been given permission. He was not certain he wanted it.

"The Faithful Crown has already decided what we are," he said. "They decided it in the first week — the ark theology, the Royal Decree of Hospitality, the King's letter. The collision didn't change their theology. It *confirmed* it. Everything we do — our ships, our weapons, our ability to detect an enemy they couldn't see — it all fits the frame they've already built. We don't have to push. We just have to stop pulling. We maintain our posture — defensive cordon, diplomatic engagement, no theological endorsement. But we stop correcting. We stop issuing statements that begin with 'we are not an instrument of Providence.' We let the silence do the work."

He paused. The room was still looking at him. He pressed on because stopping now would be worse than continuing.

"Three objectives," he said. "First: stability. The Faithful Crown is held together by its theology — the war, the faith, the belief that God is with them even when the Adversary is winning. If we break that belief — if we force them to confront the possibility that we are just an accident of physics — we don't just lose the alliance. We lose the Crown. The coalition fractures. The war effort fractures. The [[Heretic Legion]] gets a strategic opening it has been trying to create for eight centuries. We avoid that by letting their theology hold them together. We don't have to believe it. We just have to stop undermining it."

[[Étienne Morel|Morel]] opened his mouth. [[Marcus Webb|Webb]] kept speaking — not because he was not afraid of the French Foreign Minister, but because stopping now would let the objection land, and the objection would kill the proposal before it reached its second objective.

"Second: access. The Crown has already opened its borders, its ports, its military councils. That access is built on trust — trust that we are what the King believes we are. If we dismantle that trust, we lose the access. We lose the intelligence. We lose the ability to monitor the Heretic Legion's movements, to position our assets, to understand the TC world's military geography before the Legion makes a move against the portal. The access is worth more than the honesty required to maintain it — not philosophically, but operationally. In terms of lives. In terms of the war that is coming whether we believe in it or not."

"And third?" [[Terrence Kohler|Kohler]] asked. The question was quiet — not a challenge, but an invitation. The rear admiral had been watching [[Marcus Webb|Webb]] with the expression of a man who was evaluating a piece of intelligence and had not yet decided whether it was actionable.

"Third. The summit." [[Marcus Webb|Webb]] leaned forward — the analyst's instinct, the briefing-room habit, the physical shift that signalled a pattern was about to resolve. "We propose a grand council. In London. Here. The Faithful Crown — the King, the Synod, the military command — invited to a summit in the modern city they have been watching from across the portal. Frame it —" He paused. The next word was hard, and he chose it carefully. "Frame it as a historic convocation. A gathering of powers. We don't say *Providence*. We don't say *divine summons*. We let them say it. And we invite the [[Iron Sultanate]] as well."

The room went still in a way it had not been still before.

"The [[Iron Sultanate]]," [[William Ashford|Ashford]] repeated. The words were flat — the Foreign Office's careful, deliberate flatness, the tone [[William Ashford|Ashford]] used when a proposal was so far outside the diplomatic framework that he needed time to understand it. "The Sultanate is not our ally. We have had no contact with the Sultanate. We know almost nothing about the Sultanate — its government, its military, its relationship with the [[Crown of England]]. And you are proposing we invite them to a summit in London?"

"The Sultanate has been watching," [[Marcus Webb|Webb]] said. "They sent a courier vessel from the Crown's fleet anchorage on Day 5. They know the portal exists. They know the fleets are assembled. They have not acted — not because they are not interested, but because they are waiting to see which way the Crown moves. If we invite only the Crown, we signal that the Crown is our chosen partner — that the Sultanate is a secondary power, a wildcard that will be dealt with later. That signal will be received. The Sultanate will interpret it as alignment. And an excluded Sultanate — a Sultanate that believes the modern world has chosen the Crown over them — is a Sultanate that will act independently. Possibly against us. Possibly against the Crown. Definitely in ways we cannot predict."

He paused. The room was listening now — really listening, the way a room listens when a pattern has been named and the pieces are falling into place.

"If we invite both," [[Marcus Webb|Webb]] continued, "we do three things at once. We bring the Sultanate inside the tent before it decides to build its own. We signal that the modern world is not choosing sides in the TC world's internal politics. And we give the Crown a reason to accept the summit — a theological reason, a historical reason, a reason that makes the invitation not a diplomatic courtesy but a divine command. The King has never met the Sultanate in peace. No Faithful king has. The war against Hell has made them rivals, not allies, and the rivalry has never been tested by the possibility of a common enemy. We offer that test. We offer it in our city, on our terms, under our roof. And we let the Crown call it what it must — Providence, fate, the hand of God. We don't argue. We don't endorse. We let the silence speak."

He stopped. The room was silent — not the silence of a proposal being considered, but the silence of a proposal that had been considered and was now waiting for the objection that would kill it or the silence that would let it live.

[[Étienne Morel|Morel]] spoke. His voice was quiet — quieter than before, and the quiet was not approval but something closer to grief, the grief of a man who had just watched a principle be sacrificed on an altar of practicality.

"You are proposing that we lie to them. Not explicitly — I understand the distinction. You are proposing that we create a silence into which their theology can expand, and that we then benefit from the expansion. This is not engagement. This is not diplomacy. This is manipulation — the manipulation of a people who are already suffering, already fighting, already dying, and who will trust us more because we have let them believe something that is not true. That is not strategy, analyst. That is cruelty wearing the clothes of necessity."

[[Marcus Webb|Webb]] did not answer. He had made the proposal. The proposal was now someone else's to defend or to bury. He looked at [[Stephen Davenport|Davenport]] — the Prime Minister, the man who would decide — and felt the silence press against him like a weight he had not been trained to carry.

[[James Hawthorne|Hawthorne]] spoke instead.

"The Foreign Minister is right," he said. "It is manipulation. It is —" He paused. The word he wanted was not available, or was too available, and choosing it would make things worse. "It is a strategy that costs us something. It costs us the honesty of the letter the Prime Minister wrote to King Robert. It costs us the moral clarity of the posture we have maintained since the first parley. But —"

He looked at the video screen — at [[Étienne Morel|Morel]]'s spare, intellectual face, at the professor who had entered politics late and carried the lecture hall into every conversation.

"But the alternative costs more. If we dismantle the theology — if we force the Crown to see us as we are, an accident of physics with no divine mandate and no moral authority beyond what we claim — we will not liberate them from superstition. We will shatter them. The Crown is not strong enough to survive the truth, and the truth is not kind enough to wait for the Crown to become strong. They have been fighting Hell for eight centuries. They are tired. They are bleeding. They are held together by belief — and if we take the belief away, we do not leave them free. We leave them broken. In the path of an enemy that will not be gentle with the pieces."

[[Stephen Davenport|Davenport]] looked at the table — at the black folder, at the coffee cups, at the notes that no one was taking because no one needed to remember what had just been said. He thought about the letter he had written to [[King Robert the Longsword|King Robert]] — the letter that had refused Providence, that had offered friendship instead, that had been honest in the way that honesty was the only currency a king who wept at the thought of a world without war would accept. He thought about the king's letter — the calligraphy, the crimson seal, the trembling words of a man who had spent his whole life fighting and had finally, for the first time in eight centuries, been given permission to hope. He thought about the silence that [[Marcus Webb|Webb]] had named — the silence that was not a lie but was not the truth either, the silence that let a devout man believe what he needed to believe because the alternative was worse.

He thought about the moral cost — and he counted it, item by item, the way a chancellor counts a budget, the way a prime minister counts votes, the way a man who has spent his life in public service counts the things he will not be forgiven for. And he decided.

"[[Étienne Morel|Foreign Minister Morel]]'s objection is noted," he said. His voice was quiet — the voice of a man who had made a decision and was not going to pretend it was easy. "The objection will be recorded. It will stand. [[France]] is not — and will not be — bound by a strategy it cannot endorse. But the United Kingdom will proceed. The [[The Ark Strategy|Ark Strategy]] —" He paused. The name was not his, but it was the name the room would need, and he gave it to them because names, like silences, had power. "The [[The Ark Strategy|Ark Strategy]] is authorised. We will correct nothing. We will endorse nothing. We will propose the summit — the Crown, the Sultanate, the modern coalition, a great council in London — and we will let the Faithful call it what they must. The objective is stability. The objective is access. The objective is to prevent a war we are not equipped to fight from starting before we are ready to fight it. The means are —" He stopped. "The means are what they are. We will carry them."

The room absorbed the decision. [[Étienne Morel|Morel]]'s face on the video screen was the face of a man who had lost an argument and was already calculating the next one. [[James Hawthorne|Hawthorne]] looked at the table. [[Terrence Kohler|Kohler]] nodded once — the gesture of a commander who had received an order and would execute it. [[Hugh Renwick|Renwick]] closed his black folder with the quiet, precise motion of a man who had been prepared for this outcome before the meeting began.

[[Marcus Webb|Webb]] sat in his chair at the edge of the table and did not speak. He had made the proposal. The proposal was now strategy. He was not certain whether he was proud or ashamed, and he was not certain he was allowed to be either.

The meeting ended. The room emptied. [[William Ashford|Ashford]] gathered his notes. [[Hugh Renwick|Renwick]] departed without a word — the grey man, the grey suit, the grey silence he carried with him like a briefcase. The video screens went dark — [[Terrence Kohler|Kohler]] to his fleet, [[James Hawthorne|Hawthorne]] to his ship, [[Étienne Morel|Morel]] to the Quai d'Orsay and the long, patient work of French diplomacy finding its own path.

[[Stephen Davenport|Davenport]] stayed in his chair. The room was empty. The ventilation hummed. The morning light through the high windows — grey, English, the light of a London summer that had forgotten to be warm — fell across the table like a question that had not been asked. He thought about the letter he had written to [[King Robert the Longsword|King Robert]] — the five pages of A4, the cramped handwriting, the refusal of Providence and the offer of friendship. He thought about the strategy he had just authorised — the silence that was not a lie and was not the truth, the manipulation that was not cruelty and was not kindness, the decision that was not right and was not wrong and was something worse than either: *necessary*.

He had told the king he could not be an instrument of Providence. Now he was letting the king believe he was — not because he believed it, but because the belief was useful. The distinction was real. It was also, he suspected, the kind of distinction that only mattered to people who were trying to forgive themselves for something they had already decided to do.

He stood. The meeting was over. The strategy was authorised. The invitations would go out — to the Crown, to the Sultanate, to a council that had never been held and might never be held again. And somewhere on the other side of the portal, a king who had wept at the thought of a world without war was about to receive a letter that he would read as the next command of Providence.

[[Stephen Davenport|Davenport]] walked out of the briefing room and down the corridor and into the rest of the day. He did not look back. He did not need to. The decision had been made, and the cost — the cost was already settling, like cold water filling a room he would have to live in for the rest of his life.

### Scene 2: *[[HMS Herald]]* — The Great Cabin

**[TIME]: Day 17 — 1914-09-30, 10:00 TC / 2026-07-04, 22:00 Zulu**

**[POV]: Third Person — [[King Robert the Longsword|King Robert]]**

The great cabin of *[[HMS Herald]]* was not the wardroom of *[[HMS Resolute|Resolute]]*. It was smaller — a captain's cabin pressed into service as a council chamber, the dark oak of the table scarred by decades of charts and cutlery and the occasional sword, the stern windows smaller and higher, the coal smoke from the stacks drifting past the glass in grey veils. But it was the best the Faithful command had, now that the *[[HMS Resolute|Resolute]]* was listing at anchor half a mile to port, her flooding contained but her hull breached and her turrets silent and her crucifix tilted at an angle that measured the wound the morning had given her. The *[[HMS Herald|Herald]]* — [[Richard Morgan|Morgan]]'s ship, a fast cruiser rather than a dreadnought, lighter and quicker and never intended to serve as a flagship — had become the centre of the Faithful cordon by default, and her great cabin had become the place where the Crown of England decided what to do about the miracle that had just wounded its flagship and annihilated its enemy in the space of a single morning.

[[King Robert the Longsword|King Robert]] sat at the head of the table. He had come aboard the *[[HMS Herald|Herald]]* at dawn — the royal barge crossing from the *[[HMS Beacon|Beacon]]*, his personal vessel, through the grey Atlantic swell, past the crippled *[[HMS Resolute|Resolute]]*, past the circling torpedo-boat destroyers, past the distant shimmer of the portal. He had come because the council was divided, and a divided council in the presence of a miracle was a council that needed its king.

The faces around the table were the faces of the Faithful command — the men who had been guarding the portal for two weeks and were now discovering that guarding was not the same as understanding. [[Alistair Pembroke|Pembroke]] sat to the king's right — the captain of the *[[HMS Resolute|Resolute]]*, his ship crippled but his authority intact, his expression the careful, measured neutrality of a commander who had lost a vessel and was not yet ready to mourn it. [[Father Beauchamp|Beauchamp]] sat beside [[Alistair Pembroke|Pembroke]], his Orichalcum crucifix still absent, his hands folded on the dark oak, his face the face of a man who had built a theology and was now watching it tested in ways he had not anticipated. He turned and walked through the door. He thought about the king's faith and the moderns' silence and the summit that was being assembled in a city he had never seen. And he thought about the question he had asked [[Bishop Aldric|Aldric]] — whether the moderns knew what they were doing — and the answer he had been given, which was not an answer but a warning, and not a warning but a promise: *we will discover it. Not now. But eventually.*

He did not know if the promise was a comfort. He did not know if it was a threat. He knew only that the courier ships were departing — one south, one north — and that the summons had been issued, and that the city on the other side of the portal was waiting, and that he would be on one of the ships when they sailed.

### Scene 3: [[London (Modern)]] — The Private Office

**[POV]: Third Person — [[Stephen Davenport|Davenport]]**

The office was quiet. And now — now he had authorised a strategy that stopped correcting the king's mistake. Not because the mistake was harmless. Because the mistake was useful. He had not decided to do it. He had decided to let it happen. The distinction was real. It was also, he suspected, the kind of distinction that history would not make — and that God, if there was a God, would not accept.

He looked at the three documents on his desk. The king's acceptance. The courier dispatch. The draft agenda. He did not pray. He had never prayed. But he sat in the silence — the same silence he had chosen not to fill — and he waited, and the waiting was not prayer but was not nothing either, and the grey light through the window held the office like a question that had not been asked and would not be answered until the summit began.
