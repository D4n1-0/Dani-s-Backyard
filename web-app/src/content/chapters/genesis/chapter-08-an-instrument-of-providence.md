# Chapter 08 – An Instrument of Providence

## Summary

The morning after [[Malcolm Renfield|Renfield]]'s return, his report circulates through the international command — twelve pages of black-and-white observation that force the coalition to confront what the [[Crown of England]] actually is. On the hangar bay of *[[USS Gerald R. Ford (CVN-78)|USS Gerald R. Ford]]*, the newest carrier in the fleet and now the coalition's hub, [[Terrence Kohler|Rear Admiral Kohler]] convenes the senior military leadership to face the question the report raises: does this change their posture? The fracture between France and Britain surfaces immediately. [[James Hawthorne|Hawthorne]] argues that the Crown cannot reform without destroying itself; [[Étienne Roussel|Roussel]] argues that engagement without conditions is complicity. Before the meeting can resolve, word arrives: [[Rebecca Osei|Lieutenant Commander Osei]] is returning through the portal, and [[Inquisitor Paulus|Inquisitor Paulus]] has arrived ahead of her, demanding a full Inquisition examination of a modern soul. In the bowels of the carrier, Paulus performs [[The Sifting]] on [[Rebecca Osei|Osei]] — a brutal, intimate ritual that tests the secular framework to its breaking point and finds something neither blankness nor taint: a soul that has been moved by the Crown's world without being claimed by it. Paulus leaves without conclusion. In modern London, [[Bishop Aldric]] receives an unexpected visitor — [[Peter Chisholm|Archbishop Chisholm]] — and the two men of God from opposing worlds discover that they are not as far apart as either had feared, and not as close as either had hoped. [[Rebecca Osei|Osei]], freed from the Sifting, delivers [[King Robert's Letter|a letter]] to [[Stephen Davenport|Davenport]] aboard the *[[USS Gerald R. Ford (CVN-78)|Ford]]* — a letter from a king who believes the modern world is the answer to eight centuries of prayer. [[Stephen Davenport|Davenport]], alone with the vellum and the crimson seal, must decide how to reply to a man who believes in Providence and has named him its instrument.

## Scenes

### Scene 1: *[[USS Gerald R. Ford (CVN-78)|USS Gerald R. Ford]]* — The Hangar Bay

**[TIME]: Day 12 — 2026-06-29, 07:00 Zulu / 1914-09-25, 19:00 TC**

**[POV]: Third Person — [[James Hawthorne|Hawthorne]]*

The *[[USS Gerald R. Ford (CVN-78)|Ford]]*'s hangar bay had been emptied of aircraft. The Super Hornets and the Growlers and the Lightning IIs had been struck down to the maintenance bays below, the deck crews ordered to clear the forward two hundred feet for an improvised conference space. Folding tables had been set in a rough rectangle — NSN-standard, the grey polymer tables the Navy used for everything from mess to mass casualty — and laid with bottled water, coffee urns, and the NATO-standardised notepads that somehow followed the American fleet wherever it sailed. The bay still smelled of jet fuel and hydraulic fluid and the cold salt of the Atlantic, gusting through the open doors that faced the portal on the starboard side. The grey Atlantic rolled past, indifferent. Through the open bay doors, the shimmer was visible on the horizon — a vertical seam of silver light, patient and permanent, and around it the assembled fleet: the *[[USS Harry S. Truman (CVN-75)|Truman]]*, the *Queen Elizabeth*, the French division, the Dutch and Norwegian and Spanish frigates, the whole weight of modern naval power keeping station on a problem it could not solve.

[[Terrence Kohler|Kohler]] was already at the head of the table when [[James Hawthorne|Hawthorne]] arrived — a rear admiral with the permanent squint of a man who had spent thirty years staring at horizons, his uniform crisp, his coffee untouched, the copy of [[Malcolm Renfield|Renfield]]'s report face-down beside his notepad as if he were not yet ready to look at it again. [[Daniel Brennan|Brennan]] stood to his right, arms folded, his expression unreadable. [[Étienne Roussel|Roussel]] had taken the far side of the table — the French commander in his blue uniform, his bearing carrying the Maritime Nationale's particular combination of formal dignity and Gallic skepticism. A Dutch captain — *Van der Graaf*, [[James Hawthorne|Hawthorne]] recalled, from *[[HNLMS Tromp (F803)|HNLMS Tromp]]* — occupied the mid-point, and a Norwegian commander from one of the Fridtjof Nansen-class frigates stood near the coffee urn, looking as though he wished he had not been invited.

There were no civilians. [[William Ashford|Ashford]] had argued that the Foreign Office should be represented — had, in fact, argued that the entire meeting should be held in London, in a COBR room or a Cabinet Office suite, where the political framework could absorb the report's implications before the military commanders acted on them. [[Terrence Kohler|Kohler]] had disagreed. The report was about the Crown's *nature*, not its diplomatic posture. The nature of an adversary — or an ally, or something that was neither word — was a military question before it was a political one, and [[Terrence Kohler|Kohler]] was not going to let the civilians spend a week arguing about adjectives while the fleet held station beside a portal to a parallel dimension. He had convened the meeting at dawn, on his ship, without the Foreign Office, and he had let it be known through channels that the purpose was not to debate [[Malcolm Renfield|Renfield]]'s conclusions but to answer the single question those conclusions raised.

[[Terrence Kohler|Kohler]] began without preamble.

"We have the report. You have all read the report. The British Foreign Office's envoy crossed into Faithful Crown territory, spent less than twenty-four hours in their London, and produced twelve pages of assessment that this room now has to act on — or not act on. I am not interested in debating whether the report is accurate. [[Malcolm Renfield|Renfield]]'s credentials are not in question. The question is whether this changes our posture, and if so, how."

He looked around the table. The hum of the *[[USS Gerald R. Ford (CVN-78)|Ford]]*'s reactors filled the silence — a low, steady vibration through the deck plates, the carrier's heartbeat, the sound of a machine that had been built to project power into any ocean on any world and had never expected to project it into a different reality entirely.

[[Étienne Roussel|Roussel]] spoke first.

"With respect, Admiral, the report changes everything." [[Étienne Roussel|Roussel]]'s English was excellent — accented but precise, the English of a man who had trained at the NATO Defence College in Rome and had learned to argue in three languages before breakfast. "We have now been told, in the words of the envoy himself, that the [[Crown of England]] is a theocracy that 'has confused mercy with weakness' and 'has normalised cruelty as a form of order.' These are not diplomatic infelicities. These are not cultural misunderstandings. These are the structures of a state that we cannot — that France cannot — engage as a neutral partner. The Crown does not see its own cruelty. That is the envoy's central finding. A partner that does not see its own cruelty is a partner that will repeat it, and we will be complicit in that repetition."

[[James Hawthorne|Hawthorne]] had not intended to speak first. He had intended to listen, to let the room find its own shape, to reserve his words for the moment when they were needed. But [[Étienne Roussel|Roussel]] had placed the question exactly where it belonged — not in the realm of military procedure but in the realm of moral judgment — and [[James Hawthorne|Hawthorne]] had spent five days now wrestling with the same question and had not reached a comfortable answer.

"The Crown does not see its own cruelty," [[James Hawthorne|Hawthorne]] said. "That is the report's finding. But it is also the report's tragedy. [[Malcolm Renfield|Renfield]] was clear: the Crown is not evil. It is blind. Eight centuries of continuous holy war have done something to them — have reshaped their understanding of mercy, justice, the dignity of the individual — and they cannot see what they have become because the war that shaped them is the same war that every living Faithful soul has spent its entire existence fighting."

He paused. The room was listening. [[Terrence Kohler|Kohler]]'s expression was unreadable. [[Daniel Brennan|Brennan]] had uncrossed his arms.

"The Crown is not going to reform because we ask it to," [[James Hawthorne|Hawthorne]] continued. "It *cannot* reform — not in the way Capitaine de vaisseau [[Étienne Roussel|Roussel]] is proposing. The public penance, the Office of the Propagation of Virtue, the interrogation chambers beneath New Antioch — these are not optional features of the Faithful state. They are the machinery by which it survives. You cannot amputate the Office of the Propagation of Virtue and expect the Crown's military to continue functioning. You cannot abolish the public penance and expect the Faithful to continue accepting the sacrifices the war demands of them. Faith and cruelty are welded together in that world. You cannot separate them without destroying the foundation on which the Crown's entire civilisation stands."

"Then we are not talking about reform," [[Étienne Roussel|Roussel]] said. "We are talking about acceptance. Capitaine de frégate [[James Hawthorne|Hawthorne]] is asking us to accept the Crown as it is — cruelty and all — because the alternative is worse. That is not diplomacy. That is appeasement."

"It is patience," [[James Hawthorne|Hawthorne]] said. "The Crown has had eight hundred years to become what it is. It will not change in eight days. If we impose conditions now — if we signal that our engagement is conditional on their conforming to our standards — they will interpret that as rejection. And a rejected Faithful Crown is a Faithful Crown that retreats into suspicion, closes its borders, and treats the portal as a threat rather than an opportunity. We will have lost any chance of influencing them toward reform, and we will have lost it because we demanded the reform before we had earned the right to ask."

[[Étienne Roussel|Roussel]] shook his head. "This is the argument of the coloniser who says: *we must trade with the slavers because trade opens doors.* The doors may open. What walks through them is not always liberation."

The silence that followed was heavy. [[James Hawthorne|Hawthorne]] could feel the fracture spreading — not just between him and [[Étienne Roussel|Roussel]], but across the whole room. The coalition that had assembled in such high purpose over the past ten days was discovering that purpose was not the same as agreement, and that agreement on the nature of the Crown was harder than agreement on the need to contain it.

[[Terrence Kohler|Kohler]] had been watching the exchange without intervening. Now he leaned forward, his hands flat on the table.

"The report changes how we see them," he said. "It does not, in my judgment, change what we are doing here. We are here to maintain a defensive cordon, to prevent escalation, and to create the space for diplomacy to function. The Crown has not attacked us. The Crown has shown no hostile intent toward any vessel in this cordon. The Crown has, in fact, demonstrated remarkable restraint in the face of a fleet that could end their civilisation in a single afternoon. I am not prepared to alter our Rules of Engagement or our diplomatic posture on the basis of a report that confirms —" he tapped the document — "that the Crown is exactly what it told us it was: a theocracy at war with Hell. We knew that before [[Malcolm Renfield|Renfield]] crossed. We know it more clearly now. The question is what we do with the knowledge, and my answer is: we hold. We hold, and we let the diplomats earn the time for the Crown to learn what it cannot learn from us in a week."

[[Étienne Roussel|Roussel]] did not look satisfied. [[James Hawthorne|Hawthorne]] did not look satisfied. [[Daniel Brennan|Brennan]] still had not spoken. The Dutch captain studied his notepad as if it contained answers the room had not yet found.

And then the door at the aft end of the hangar bay opened, and a young lieutenant commander in the uniform of the US Navy — [[Terrence Kohler|Kohler]]'s flag aide — crossed the bay at a pace that was not quite a run but was certainly not a walk.

He bent and spoke quietly to [[Terrence Kohler|Kohler]]. [[Terrence Kohler|Kohler]] listened, nodded once, and turned back to the table.

"It seems the other side has sent us a visitor. [[Inquisitor Paulus|Inquisitor Paulus of New Antioch]] has crossed the threshold. He is aboard one of our destroyers at the portal line, and he is requesting —" [[Terrence Kohler|Kohler]] paused, as if the word he was about to use required a moment of reflection — "permission to conduct an examination. A full Inquisition-level protocol. On [[Rebecca Osei|Lieutenant Commander Osei]], who is returning from the Faithful Crown's London at approximately the same moment. He wishes to examine her before she debriefs."

The room absorbed this. [[James Hawthorne|Hawthorne]] felt something cold settle in his chest. [[Rebecca Osei|Osei]] — who had crossed as the first modern human into the Faithful Crown's world, who had spent a week in their London, who had watched their streets and spoken to their king and carried [[Father Beauchamp|Beauchamp]]'s crucifix against her skin. And now the Faithful Crown's own inquisitor wanted to test her before she could tell anyone what she had seen.

"Does she consent?" [[James Hawthorne|Hawthorne]] asked.

"She has not been asked yet. She is still in transit."

"Then she cannot be examined without consent. That is —"

"I am aware of what it is, Captain Hawthorne. The question is not whether we force her. The question is whether we allow it, if she agrees." [[Terrence Kohler|Kohler]] looked around the table. "The inquisitor is not asking for a diplomatic reception. He is not asking for a tour. He is asking to perform a religious ritual on one of our officers, in controlled conditions, without witnesses, using implements and procedures that we do not understand and cannot verify. The legal framework for this does not exist. The diplomatic framework for this does not exist. The medical framework for this does not exist. We are being asked to invent a protocol in the space of a helicopter flight."

"Then refuse him," [[Étienne Roussel|Roussel]] said. "This is exactly the kind of entanglement the [[Cautious Secular Engagement|CSE doctrine]] was designed to prevent. We do not participate in the theological procedures of a foreign state. We do not submit our personnel to their rituals. That is the doctrine."

"The doctrine was designed before we knew the Faithful Crown existed," [[Terrence Kohler|Kohler]] said. "And the inquisitor is not asking. He crossed the threshold without permission — which he is entitled to do, since the portal is international and open — and he is now on one of our ships, insisting that his mandate from the Patriarch of New Antioch supersedes any authority we might imagine we have to refuse him. He is being polite. He is also being immovable." He turned to [[James Hawthorne|Hawthorne]]. "This is your officer, Captain. What is your recommendation?"

[[James Hawthorne|Hawthorne]] thought about [[Rebecca Osei|Osei]] — about the way she had stood in the bow of the RIB at the first parley, empty hands open, steady in the face of a priest who had asked if she served the Adversary. About the way she had volunteered to cross, knowing that she might not return. About the Orichalcum crucifix she wore and the week she had spent in a city that had been fighting Hell for eight centuries. She was not the kind of officer who needed protection from questions. She was the kind of officer who answered them.

"If she consents," [[James Hawthorne|Hawthorne]] said, "we let her. And we let the inquisitor see what she is."

### Scene 2: *[[USS Gerald R. Ford (CVN-78)|USS Gerald R. Ford]]* — The Bowels

**[TIME]: Day 12 — 2026-06-29, 14:00 Zulu / 1914-09-26, 02:00 TC**

**[POV]: Third Person — [[Rebecca Osei|Osei]]*

The compartment was below the waterline — a storage bay normally used for aviation spares, stripped of its shelving and its equipment and its fluorescent light. The deck crew had cleared it in thirty minutes flat, on [[Terrence Kohler|Kohler]]'s orders, and the result was a rectangular steel box thirty feet by twenty, lit only by the candles that the inquisitor had placed at the cardinal points. The air was cold, the Atlantic leaching through the hull, and the hum of the reactors was louder here — a constant presence, as if the carrier were breathing around them.

[[Rebecca Osei|Osei]] sat on a steel folding chair in the centre of the compartment. Her uniform was the same one she had worn across the portal seven days ago — washed, pressed, the rank slides on her shoulders catching the candlelight in small glints of gold. The [[Orichalcum]] crucifix [[Father Beauchamp|Beauchamp]] had given her hung around her neck, its surface dark and unresponsive. She had not removed it. She had not considered removing it. Whatever the inquisitor was about to do, it would not be made easier by the absence of a cross.

[[Inquisitor Paulus|Paulus]] was working his way around the compartment, anointing the bulkheads with chrism — the same silver residue she had seen on the water during the parley, the same blessed substance that [[Father Beauchamp|Beauchamp]] had used when he crossed. He moved slowly, methodically, his grey robes whispering against the steel deck, his Orichalcum-tipped staff tapping a rhythm that was almost musical. The prayers were in Latin — old Latin, the Latin of the Synods and the saints — and his voice was soft, almost gentle, the voice of a man who had spent years asking questions and had learned that the quietest voice often produced the truest answers.

He had not introduced himself. He had not explained what he was about to do. He had only looked at her — those grey eyes, grey as the Atlantic, grey as the harbour at Dover in winter — and asked a single question: *Do you consent?*

She had answered without hesitation. *Yes.*

The chain of command had not been consulted. The Foreign Office had not been consulted. The lawyers who were, even now, arguing about whether the [[Cautious Secular Engagement]] doctrine permitted or prohibited the ritual that was about to occur had not been consulted. [[Rebecca Osei|Osei]] had consulted herself — her own conscience, her own body, her own knowledge of what she had seen in the Faithful Crown's London and what she had learned about the people who lived there — and she had decided that the inquisitor's examination was not a threat. It was a gift. A chance to prove, beyond any theological argument or diplomatic protocol, that the modern world was what it claimed to be.

She believed in her own soul. She had never had reason to doubt it before. She was not about to start now.

The inquisitor completed his circuit of the compartment and stopped in front of her. The candles flickered — the carrier's ventilation system breathed through the space with a constant low murmur, and the flames danced in its current. The chrism was drying on the bulkheads, leaving silver trails like the tracks of snails across the grey-painted steel. The compartment smelled of incense now — something ancient, something liturgical, something that belonged in a cathedral rather than in the bowels of an American aircraft carrier.

"Lieutenant Commander [[Rebecca Osei|Osei]]." The inquisitor's voice was quiet, but the compartment carried it — the steel walls bouncing the sound back in a way that made it feel larger than it was. "You have consented to the [[The Sifting|Sifting]]. Do you understand what the Sifting is?"

"I understand that it is a deeper protocol than the [[Rite of Discernment]]. I understand that it is designed to detect hidden taint. I understand that you will test me — physically and verbally — and that the test is not about my beliefs. It is about my nature."

The inquisitor inclined his head. "A precise summary. You have been briefed."

"I have been living in your London for seven days, Inquisitor. I have asked many questions."

"I am sure you have." If the inquisitor was surprised that she had learned the details of an obscure Inquisition protocol while living as a guest in the Faithful Crown's capital, he did not show it. "Then you will also know that the Sifting is not gentle. The Orichalcum staff must rest against your sternum — against the skin, not the uniform — and it will remain there for the duration of the silent phase. The questions I ask will be personal. They will touch on matters that you may prefer to keep private. You may refuse to answer any question. I must advise you that refusal will be noted, but it will not — contrary to the reputation my Office has perhaps acquired — be held against you."

[[Rebecca Osei|Osei]] almost smiled. The reputation that the Office had acquired was not a matter of speculation. She had seen the Office's fortress on the Strand, its lower windows open to the interrogation chambers, the sound of chanting drifting from the upper floors. She had seen the man in the stocks and the woman marched to correction. She knew exactly what the Office was capable of.

And yet the inquisitor was being — she searched for the right word — *fair*. He was giving her the option to refuse. He was telling her what the procedure would involve. He was treating her as a subject with agency, not an object to be tested.

That was more than the man in the stocks had been offered.

"I understand," she said. "Proceed."

The inquisitor opened a small leather case that lay at his feet. From it he withdrew three objects: a vial of chrism, identical to the ones she had seen [[Father Beauchamp|Beauchamp]] use; a small silver thurible, already smoking with incense; and a strip of linen, white and unmarked, that he held between two fingers as if it were a relic.

He approached her. The Orichalcum staff he left standing — it was tall enough to rest on the deck at an angle, its silver-blue surface catching the candlelight and returning it in shards of cold fire.

"I will anoint you now," he said. "Forehead, sternum, and both palms. The chrism is blessed, not cursed. It will not harm you. You may feel warmth, or cold, or nothing at all. Whatever you feel, do not resist it."

She nodded.

The inquisitor dipped his thumb into the vial of chrism and drew it across her forehead — a single line, from hairline to bridge of nose. The chrism was cool, and it left the same silver residue she had seen on the water during the parley. He repeated the motion on the bare skin above her collarbone — she had unbuttoned the top of her uniform, as instructed, and the air of the compartment was cold against her throat. Then he took her hands — one at a time, palm upward — and drew the chrism in a cross on each palm.

She felt warmth. That was unexpected. The chrism itself was cool, but something beneath it — something in the way the silver residue caught the candlelight, something in the prayer the inquisitor was murmuring under his breath — generated a faint, spreading warmth that had no physical source she could identify. It was not uncomfortable. It was not frightening. It was simply — present. An acknowledgment. A recognition.

The inquisitor finished the anointment and stepped back. "The silent phase begins now. I will place the staff against your sternum. You will remain still. You will not speak. You will simply — be. Do you understand?"

"Yes."

He lifted the Orichalcum staff. It was heavier than it looked — she could see the effort in his forearms as he brought it level — and the tip, polished to a mirror sheen, came to rest in the hollow at the base of her throat, directly above the chrism cross. The metal was cold against her skin. Not the cold of the Atlantic, not the cold of the compartment's air-conditioned ventilation, but a deeper cold — a cold that seemed to come from the metal itself, from whatever property made Orichalcum more than steel, from whatever blessing had been laid into its forging.

The inquisitor held the staff steady. His grey eyes fixed on her face — not her eyes, but her mouth, her throat, the pulse visible at her temple. He was reading her. She understood now what the silent phase was for. It was not about the staff. The staff was an instrument, but the reading was the point — the way her body responded to the metal's pressure, the way her breathing changed, the way her pulse quickened or slowed or held steady. The inquisitor was a scholar of the body's betrayals. He had spent years in the interrogation chambers beneath New Antioch, and he had learned to read a soul the way a surgeon reads a wound.

She held still.

The seconds passed. A minute. Two. The staff remained cold. Her pulse remained steady. Her breathing remained even. She was not afraid — or rather, she was afraid, but the fear was a tool like any other, useful for sharpening the attention, useless for making decisions. She had stopped being afraid of being afraid a long time ago.

The inquisitor withdrew the staff. The silent phase was over.

"The verbal phase begins now," he said. His voice was quieter than before — not softer, but deeper, as if the Sifting had opened something in him that the anointment had only prepared. "I will ask you questions. You will answer them. You may answer honestly or not, as you choose. The Sifting will know the difference. Lie to me, and the staff will remain cold. Lie to yourself, and the Sifting will find the lie regardless. Do you understand?"

"Yes."

"Then tell me, Lieutenant Commander [[Rebecca Osei|Osei]]: what do you fear?"

The question was simple. The answer was not.

She thought about the man in the stocks — the bruised hands, the bowed head, the silence of a crowd that had learned not to see what was in front of them. She thought about the woman marched to correction for speaking without approval. She thought about the children in the fields, carrying pikes against a raid that might never come, raised in a world where every prayer was a weapon and every act of mercy was measured against the possibility that it might be exploited by the Adversary. She thought about [[Malcolm Renfield|Renfield]]'s face as he walked through the Strand — the careful neutrality of a career diplomat cracking under the weight of what he was seeing. She thought about the report he had filed, and she thought about what it would mean for the world she was returning to.

"I fear that your world has taught mine something we did not want to learn," she said. "That righteousness and cruelty can live in the same house. That faith can justify anything, if you believe hard enough. That the difference between a crusader and a tyrant is smaller than any of us want to admit."

The inquisitor's expression did not change. But something in his posture shifted — a fractional straightening of the spine, a micro-movement of the hand on the staff.

"The [[Crown of England]] is not a tyranny," he said. It was not a defense. It was a challenge.

"I didn't say it was. I said the difference between a crusader and a tyrant is smaller than any of us want to admit. Your world has spent eight centuries fighting a war for the survival of the human soul. That war has shaped everything — your laws, your customs, your understanding of right and wrong. You have no concept of civilian life as we understand it. Every citizen is a soldier. Every prayer is a weapon. And somewhere along the way, you stopped being able to tell the difference between justice and cruelty." She paused. The staff was still cold. Her voice was steady. "That is what I fear. Not that your world is evil. That it is righteous — and that its righteousness is a machine that has been running for so long that no one remembers how to turn it off."

The silence that followed was long. The inquisitor studied her face — the same clinical reading, the same careful attention to pulse and breath and the micro-movements of the throat. She returned his gaze without flinching.

"You have spoken to [[Malcolm Renfield|the envoy]]," the inquisitor said.

"I spoke to him in London. He asked me what I had observed. I told him the truth."

"And what truth was that?"

"That the Crown is not our enemy. That the Crown is not hostile. That the Crown is doing what it believes is right — and it cannot see that what it believes is right has become indistinguishable from what is cruel." She paused again. "You asked me what I fear. I fear that I am wrong. I fear that there is no way to help a people who have been fighting Hell for eight centuries without becoming something Hell would recognise. I fear that we will fail you — not because we want to, but because the problem you present is bigger than our politics can hold. And I fear that if we fail you, no one will come after us. You will be alone again — and the war will continue, and the stocks will fill, and the children will carry their pikes into the fields, and no one will ever know that there was a moment when everything might have changed."

The inquisitor lowered the staff. The metal scraped softly against the steel deck as he set it upright, and the candlelight caught its surface and threw a dozen tiny reflections across the grey-painted bulkheads.

"You are not afraid for yourself," he said. It was not a question.

"No."

"You are afraid for us. For the world you visited."

"Yes."

The inquisitor was silent for a long moment. Then he raised the staff again — not to her sternum this time, but held before him like a processional cross, the Orichalcum tip catching the light.

"I have examined souls in the interrogation chambers beneath New Antioch for twenty-three years," he said. "I have examined heretics and saints, traitors and martyrs, the possessed and the blessed and the merely confused. I have seen souls that were black with corruption and souls that were so pure they hurt to look at. I have never —" He stopped. The word that came next was difficult for him, and she could see him choosing it carefully, the way a scholar chooses a citation. "I have never touched a soul that was merely — present. Awake. Aware of itself and its responsibilities and the weight of what it had witnessed, without either the scarring of the Adversary or the indifference of blankness. The Rite of Discernment found nothing in you. The Sifting finds — something else. Something the Rite cannot name. You are not touched by Hell. You are not touched by God. But you are touched — by us. By my world. By what you saw in our streets and what you heard in our prayers and what you witnessed in a square where a man was in the stocks and no one stopped to look at him."

He lowered the staff again. The verbal phase was over. The Sifting was over. "You are dismissed, Lieutenant Commander [[Rebecca Osei|Osei]]," the inquisitor said. "You may go. I have — I have no conclusion to offer. I will inform my superiors that the Sifting was inconclusive. That is the truth. It is also inadequate. I will need to think further."

He did not reply. He was already turning away, gathering his leather case and his thurible and his chrism, preparing to leave the steel box where he had tested a modern soul and found something he could not categorise.

### Scene 3: [[London (Modern)]] — The Hotel Room

**[POV]: Third Person — [[Bishop Aldric]]*

The silence that followed was heavy. The *[[USS Gerald R. Ford (CVN-78)|Ford]]*'s reactors hummed through the deck plates. The portal shimmered on the darkening horizon. The letter lay on the steel desk between them, its crimson seal catching the fluorescent light, its elegant dark hand spelling out a question that no Prime Minister had ever been trained to answer.

[[Stephen Davenport|Davenport]] looked at the vellum and thought about [[Peter Chisholm|Chisholm]] — about the question [[Peter Chisholm|Chisholm]] had asked him after the COBR meeting, the question that had started all of this. *What if the Faithful are not wrong, only mistaken about what it means?* What if [[King Robert the Longsword|King Robert]] was right — right that the modern world had been preserved for a purpose, right that the portal had opened at a specific moment for a specific reason, right that the two worlds were now joined in a way that could not be undone — and wrong only about what the purpose was? What if the purpose was not rescue? What if the purpose was witness? What if the modern world was not the answer to the Faithful Crown's prayers but the evidence that the Faithful Crown's prayers had been heard — and that the answer, when it came, would not look like deliverance? It would look like a secular Prime Minister, sitting in a steel cabin on an American aircraft carrier, holding a letter from a king who believed in Providence and trying to figure out how to reply without lying and without breaking everything?

He did not know the answer. He did not know if there was an answer. He knew only that he had to write the reply — that the letter on the desk was an offer, and that offers expired, and that the longer he delayed, the stronger the suspicion would grow on the other side of the portal that the modern world was not what it claimed to be.

He reached for a pen.

"I don't know what I am going to write," he said. "But I think I need to write it alone."

[[Rebecca Osei|Osei]] nodded. She stood, straightened her uniform — the gesture was automatic, the muscle memory of an officer who had been standing at attention for so long that she no longer noticed the weight — and paused at the door.

"Prime Minister," she said. "He is a good man. [[King Robert the Longsword|King Robert]]. He is not a saint, and he is not naive, and he is certainly not unaware of the strategic value of framing our arrival as divine intervention. But he is a good man. He asked me about our hospitals, our schools, our laws for protecting children. He asked me if our people were happy. He asked me if we had ever known war — real war, the kind his world has known for eight centuries — and when I told him we had not, he wept. Not for himself. For us. For the world that had never had to learn what his world had learned. He wept, Prime Minister. A warrior-king who has stood on the bridge of a dreadnought and faced the Heretic fleet in battle, and he wept because we had never known the kind of suffering that his people have known every day for eight hundred years."

She paused. "You do not have to believe in Providence to reply to a man who does. You only have to be honest."

She left. The door clicked shut behind her.

[[Stephen Davenport|Davenport]] was alone with the letter and the humming carrier and the portal on the darkening horizon. He looked at the crimson seal — the cross, the lion, the weight of a kingdom that had been fighting Hell for longer than his entire civilisation had existed. He looked at the words *instrument of Providence* and the phrase *the answered prayer of a people who have been praying for deliverance for eight centuries* and the impossible, generous, terrifying assumption that his world was a gift.

He uncapped the pen. He pulled a sheet of paper — ordinary paper, A4, the paper of his own world's bureaucracy — from the notepad on [[Terrence Kohler|Kohler]]'s desk. He wrote the first line.

*Your Majesty —*

He stopped.

He could not write *I am not the answer to your prayers.* That was true, but it was also cruel, and it was also — he was not certain, any longer, that it was entirely true. He was the Prime Minister of a country that had built an aircraft carrier that could launch thirty-six F-35Bs and cross a dimensional portal in the process. He was the political head of a government that commanded a fleet capable of ending the Faithful Crown's civilisation in a single afternoon. If the Faithful Crown had been praying for deliverance — for someone to arrive, at the hour of their greatest need, with the power to change their world — then the modern fleet was, objectively, an answer to that prayer. It was not the answer the Faithful expected. It was not the answer the Faithful wanted. But it was an answer. It was *here*.

He could not write *we are a secular democracy and we will not adopt your theology.* That was true, but it was also a refusal — a refusal of the framework in which [[King Robert the Longsword|King Robert]] had offered the alliance — and the refusal would be heard as rejection, and the rejection would be heard as hostility, and the hostility would destroy everything the coalition had built.

He could not write *we accept your framing.* That was false. He was not an instrument of Providence. He was a politician who had been carried into office by a constitutional crisis and was now holding together a coalition government by the thinnest of majorities, and he did not believe that God had hidden his world from the Adversary or preserved it for a purpose or delivered it at the hour of the Faithful's greatest need. He believed that the portal was a physical phenomenon — inexplicable, transformative, perhaps permanently inexplicable — and that his job was to manage its consequences in a way that protected his country and served the interests of humanity as a whole.

But he could not write any of that. Not to a king who had wept at the thought of a world that had never known war.

He capped the pen. He set it aside. He looked out the porthole at the portal — the silver scar on the darkening horizon, patient, permanent, waiting — and he understood, for the first time since the crisis had begun, that the question was not *how do I reply to a king who believes in Providence?* The question was *how do I reply to a king who is right — right that our arrival changes everything, right that the two worlds are now joined in a way that cannot be undone, right that whatever future we build we will build together — and wrong only about the name of the thing that brought us here?*

He did not know the answer. He did not know if there was an answer. He knew only that he would sit in this steel cabin, in the belly of an American aircraft carrier, on a sea that belonged to no world and both worlds at once, until he found one — or until he admitted that the question was too large for any single person to answer, and asked someone to help him carry it.

## Key Decisions

- [[Terrence Kohler|Kohler]] convenes the coalition's military leadership on the *[[USS Gerald R. Ford (CVN-78)|Ford]]*'s hangar bay — not a political COBR meeting, but an operational one. He rules that the report changes how they see the Crown but does not change their posture: hold the cordon, let the diplomats work.
- [[Étienne Roussel|Roussel]] demands conditioned engagement — France will not partner with a state that normalises cruelty. [[James Hawthorne|Hawthorne]] pushes for patience — the Crown cannot reform without destroying itself, and demanding reform before trust is established will lose them the chance to influence.
- The meeting is interrupted by news that Osei is returning and Paulus has crossed the threshold, demanding the Sifting. [[James Hawthorne|Hawthorne]] recommends allowing the examination if [[Rebecca Osei|Osei]] consents.
- [[Rebecca Osei|Osei]] voluntarily undergoes [[The Sifting]] — a brutal Inquisition protocol involving chrism, the Orichalcum staff, and intimate verbal interrogation. The Sifting finds neither infernal taint nor blankness, but *presence*: a soul that has been moved by the Crown's suffering without being claimed by it. [[Inquisitor Paulus|Paulus]] leaves without conclusion — disturbed, thoughtful, and far less certain than when he arrived.
- [[Bishop Aldric]], continuing his pilgrimage in modern London, receives an unexpected visit from [[Peter Chisholm|Archbishop Chisholm]]. The two exchange theology across eight centuries of divergence. [[Peter Chisholm|Chisholm]] articulates the *Deus Absconditus* — the hidden God who conceals Himself to enable free faith. [[Bishop Aldric]] admits he was wrong about the modern world's faith — it is not absent, only quiet — and discovers a kind of wonder he had not expected to find.
- [[Rebecca Osei|Osei]] delivers [[King Robert's Letter]] directly to [[Stephen Davenport|Davenport]] aboard the *[[USS Gerald R. Ford (CVN-78)|Ford]]*. Robert writes as one sovereign to another, calling the modern world "an instrument of Providence" and offering a full military, diplomatic, and economic alliance — framed not as threat but as gratitude.
- [[Stephen Davenport|Davenport]], alone with the letter, discovers that he cannot refuse the framing without rejecting the alliance, and cannot accept the framing without lying. He does not resolve the dilemma. He sits down to write the reply, and the chapter ends with the pen capped, the question open.
