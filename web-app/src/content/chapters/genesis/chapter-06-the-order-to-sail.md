# Chapter 06 – The Order to Sail

## Summary

As diplomatic frameworks stall in Brussels and Whitehall, the military reality of [[The Anomaly|the portal]] forces a faster clock. The [[United States]] orders Carrier Strike Group Two, built around *[[USS Harry S. Truman (CVN-75)|USS Harry S. Truman]]*, to break from its Atlantic exercise and proceed east at best speed. The [[United Kingdom (Modern)|Royal Navy]] activates *[[HMS Queen Elizabeth (R08)|HMS Queen Elizabeth]]* from Portsmouth, assembling a full [[UK Carrier Strike Group]] with her own destroyer screen, while [[France]] dispatches a division of Horizon and Aquitaine-class destroyers from Brest to augment the NATO cordon. Over four days, admirals, captains, and logistics officers wrestle with the unglamorous arithmetic of expeditionary force projection — fuel, ammunition, [[Rules of Engagement]], and the unspoken question of what exactly they are sailing toward. On the Trench Crusade side of [[The Portal Threshold]], Captain [[Alistair Pembroke]] watches the modern armada assemble through the shimmer — silhouette by silhouette, day after day — and understands for the first time the true scale of the world on the other side. By the time the combined fleet begins to coalesce around the portal, a wordless understanding passes through both sides of the threshold. The gathering is complete. The next move belongs to the other side.

## Scenes

### Scene 1: *[[USS Harry S. Truman (CVN-75)|USS Harry S. Truman]]* — Break and Proceed

**[TIME]: Day 7 — 2026-06-24, 04:00 Zulu**
**[POV]: Third Person — [[Daniel Brennan|Brennan]]*

The Truman was running an [[Anti-Submarine Warfare|anti-submarine warfare]] (ASW) serial when the call came in.

[[Daniel Brennan|Brennan]] was in the combat information centre (CIC), watching the sonar picture develop — a contact that was probably a whale but had to be treated as something else, because the Atlantic had become a place where the word *something* no longer meant what it had meant a week ago. The display showed the exercise box: the Truman at the centre, her escort screen deployed in a standard ASW formation, the simulated tracks of friendly submarines and the speculative tracks of threat contacts that existed only in the scenario injects.

The contact was at one-eight-zero, twelve nautical miles, moving south at eight knots. Brennan had been watching it for twenty minutes, listening to his ASW officer's analysis — *probably a sei whale, sir, the signature is too clean for a sub, but I'm keeping two sets of ears on it* — and he had been about to call it a routine observation and move on to the next serial when the circuit changed.

The voice that broke the exercise net was not the [[Exercise Controller|exercise controller]] (EXCON).

It was the flag duty officer at [[US Fleet Forces Command]] (FFC), Norfolk, speaking on a circuit that bypassed the exercise chain of command entirely. Brennan had heard that circuit used exactly once before, in a drill scenario designed to test the chain of command for nuclear incidents. The voice was female, flat, professional, and utterly without context.

*TRUMAN, this is FFC. Authenticate Zulu Delta Seven. Break exercise. Proceed independently to staging area Whiskey. Coordinates follow in separate flash traffic. Best speed. Acknowledge.*

The CIC went silent. The ASW officer stopped mid-sentence. The contact at one-eight-zero ceased to matter.

Brennan reached for the authentication board on the console beside him. Zulu Delta Seven. He worked through the challenge-response protocol from memory — a reflex built from years of drills — and confirmed the match. The order was genuine. The authentication was dual-level, which meant it had been generated at a flag officer level and verified by a second authority. This was not a drill, not a test, not an exercise inject.

He keyed the mic. "TRUMAN copies. Breaking exercise. Proceeding to staging area Whiskey. Best speed. Acknowledge routing."

The FFC duty officer read back a series of coordinates and a frequency for flash traffic. Brennan wrote them on the back of his hand — a habit from flight school that he had never broken — and nodded to his operations officer, who was already pulling up the chart plot.

The exercise controller came on the net a moment later, his voice carrying the strain of a man who had just been informed that his exercise was cancelled by order of the four-star at Fleet Forces Command, and that he was to release the Truman and her screen for real-world tasking.

*TRUMAN, EXCON. Confirm you are detaching from the exercise serial.*

"Confirmed, EXCON. Detaching with screen. Will advise when clear of the exercise area."

A pause. Then the exercise controller's voice again, quieter, stripped of its command frequency formality: *Understood, Truman. Good hunting.*

Brennan acknowledged and turned to face the room. The CIC crew were watching him — the ASW officer, the tactical action officer (TAO), the junior enlisted personnel at their consoles, the young seaman who had been tracking the whale contact and now had nothing to track. They had all heard the order. They all understood what it meant. The exercise was over. Something real was happening.

"Set material condition Yankee," Brennan said. His voice was calm, deliberate — the voice of a man who had spent twenty-seven years training for this moment without ever believing it would arrive. "Recall air wing. Navigation, lay in course for the staging coordinates. Chief Engineer, my cabin in five minutes. TAO, begin drafting a transit plan — I want escort positions, emissions control (EMCON) state, and an anti-surface warfare (ASUW)/ASW threat assessment before we reach the edge of the exercise area."

The room moved. The ops officer began plotting the course. The TAO pulled up a blank transit template. The 1MC came to life — *All hands, set material condition Yankee. Set material condition Yankee throughout the ship.* — and the ship began to shift from exercise posture to real-world readiness.

Brennan did not wait to watch. He climbed the ladder to his sea cabin, two decks up, and closed the door behind him.

In his sea cabin, Brennan sat down at the small desk and pulled up the flash traffic. The coordinates resolved to a position in the Eastern Atlantic, roughly one hundred and fifty nautical miles west-southwest of the Isles of Scilly. He had studied the chart of that area at the exercise staging conference — a NATO map with a red circle drawn in grease pencil and the words *ANOMALY — CLASSIFIED* stamped across it in block capitals.

The briefing had been a thirty-minute session in a secure conference room at US Naval Forces Europe (NAVEUR) HQ in Naples, delivered by a TWO-star who had looked like he had not slept since the cable arrived from London. The admiral had used the phrase *unknown interdimensional phenomenon* six times and the phrase *we have no idea what we're dealing with* eight times. Brennan had taken notes — the known dimensions of the distortion, the estimated depth of the energy signature, the fact that a British warship had made contact with a civilisation from a parallel Earth that called itself the Crown of England — and he had asked the only question that mattered.

*Admiral, what are our Rules of Engagement?*

The admiral had looked at him for a long moment. *Captain, we don't have any. You're going to be writing them on the way.*

Brennan had nodded, closed his notebook, and returned to his ship with the uncomfortable certainty that no one in the chain of command understood what they were asking him to sail toward.

The Chief Engineer knocked and entered. He was a commander with twenty-three years of service and a face that had been shaped by the engine rooms of five different ships — the *Kitty Hawk*, the *Stennis*, the *Bataan*, two destroyers whose names Brennan could never remember. He had the ship's fuel states memorised, and the expression of a man who had already done the arithmetic and did not like the answer.

"Sir. The Truman is fine — nuclear plant gives us all the time in the world. But the escorts —" He paused, working his jaw. "The *[[USS Gravely (DDG-107)|Gravely]]*, the *[[USS Stout (DDG-55)|Stout]]*, the *[[USS Bainbridge (DDG-96)|Bainbridge]]*. They're not topped off. We were supposed to have an underway replenishment (UNREP) in three days as part of the exercise schedule. A USNS tanker, standard drill. But we're not doing the drill anymore. We're sprinting east. And the escort fuel states are —"

"Give me the numbers."

"*[[USS Gravely (DDG-107)|Gravely]]*: fifty-one percent. *[[USS Stout (DDG-55)|Stout]]*: forty-four. *[[USS Bainbridge (DDG-96)|Bainbridge]]*: the best of the three at fifty-eight, because she did a shaft repair two days ago and they didn't run her at full transit speed during the repair window. I've done the calculations based on best speed to the staging area." He pulled a folded sheet of paper from his pocket and placed it on the desk. "*[[USS Gravely (DDG-107)|Gravely]]* arrives with approximately eleven percent. *[[USS Stout (DDG-55)|Stout]]* with nine. *[[USS Bainbridge (DDG-96)|Bainbridge]]* with sixteen, but that's assuming no higher-speed manoeuvring en route — one ASW sprint, one evasion drill, and she's down to twelve."

Brennan looked at the numbers. Eleven percent was not a fuel state. It was a distress call waiting to happen. If the *[[USS Gravely (DDG-107)|Gravely]]* arrived on station with eleven percent and was required to manoeuvre immediately — a threat contact, a warning shot, a collision avoidance turn — she would be burning emergency reserves within hours.

"The nearest USNS tanker is four hundred miles south of our track," the Chief Engineer said. "I checked. The *Leroy Grumman*. She's waiting for tasking orders that no one has issued yet. If we can get her diverted north, we can set up a mid-transit UNREP at the two-thirds point. That gives the escorts enough fuel to reach station with twenty-plus percent and a full day of manoeuvre margin."

"Do it."

"I don't have the authority to divert a USNS vessel, sir."

"You don't need it. You need to make the call. Tell the *Grumman*'s captain what we need. If he says yes, I'll handle the paperwork."

The Chief Engineer nodded. "Aye, sir." He paused at the door. "Sir. One more thing."

"Go ahead."

"The crew. They know we're not going to an exercise. The word is already out. I had a petty officer in main propulsion ask me if we were going to war. I told him I didn't know." He met Brennan's eyes. "Do I?"

Brennan considered the question. "We are going to a location where an unknown phenomenon has opened a connection to a parallel reality. We are going to join a multinational fleet that is assembling to observe, contain, and if necessary defend against threats from that reality. We do not know if those threats exist. We do not know if the parallel civilisation is hostile. We do not know what we will find when we get there." He paused. "That is not war. But it is not peace, either. Tell your petty officer to treat it as a deployment to a contested environment. That is the accurate answer."

"Aye, sir."

The Chief Engineer left. Brennan sat alone in the small cabin, the flash traffic printout in his hand, the coordinates of the portal burned into his memory. The ship hummed around him — the vibration of the nuclear plant through the hull, the distant sound of the 1MC calling the air wing to ready rooms, the footsteps of the officer of the deck (OOD) on the bridge above, the muffled roar of the catapult crew testing the steam system. The routine sounds of a carrier preparing to move.

He looked at the coordinates again. The red dot in the Eastern Atlantic. The place where the world had changed.

He had commanded the Truman for eighteen months. He had sailed her through the Strait of Hormuz, where Iranian fast-attack craft had shadowed his formation at disrespectful range. He had sailed her through the South China Sea, where Chinese destroyers had cut across his bow to make a point. He had sailed her through the Norwegian Sea, where Russian submarines had played cat-and-mouse with his ASW screen for three straight weeks. He had launched strikes against ISIS in the badlands of eastern Syria, run air defence for the withdrawal from Afghanistan, stood watch in the dark hours of the Indian Ocean while the ship's company slept and the navigation lights of distant freighters slid past like ghosts.

He had never sailed toward something he could not define.

The briefing in Naples had used words — *interdimensional*, *anomaly*, *jurisdiction*, *theocratic parallel civilisation* — but the words were containers for a reality that no one had yet filled. Brennan did not know what he would find at the coordinates. He knew only that the President of the United States had decided that the US Navy needed to be there, and that the Truman was the closest carrier to the portal, and that the order had come from Norfolk with a two-man authentication and the weight of history behind it.

*Best speed*, the message had said. As if speed would solve the problem of not knowing what you were running toward.

He folded the coordinates, tucked them into his pocket, and walked out of the cabin.

On the bridge, the navigation team was already laying in the course. The officer of the deck — a lieutenant commander with twelve years of service and a calm that Brennan had come to rely on — stood at the chart table, tracing a line from the Truman's current position to the staging area. The escort screen had already begun to reform, the destroyers moving into their transit positions with the practiced economy of a team that had done this a hundred times.

"Captain on the bridge."

Brennan acknowledged the call and took his position at the forward window. The Atlantic stretched ahead of him, grey and featureless, the horizon a clean line between sea and sky. Somewhere to the east, beyond the curve of the Earth, a hole in the world was waiting for him.

"Navigation, what's our ETA to the staging area?"

"At best speed, sir — thirty-one knots sustained — we arrive in approximately forty-three hours."

"Make it so."

The Truman began to turn, her wake curling white against the grey sea, her escorts adjusting formation. Behind them, the exercise controller's voice came over the net one last time — a formal release, a handshake, a return to a world that no longer mattered.

Brennan watched the horizon and said nothing.

### Scene 2: [[France]] — The Division Sorts

**[TIME]: Day 8 — 2026-06-25, 06:00 Zulu**
**[POV]: Third Person — [[Étienne Roussel|Roussel]]*

The orders arrived at [[Étienne Roussel|Roussel]]'s quarters in Brest at six in the morning, delivered by a lieutenant from the naval préfecture who had been told to place the envelope in the captain's hands and no one else's.

Roussel was already awake. He had been awake since four, standing at the window of his flat on the Rue de Siam, watching the harbour lights fade into the grey dawn, the hulls of the Atlantic squadron taking shape as the night retreated. He had not known, at four, that the order was coming. But he had felt it — a restlessness that had pulled him from sleep and set him at the window, coffee cooling in his hand, waiting for something he could not name.

The lieutenant's knock was soft but definite. Roussel opened the door, took the envelope, and closed the door without a word. He recognised the seal — the Ministry of the Armed Forces, not the naval staff — and the weight of the paper told him the rest.

He opened it standing in his kitchen, the morning light grey over the harbour, the coffee cold on the counter. He read the order once. Then again. Then he set it down, drank the coffee in two swallows, and began to pack.

The division would consist of two vessels: his flagship, the Horizon-class air defence destroyer *[[Chevalier Paul (D621)|Chevalier Paul]]*, and the Aquitaine-class multi-mission frigate *[[Bretagne (D655)|Bretagne]]*. They were to detach from the Atlantic squadron, proceed independently to the international waters off the UK's western approaches, and place themselves under the operational coordination of the NATO task force command assembling around the portal.

The order was signed by the Minister of the Armed Forces personally — not a deputy, not a staff director, not a naval chief of staff. The Minister's signature, in blue ink, at the bottom of the second page. That told Roussel everything he needed to know about the political weight of this deployment. The Minister did not sign tasking orders for destroyer divisions. The Minister signed strategic directives. The distinction mattered.

He dressed in his uniform — the dark blue of the Marine Nationale, the twin gold stripes of a capitaine de vaisseau — and walked out of the flat without looking back. The car was waiting at the kerb, a lieutenant at the wheel, engine running. The drive to the naval base took twelve minutes through the waking streets of Brest. Roussel watched the city pass — the shuttered bakeries, the early commuters on the bridge over the Penfeld, the lights of the tramway depot, the statue of the three naval brothers on the Place de la Liberté. He had grown up here, in a flat overlooking the harbour, watching the ships of the Marine Nationale come and go. His father had served on the *Richelieu*, the great battleship of a vanished era, had sailed her from Dakar to New York for refit in 1943, had watched the French flag raised over a rebuilt navy after the war. His grandfather had served on the *Jean Bart*, had been sunk at Casablanca in 1942 by American dive-bombers, had floated in burning oil for six hours before a destroyer pulled him from the water. He had survived to tell the story, and he had told it every Christmas for the rest of his life, and Roussel had listened every time.

Three generations of naval officers. Three wars. Three different navies — the battleship navy of the Third Republic, the shattered fleet of Vichy, the modern force of the Fifth Republic. And now Roussel, commanding a destroyer division sailing toward a hole in the world.

He arrived at the quay at seven-fifteen. The *[[Chevalier Paul (D621)|Chevalier Paul]]* was already preparing for sea — the duty watch moving through the pre-underway checklist with the focused silence of a crew that had done this a hundred times and knew the stakes were different this time. The engineering team was spinning up the gas turbines, the exhaust shimmering in the cold morning air. The harbour tugs were manoeuvring into position alongside, their diesels rumbling in the grey dawn. On the signal deck, a quartermaster was hoisting the French ensign — the tricolour catching the first weak sunlight — and Roussel felt something move in his chest that he had not felt since his first command.

He climbed the gangway, returned the salute of the officer of the watch, and climbed to the bridge.

The *[[Bretagne (D655)|Bretagne]]* was visible across the basin, her own preparation underway. Roussel watched her for a moment — the clean lines of the Aquitaine design, the tricolour at her stern, the launch for her hull-mounted sonar already lowered for a last-minute systems check. He had served on the *[[Bretagne (D655)|Bretagne]]* as a lieutenant, years ago, before the war in the Sahel, before the rise of the Indo-Pacific pivot, before the world had discovered that the Atlantic Ocean contained something that made every previous strategic calculation obsolete. He remembered the wardroom of the *[[Bretagne (D655)|Bretagne]]* — the smell of coffee and diesel, the shared laughter of a junior officer division that had not yet learned to be afraid of the world. He had been afraid of nothing, then. He had been twenty-seven years old.

"Captain." His executive officer, a capitaine de frégate named Duval, stood at his side. Duval was forty-two, steady-eyed, a career navigator who had served on every class of frigate in the French fleet. He had the calm of a man who had weathered storms — literal and metaphorical — and had learned that panic was a luxury the bridge could not afford. "The harbour master is asking if we have an estimated departure time."

"One hour," Roussel said. "And signal the [[Bretagne (D655)|Bretagne]]: prepare for independent transit. We will not be waiting for a group rendezvous."

Duval hesitated. "Independent transit, sir? The operational plan calls for the division to remain concentrated."

"The operational plan was written by a staff officer in Paris who has not yet left his desk. We are two hundred and fifty nautical miles from the portal. The Americans are already underway. The British are assembling their carrier group. If we wait to concentrate, we arrive last. France does not arrive last." Roussel turned to look at his executive officer. "You understand what this deployment means."

"I understand the strategic logic, sir. France must be present at the portal as an independent naval power, not as an adjunct to the American fleet."

"Then you also understand that presence requires speed. We are not here to participate in an exercise. We are here to ensure that when decisions are made about that portal — military decisions, political decisions, decisions that will shape the future of Europe — France has a voice. And a voice requires a seat. And a seat requires hulls in the water." He looked out at the harbour — the grey water, the grey sky, the grey hulls of the French Navy preparing to sail into a grey future. "The sooner we are on station, the sooner that voice is heard."

Duval nodded and moved to the communications console to draft the signal.

Roussel stood at the bridge window, watching Brest slide past as the tugs eased the *[[Chevalier Paul (D621)|Chevalier Paul]]* away from the quay. The city was waking up — the first ferries crossing the harbour, streaking white wakes across the grey water, the morning traffic building on the bridge over the Penfeld, the lights of the naval school still burning in the dawn. He could see the roof of his flat from the harbour, a small grey shape among many, tucked into the hillside above the Rue de Siam. He did not know when he would see it again.

The *[[Chevalier Paul (D621)|Chevalier Paul]]* cleared the harbour breakwater at eight-twelve, her gas turbines spooling to transit speed, the bow lifting to the first Atlantic swell. Behind her, the *[[Bretagne (D655)|Bretagne]]* fell into formation at the prescribed interval — six hundred metres, standard cruising formation — and the two French warships set their course east-northeast toward a destination that existed on no chart.

Roussel stood on the bridge wing as the coastline of Brittany fell away astern — the Pointe du Petit Minou lighthouse, the headland at Camaret-sur-Mer, the dark spine of the Presqu'île de Crozon. He had sailed this coast a hundred times, in every season, in every weather. He knew its shapes and its moods. He knew the smell of the air when the Atlantic was building and the sound of the wind when it came from the northwest. He knew that the sea did not care about theologies or jurisdictions or the delicate architecture of international law.

He did not know what he would find at the portal. He knew only that France had given him an order, and he would carry it out with the same precision that three generations of naval officers before him had carried out theirs.

He remained on the bridge wing until the coast was a grey line on the horizon, and then he went inside to study the rules of engagement that did not yet exist.

### Scene 3: [[Northwood Headquarters]] — The Ugly Arithmetic

**[TIME]: Days 7–9 — 2026-06-24 to 2026-06-26**
**[POV]: Third Person — [[Michael Torres|Torres]]*

[[Michael Torres|Torres]] had been at Northwood for six hours when the first fuel query came in, and he knew, with the bone-deep certainty of a man who had spent sixteen years in naval logistics, that the number was not going to work.

He was a captain in the US Navy's supply corps — not a glamorous branch of the service, but a necessary one. The fleet did not move without people like him. The pilots who launched from the Truman's deck, the gunners who manned the destroyer's Close-In Weapon System (CIWS), the admirals who planned the operations — they all depended on the logistics chain, and the logistics chain depended on Torres. He was assigned to the Naval Supply Systems Command (NAVSUP) Europe, embedded at Northwood as the logistics liaison between US Naval Forces Europe and the Royal Navy. His office was a windowless room in the operational support wing, two desks bolted to the deck, a secure terminal that beeped every time a new message arrived, and a whiteboard that had been filled with numbers that kept changing every time he looked at them.

The query came from the *[[USS Gravely (DDG-107)|Gravely]]*. The Burke-class destroyer was sprinting east with the Truman, her engineering department working the numbers as they went. The message was terse — the kind of efficiency that came from a shipboard engineer who had too much to do and too little time to do it — but the content was urgent. *Fuel state: forty-two percent. Estimated arrival at station with eleven percent remaining at current speed. Request UNREP coordination.*

Forty-two percent. Torres had seen worse. He had seen a destroyer arrive in port with four percent remaining, her tanks practically dry, the crew running on fumes and prayer. But that had been a scheduled transit with a known destination and a pier waiting at the end. This was different. The *[[USS Gravely (DDG-107)|Gravely]]* was burning fuel at best speed to reach a staging area that was not a port, with no guarantee of resupply on arrival. The nearest USNS tanker — the *Leroy Grumman* — was four hundred miles south of the *[[USS Gravely (DDG-107)|Gravely]]*'s track, still loitering on a station that had been assigned before the portal crisis, waiting for tasking orders that no one at NAVSUP had thought to update.

Torres picked up the phone.

He started with NAVSUP Naples, the regional logistics hub for US naval forces in Europe. The officer who answered was a lieutenant commander he had worked with before — competent, overworked, the kind of officer who kept a detailed spreadsheet and updated it obsessively. Torres explained the situation: the *[[USS Gravely (DDG-107)|Gravely]]*, the fuel state, the need for a mid-transit UNREP. The lieutenant commander listened, typed, and came back with an answer.

*The* Grumman *is the closest asset. She's currently on a logistics support station for the Sixth Fleet exercise schedule. I don't have the authority to retask her.*

*Who does?*

A pause. *You'd need NAVEUR operations. Or the task force commander. Or someone in Washington. I don't know who wrote the tasking order for the* Grumman*, but it wasn't us.*

Torres thanked him, hung up, and called the *Leroy Grumman* directly.

The captain of the *Grumman* — a master in the Military Sealift Command, a civilian mariner with thirty years of experience and an accent that placed him somewhere in the Gulf Coast — answered on the second ring. Torres introduced himself and laid out the situation. He did not ask permission. He told the captain what was needed.

*So you want me to abandon my assigned station and steam north to meet a destroyer that's running on fumes.* The captain's voice was flat, unreadable.

*I want you to adjust your course to support a real-world operational requirement. The exercise station is not the priority. The portal is.*

A long pause. Torres could hear the *Grumman*'s engine room in the background — the low drone of the diesel plant, the distant clang of a hatch being secured. Then the captain said, *I'll adjust my course. You sort out the paperwork.*

*Thank you.*

*Don't thank me yet. My fuel state is not infinite either. I can give you one UNREP window, maybe two, before I need to pull back to Rota for replenishment. After that, your destroyers are on their own.*

Torres hung up. He looked at the whiteboard and updated the *[[USS Gravely (DDG-107)|Gravely]]*'s fuel projection. The numbers were still bad — the *Grumman*'s diversion would give the screen enough margin to reach station, but not enough to sustain operations beyond the first forty-eight hours. He added a note in the margin: *Tanker capacity — critical. Need to request additional USNS assets from the continental United States (CONUS).*

That was the first hour.

The second hour was the Royal Navy.

Torres called the Royal Fleet Auxiliary (RFA) command at Marchwood, the UK's naval logistics hub on the Southampton Water. The officer who answered was a British commander who sounded like he had not slept in days — his voice was hoarse, his sentences clipped, and he answered the phone with a curt *RFA command, state your business* that told Torres everything he needed to know about the mood at Marchwood.

He explained who he was and what he needed: coordination data on the UK Carrier Strike Group's (CSG) logistics posture, fuel states for the Queen Elizabeth and her screen, the availability of RFA tankers for potential joint US-UK replenishment operations.

The British commander laughed. It was not a happy laugh.

*You want the good news or the bad news?*

*Both.*

*Good news: the Queen Elizabeth is sailing with a full load of aviation fuel and sixty days of provisions. The Type 45s are in good material condition and their fuel states are adequate for transit. Bad news: the tankers that are supposed to support them are in the same condition as your USNS assets. We've activated* Tideforce *— she's fuelling now, but she won't be ready for sea for another thirty-six hours. We're recalling* Wave Knight *from a maintenance period that was supposed to last another two weeks. And we've got a third vessel —* Wave Ruler *— that's been in the extended readiness pool since 2023. It will take at least ten days to bring her crew back, run her trials, and get her to sea.*

Torres wrote the names on the whiteboard. *Tideforce*. *Wave Knight*. *Wave Ruler*. Three tankers, none of them ready, all of them needed.

*What's your worst-case timeline for the full CSG logistics chain?*

*If everything goes perfectly — the* Tideforce *gets to sea in two days, the* Wave Knight *finishes her workup in five, and the* Wave Ruler *is not needed because we don't stay on station longer than a week — then we can sustain the CSG for approximately fourteen days before we need to start rotating ships back to port for fuel.* A pause. *If things go badly — if the operations require extended station time, if we lose a tanker to a mechanical casualty, if the Admiralty decides to double the number of escorts — then we can sustain the CSG for maybe seven days, and after that we're cannibalising fuel from one ship to keep another on station.*

Torres updated the whiteboard. He started a column for the UK CSG — fuel states, logistics status, days on station — and the numbers were worse than the US column. The UK had the will, the ships, the crew. But the tankers were coming out of reserve, and the supply chain was being invented as they went.

The next three hours were a blur of additional calls. Torres spoke to the Dutch liaison about the frigate HNLMS *[[HNLMS Tromp (F803)|Tromp]]* that was being detached from a NATO standing maritime group. He spoke to a Spanish captain whose patrol vessel *Infanta Cristina* was diverting from a fisheries patrol in the Bay of Biscay. The second day was the ammunition question.

Torres had not thought about ammunition. He was a logistics officer, not a combat planner — his job was fuel, food, spare parts, the mundane architecture of naval operations. But the second day brought a query from the Truman's supply officer that made him realise the scope of the gap.

Torres stared at the message. The Truman's air wing was carrying inert training rounds — practice bombs, exercise missiles, dummy torpedoes. If the Truman needed to launch a strike, if the fleet needed to defend itself, if anything came through the portal that required a kinetic response, the air wing would have to rearm. He did not know the answer. He did not know anyone who knew the answer. He wrote the question in the ROE column, because it belonged there — the legal framework and the operational capability were the same problem, and neither had been solved.

The third day, he started a new column on the whiteboard. He wrote *ROE* at the top in black marker, and beneath it he wrote:

He stared at the words. They were true, and they were useless. The fleet was assembling, the ships were arriving on station, and no one had told them what they were allowed to do when they got there.

Torres asked the question in three different channels that day: to the NAVEUR operations officer, a Navy captain who had been at his desk for seventy-two hours straight; to the UK liaison officer, a Royal Navy commander who had the hollow eyes of a man who had been in meetings since the portal opened; to the Judge Advocate General (JAG) officer assigned to the Northwood staff, a US Navy lieutenant commander who had spent her career on maritime law and had never encountered a question that began with *What if the opposing force is from a parallel dimension?*

No one was working on it. No one knew where to start. The lawyers were waiting for political guidance. The politicians were waiting for military advice. The ROE column on the whiteboard remained blank.

That night, at two in the morning, Torres walked to the Northwood cafeteria — a fluorescent-lit room with vending machines and a coffee dispenser that had been cleaned sometime in the previous administration. He was the only person there. He thought about calling his wife. It was eight in the evening in Norfolk, Virginia, and his daughter would still be awake. He did not make the call.

He finished the coffee, returned to his windowless office, and updated the whiteboard. The numbers were worse than they had been the previous day. He picked up the phone and began another round of calls.

The phone rang. Another query. He answered it.

### Scene 4: *[[HMS Resolute]]* — The Watcher

**[TIME]: Days 7–10 — 1914-09-20 to 1914-09-23 TC**
**[POV]: Third Person — [[Alistair Pembroke|Pembroke]]*

The first day, [[Alistair Pembroke|Pembroke]] saw only the usual picket vessels — the smaller modern ships that had been on station since the first contact. He had learned to recognise them in the days since [[Father Beauchamp|Beauchamp]]'s delegation had crossed through [[The Portal Threshold|the shimmer]]: the lean grey destroyers with their angular superstructures, the patrol vessels that moved in predictable arcs, the occasional aircraft that appeared from nowhere and vanished the same way. He noted them in the log, compared the count to the previous day's, and thought nothing of it. The modern world was maintaining its cordon. That was expected.

The second day, the silhouettes changed.

Pembroke stood on the wing of the *[[HMS Resolute|Resolute]]*'s bridge, his hands braced on the teak rail, his eyes fixed on the shimmer. The morning light was weak — a grey Atlantic dawn on both sides of the portal — and the distortion of the threshold made distant shapes waver like heat haze on a summer road. But Pembroke had spent forty years reading the horizon, and he knew what he was seeing.

The picket screen was still there, the familiar grey destroyers holding their positions. But behind them, coming over the eastern horizon, was something new.

A deck. Flat and wide, stretching the length of a city block, with a tower offset to one side like a castle keep built at the edge of a plateau. The vessel that carried it was immense — larger than any ship Pembroke had ever seen, larger than the Moving Fortress of Britannia, larger than the *[[HMS Resolute|Resolute]]* and her entire squadron combined. It carried no guns that Pembroke could see, no turrets, no casemates, no visible armament of any kind. But it radiated authority. It moved with the slow, deliberate purpose of something that did not need to prove its power because its power was self-evident in its existence.

Pembroke watched it for a long time. He did not speak. His first lieutenant, standing at his side, also did not speak. The bridge crew worked in silence, the only sounds the creak of the ship's motion and the low hum of the forced-draft fans that fed the *[[HMS Resolute|Resolute]]*'s boilers.

Finally, Pembroke turned to his signals officer. "Signal the lookouts. I want a hourly report on the disposition of the new arrivals. Count them. Note their positions. Note any change in their formation."

"Aye, Captain."

"And send word to the picket line: maintain readiness. No provocations. But no complacency either."

The signals officer saluted and moved to the lamp.

The *[[HMS Resolute|Resolute]]*'s picket line held its position. The new vessel — Pembroke did not know its name, its nation, its purpose — continued its approach, slowed, and took station at the edge of the modern cordon. Behind it, smaller ships were arriving, fanning out into a screen formation that Pembroke recognised even if the hull shapes were unfamiliar. The modern navy was deploying a defensive perimeter around its capital ship. Standard doctrine. Pembroke had done the same thing a thousand times.

The third day brought the second vessel.

Pembroke was in his cabin when the lookout reported it — a sharp rap on the door, a seaman's voice: *Captain, new contact on the other side. Different from the first.* He climbed to the bridge in time to see it sliding through the shimmer, its profile resolving as it cleared the distortion.

Different configuration. Two towers instead of one, positioned at opposite ends of the flat deck like the horns of a bull. A different arrangement of masts and sensors. Pembroke studied it through his glass, noting the differences, cataloguing them in the part of his mind that had spent decades assessing enemy formations. This vessel was slightly smaller than the first, but its lines were purposeful, aggressive. It moved with the same authority, taking station to the north of the first capital ship, and a screen of smaller warships moved with it — lean vessels with angular superstructures and the unmistakable shapes of missile launchers.

Pembroke lowered his glass.

"Signal the *Herald*," he said. "Tell Doctor-Abbot [[Edmund Lassiter|Lassiter]] that the modern fleet is reinforcing. I want his assessment of the new vessel's capabilities — speed, range, any observations his people have made from the other side."

The signals officer hesitated. "The *Herald* is still anchored on our side, Captain. The delegation has not yet returned."

"Then send a boat. I want Lassiter's eyes on this."

"Aye, Captain."

Pembroke remained on the bridge, watching the second vessel settle into position. He summoned his signals officer again, keeping his voice level. "Are the watchers reporting any movement from the Heretic picket lines?"

"I checked with the forward observation post an hour ago, Captain. The enemy positions are unchanged. No movement. No signals. No activity."

"No activity at all?"

"None, Captain. The enemy is quiet."

Pembroke turned to look south, toward the distant haze where the Heretic picket line lay — a formation of black-hulled vessels that had shadowed the Faithful fleet for weeks, never engaging, never withdrawing, always present at the edge of the horizon like a bad memory. They had been there since before the portal opened, maintaining the eternal vigilance that defined the war. They had not moved. They had not fired. They had not responded to any signal.

"Too quiet," Pembroke said. "Maintain observation. I want a report every two hours on the Heretic line. If so much as a flag changes position, I want to know."

"Aye, Captain."

The fourth day, the French vessels arrived.

Pembroke was on the bridge when the lookout called them out — two new contacts emerging from the distortion, their profiles distinct from the British and American ships. Longer, sleeker, with a sharper bow and a different arrangement of masts and sensors. They slid into the cordon as if they had been expected, as if the fleet had been waiting for them, and took station to the south of the American formation.

A third nation. A third flag. A third voice in a conversation that Pembroke could not hear but could see taking shape in the disposition of hulls on the water.

He thought of the Iron Sultanate. Of the courier vessel that had been despatched southward days ago, its mission unknown, its destination the Southern Reaches. He thought of the cable from the Office of the Propagation of Virtue — *verify before you venerate* — and the inquisitor who was even now making his way from New Antioch to the portal. He thought of King Robert the Longsword, and the Synod, and the divided counsels of a civilisation that had fought Hell for eight centuries and was only now beginning to understand the true shape of the world beyond its war.

The modern world was assembling a coalition. The Faithful world was fracturing.

He did not say this aloud. But he thought it.

The fourth night, Pembroke stood the midwatch alone.

He had dismissed his first lieutenant at midnight, sent the junior officer of the watch below at one, and taken the bridge himself at two. The *[[HMS Resolute|Resolute]]* was steady on station, her engines at idle, her picket line dark and silent. The wind was cold and salt-laden, coming from the northwest, carrying the smell of open ocean and the distant hint of something else — something that might have been the portal itself, an ozone tang that clung to the back of the throat.

The bridge wing was dark. The only light came from the dimmed compass binnacle and the faint glow of the *[[HMS Resolute|Resolute]]*'s signal lanterns, their glass painted blue to reduce visibility. Above the bridge, the thirty-foot crucifix caught the quarter moon — a dark shape against a darker sky, the silver thread of the prayers of warding catching the light like a spider's web.

Through the portal, the modern fleet glowed.

Pembroke could see them clearly in the darkness — the running lights of the destroyer screen, the floodlit decks of the two capital ships, the occasional sweep of a radar array that must have been visible for miles in every direction. There were so many of them. More than he had counted during the day. More than he had expected. Ships of every size and shape, from the immense carriers to the smallest patrol vessels, all of them holding station in a cordon that encircled the gateway between worlds. They were waiting. They were watching.

He had spent forty years at sea. He had fought the Heretic fleet in the Bay of Biscay, in the Norwegian Sea, in the approaches to the Mediterranean. He had commanded the *[[HMS Resolute|Resolute]]* through the Battle of the Western Approaches in 1903, when the Heretic battleship *Crown of Thorns* had broken through the picket line and he had rammed her with the *[[HMS Resolute|Resolute]]*'s reinforced bow, grinding steel against steel until the enemy's crew had been driven back by the boarding party with crucifix and blade. He had seen the Moving Fortress of Britannia deploy its full battery — thirty-two twelve-inch guns firing in sequence, the shockwave rolling across the water like the hand of God. He had watched the Sacred Knights of the Order ride their drakes into the mouth of Hell on the Plains of Cordoba, their lances tipped with consecrated silver, their war cries lost in the roar of infernal artillery.

He had never seen anything like this.

The scale of it. The sheer industrial weight of a world that had never known war — not real war, not the kind that had shaped Pembroke's civilisation, not the endless grinding attrition of a conflict that had lasted longer than any kingdom on Earth — and had nevertheless produced vessels that dwarfed anything the Faithful Crown could put to sea. He tried to imagine the docks that had built those ships, the foundries that had forged their steel, the training schools that had produced their crews. He could not. His imagination failed at the scale.

*They are the ark*, [[Father Beauchamp|Beauchamp]] had said. *We are the cargo. Our world is their vessel, preserved through the Flood, and they are the survivors who will carry us into a new creation.*

Pembroke had accepted that theology. He had participated in the Rite of Discernment. He had given his approval to the delegation, to the embassy, to the cautious and hopeful engagement that had marked the first days of contact. He had believed — he still believed — that the people on the other side were not demons, not heretics, not enemies. They were something new. Something unprecedented. Something that God had placed in the path of the Faithful for a purpose that no one had yet understood.

But standing on the bridge wing, watching the lights of a fleet that could have conquered his entire world in a single afternoon, he wondered if Beauchamp had been wrong.

Not about the theology. About the hope.

He had hoped that contact with the modern world would be a meeting of equals — two civilisations, each with its own strengths and weaknesses, each with something to offer the other. He had imagined a partnership: Faithful steel and modern science, Faithful faith and modern reason, two worlds joined by a common humanity and a common purpose.

The fleet on the other side had shattered that hope. Not deliberately. Not through any act of aggression. Simply by existing. By being what it was. The modern world did not need the Faithful Crown's protection. It did not need their alliances, their treaties, their ancient wisdom. It had nuclear-powered ships that could circle the globe without refuelling, aircraft that could strike from beyond the horizon, radar that could see everything the Faithful fleet did before the Faithful fleet knew it was being watched.

The ark did not need the cargo. The cargo was a passenger.

He wrote the thought in his private log, in the small leather book he kept in his cabin, the one that no one else was permitted to read. He wrote slowly, by the light of a shielded lantern, the words forming in the careful hand of a man who had been taught to write in a cathedral school and had never lost the discipline.

*Day 10. The fleet on the other side is complete. I count two capital ships of unprecedented size, screened by at least ten smaller warships. Three nations have deployed vessels to the cordon — the modern English, the modern Americans, and a third nation whose flag I do not recognise but whose ships are unmistakably naval. They have not attempted to cross the threshold. They have not communicated with our picket. They have simply arrived and waited.*

*The Heretic lines remain silent. I do not understand this. The enemy has not moved since the portal opened. They have not probed our defences. They have not attempted to intercept the delegation. They have not responded to any of the signals I have ordered sent. On any other front of this war, such silence would be a prelude to attack. Here, I do not know what it means. Perhaps they are waiting. Perhaps they are watching. Perhaps they have seen the fleet on the other side and are calculating, as I am, what it means for the balance of a war that has lasted eight centuries.*

*I do not know what the modern world intends — war, peace, observation, intervention. I know only that they are there, and that we are here, and that the space between us is narrower than it was a week ago. The Heretic lines are silent. That silence is the worst omen of all.*

He closed the book, extinguished the lamp, and returned to the bridge. The watch was not over. The fleet was still there. The Heretic lines were still silent. The night stretched ahead of him, cold and patient, and the world on the other side of the portal glowed with lights that did not belong to his world's sky.

He took his position on the bridge wing and watched until dawn.

### Scene 5: The Fleet Gathers

**[TIME]: Day 10 — 2026-06-27, 20:00 Zulu**
**[POV]: Third Person — Omniscient**

The sun set over the Eastern Atlantic in layers of orange and grey, the low cloud deck catching the last light and holding it like a bruice that had been forming all day. The wind was from the northwest at fifteen knots, raising a moderate sea — four-foot swells running in long, patient lines from the direction of Greenland. The air temperature was eleven degrees Celsius. The visibility was twelve nautical miles, decreasing to eight in the patches of mist that clung to the surface like breath on a cold morning.

The fleet was complete.

At the centre of the cordon, the *[[USS Harry S. Truman (CVN-75)|USS Harry S. Truman]]* lay at station, her angular silhouette dominating the seascape. She had arrived at fourteen hundred hours, her transit completed in forty-one hours — two hours ahead of the original estimate, the Chief Engineer having coaxed an extra knot from the nuclear plant by tweaking the reactor coolant flow in a way that was technically against procedure and practically invisible to the shore-based monitoring systems. Brennan had approved the adjustment without comment. He understood that the margin between on time and early was the margin that mattered.

Her escorts — the *[[USS Gravely (DDG-107)|Gravely]]*, the *[[USS Stout (DDG-55)|Stout]]*, the *[[USS Bainbridge (DDG-96)|Bainbridge]]* — held position in a standard anti-submarine screen, their Aegis arrays sweeping the sky, the sea, the boundary between the known and the unknown. The *Grumman*'s captain had executed the diversion with professional efficiency, arriving at the rendezvous point six minutes ahead of schedule, and the UNREP had proceeded without incident. The *Grumman* would remain on station for another twelve hours, topping off the *[[USS Stout (DDG-55)|Stout]]* and the *[[USS Gravely (DDG-107)|Gravely]]*, before withdrawing toward Rota for her own replenishment. The escorts would arrive on station with fuel states in the high twenties — marginal, but survivable. Torres had made that happen.

The Hawkeye's rotodome was already spinning, its sensors warming through their startup sequence, the aircrew running through their pre-launch checklist in the cockpit. When it launched, it would climb to twenty-five thousand feet and begin a race-track orbit that would keep it on station for six hours before handing off to its sister aircraft, which was already being readied in the hangar bay. The fleet would not lose sight of the portal again.

She had sailed from Bergen on forty-eight hours' notice, her crew mustered from leave and shore duty, and she had made the transit in weather that had tested her small hull's limits. There were others. A German reconnaissance aircraft had overflown the fleet twice during the afternoon, its electronics surveillance suite recording every radar emission, every radio frequency, every data link. Germany could not send a warship without a Bundestag vote, but Germany could send an Orion, and the Orion's sensors were as sharp as any destroyer's. The aircraft's flight path was a statement in itself: Germany was watching. Germany was collecting data. Germany would be ready when the constitutional machinery finally turned.

A Royal Air Force (RAF) Poseidon broke cloud cover to the east, its turbofans audible across the water as it banked over the fleet and began a long patrol arc to the south. Its mission was anti-submarine warfare — the eternal Atlantic mission, the hunt for the hidden threat. But the threat it was hunting was not a Russian submarine or a Chinese spy vessel. It was the unknown. It was the silence that Pembroke had noted on the other side. It was the possibility that something was moving beneath the surface that no one had seen.

On the Truman's flight deck, the E-2D Hawkeye launched with a roar that echoed across the water, its turboprops clawing at the air as it climbed into the grey evening sky. The aircraft banked to the east, its rotodome a dark disc against the clouds, and settled into its orbit at the edge of the fleet's radar horizon.

The portal shimmered at the centre of the cordon.

It was flat. It was dark. It hung in the air fifty feet above the water, a vertical slit in the fabric of reality that reflected no light, emitted no sound, revealed nothing of what lay beyond. The distortion around its edges was barely visible in the fading light — a faint wobbling of the air, like heat haze over a summer road, but cold. The cold was the strangest part. Those who had approached the portal reported a drop in temperature as they drew near, a chill that had nothing to do with the Atlantic wind. The water beneath the portal was several degrees colder than the surrounding sea. No one had an explanation.

The portal did not acknowledge the fleet. It did not react to the presence of the ships, the aircraft, the thousands of human beings who had gathered to watch it. It simply hung there, a wound in the world, waiting.

Save for the silhouette.

Through the distortion, dim and wavering, the outline of *[[HMS Resolute|HMS Resolute]]* was visible — her thirty-foot crucifix catching the last light of a different sun, the silver thread of prayers catching the light like a signal fire. Her twin twelve-inch turrets were trained off-axis, a gesture of strained neutrality that Brennan had noted in the briefings and that Pembroke had ordered as a signal of non-aggression. Her signal lanterns were burning in the twilight of a world that had been at war with Hell for eight centuries, and through the distortion they looked like stars in a different sky.

She was watching.

The fleet was watching back.

On the bridge of the *[[HMS Resolute|Resolute]]*, Captain [[Alistair Pembroke]] stood in the darkness, his hands braced on the teak rail, his eyes fixed on the lights of the modern fleet. He had not moved in an hour. His first lieutenant had offered him coffee, food, a blanket against the cold. He had refused all of them. He was watching. That was what mattered.

On the bridge of the *[[USS Harry S. Truman (CVN-75)|USS Harry S. Truman]]*, Captain [[Daniel Brennan]] stood at the forward window, a mug of coffee in his hand that he had not tasted in twenty minutes. He had studied the satellite imagery of the *[[HMS Resolute|Resolute]]*. He had read the intelligence reports on the Faithful Crown's naval capabilities. He knew that the silhouette on the other side of the portal was a coal-fired battleship with twelve-inch guns and armour that would not stop a single Harpoon missile. He knew that he commanded a force that could destroy Pembroke's entire civilisation's navy in a single afternoon.

And yet.

He did not give the order to clear the flight deck for launch. He did not increase the readiness state of the escort screen. He stood at the window, looking at the silhouette on the other side of the portal, and he knew — with a certainty that had nothing to do with intelligence assessments or Rules of Engagement — that he was looking at the face of a captain who had the same fears, the same responsibilities, the same weight of command.

The space between them was measured in nautical miles and years of history, in theologies that could not be reconciled and fears that could not be spoken. The portals did not measure that distance. They only marked it.

The gathering was complete.

The next move belonged to the other side.
