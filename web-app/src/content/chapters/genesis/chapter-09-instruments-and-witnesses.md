# Chapter 09 – Instruments and Witnesses

## Summary

[[Stephen Davenport|Davenport]], alone through the night watches aboard *[[USS Gerald R. Ford (CVN-78)|USS Gerald R. Ford]]*, discovers the words for an honest reply — a letter that refuses the Providence framing without rejecting the man who offered it, and that pivots from theology to friendship without pretending the pivot is costless. The letter is transmitted to the Faithful cordon, where [[Alistair Pembroke|Pembroke]], [[Father Beauchamp|Beauchamp]], and the Faithful command wrestle with a document that gives them the alliance they hoped for on terms they never imagined. Before a unified response can form, word arrives: [[Bishop Aldric]] is returning from the modern world aboard the *[[USS Stout (DDG-55)|USS Stout]]*, which has crossed the portal with permission for a scheduled transfer to *[[HMS Resolute]]*. But *[[USS Stout (DDG-55)|Stout]]*'s SIGINT suite has detected something else — a hidden Heretic submarine, silent beneath the thermocline, systematically intercepting and relaying every transmission across the portal cordon. Ordered to force it to surface, *[[USS Stout (DDG-55)|Stout]]* moves at flank speed. The submarine uses *[[HMS Resolute|Resolute]]* as cover. In the confusion of the close-quarters chase, *[[USS Stout (DDG-55)|Stout]]*'s bow tears into *[[HMS Resolute|Resolute]]*'s port side — a catastrophic collision that shatters the fragile trust between the fleets even as it reveals the enemy that has been listening to everything. *[[USS Stout (DDG-55)|Stout]]* recovers, pursues, and destroys the Heretic submarine with a single torpedo. [[Bishop Aldric|Aldric]], watching from the open deck, sees his world's ancient enemy annihilated in seconds by a machine that minutes before crippled his world's flagship. He delivers to the Faithful command a theology forged in the silence of modern London and hardened in the fire of the collision: the moderns are not an ark and not a test. They are a people God has hidden Himself from — and the Faithful must learn to *wait* before they learn to judge.

## Scenes

### Scene 1: *[[USS Gerald R. Ford (CVN-78)|USS Gerald R. Ford]]* — The Flag Cabin

**[TIME]: Day 14 — 2026-07-01, 02:00 Zulu / 1914-09-27, 14:00 TC**

**[POV]: Third Person — [[Stephen Davenport|Davenport]]*

The cabin had not changed in six hours. The steel desk, the porthole, the photograph of [[Terrence Kohler|Kohler]]'s family, the hum of the reactor through the deck plates, the portal on the dark horizon — all of it had held steady while [[Stephen Davenport|Davenport]] had not moved from the chair, had not set down the pen, had not taken his eyes from the half-filled sheet of paper that had become, over the course of the longest night of his premiership, the most important letter he would ever write.

The first draft he had discarded. The second draft he had discarded. The third draft, the fourth, the drafts that had tried to split the difference between honesty and diplomacy, between secular refusal and strategic necessity — all of them had failed, and he had watched them fail in real time, the words curdling on the page, the careful diplomatic formulations revealing themselves as evasions, and evasions revealing themselves as the kind of cowardice that [[King Robert the Longsword|King Robert]] — who had stood on the bridge of a dreadnought and wept at the thought of a world that had never known war — would recognise and despise.

The fifth draft was different. The fifth draft had arrived, sometime after midnight, not as a solution but as a surrender — a surrender of the attempt to be both honest and strategic, a surrender of the hope that the letter could accomplish both of the things he needed it to accomplish. It could not. He had understood that, finally, in the small hours of the morning, when the carrier's ventilation system had cycled the air to a temperature just cold enough to keep him awake and the portal had flickered on the horizon like a signal fire from another world. The letter could not be honest and diplomatic. But it could be honest and *kind*. It could refuse the framing without refusing the friendship. It could say *I cannot be what you need me to be* and also *I will be what I can* — and it could leave the question of Providence exactly where it belonged: not in the realm of argument, but in the realm of mystery, where neither king nor prime minister had any more authority than the other.

He uncapped the pen. He wrote the final line. He set the pen down and read the whole thing through, from the salutation to the signature, and he did not change a word.

The letter lay on the steel desk before him, five pages of A4 in his own cramped hand, the ink dark and uneven where his hand had tired and steadied and tired again through the night watches. Through the porthole, the first grey light of dawn was beginning to touch the portal's edge — the silver scar turning pale, the dark sea lightening beneath it, the whole impossible structure of the dimensional threshold taking on the colours of morning. He had been writing for six hours. He had not noticed the time pass.

He read the letter one final time.

---

*To His Majesty King Robert, by the Grace of God and the Victory of the Cross Defender of the Faithful and Lord of the White Cliffs — from Stephen Davenport, Prime Minister of the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Northern Ireland: greeting.*

*Your Majesty —*

*I have read your letter three times, and I have sat with it through a night that has shown me, more clearly than any briefing or report, the weight of what you are asking and the courage of what you are offering. You wrote to me as one sovereign to another, in the spirit of honesty, and I will answer you in the same spirit — not because it is diplomatic, but because anything less would be an insult to the trust you have placed in a stranger from another world.*

*You have called my world "an instrument of Providence" — preserved by God beyond the reach of the Adversary, delivered at the hour of your people's greatest need. You have called us the answered prayer of a people who have been praying for deliverance for eight centuries without knowing what deliverance would look like. I understand why you believe this. I understand why your people will see it this way — why they must see it this way, because the alternative, the thought that our arrival is mere chance, is too cruel to bear after eight centuries of war. And I will not tell you that you are wrong.*

*Not because I believe you are right. But because I do not know.*

*I am not a theologian, Your Majesty. I am not a prophet. I am the Prime Minister of a secular democracy — an office created by law, accountable to an elected Parliament, answerable to a sovereign people. My government does not pray before it deliberates. My country has spent the past three centuries systematically removing God from public life — not out of hostility, but out of the hard-won conviction that faith, to be genuine, must be chosen freely, and that a government that speaks for God is a government that is no longer accountable to the people who put it there. I cannot claim to speak for Providence. I have no authority to do so. I would not wish to have it.*

*But I do not need to believe that God sent us here to know that we have an obligation to be here. Whatever force opened the portal — whether chance, or physics, or the hand of a God I do not claim to know — it has placed two worlds beside each other. One of those worlds is fighting for its survival against an enemy that the other has never had to face. That is not a theological fact. It is a moral fact. The Crown of England has fought the Adversary for eight centuries — alone, without hope of relief, without knowledge that any other world existed. You fought without knowing we were watching. Now we are here. And we have — whatever name we give to the thing that brought us — the power to help.*

*I cannot be the instrument of Providence. I have no authority to claim that title and no desire to wear it. But I can be your friend. I can be your ally. I can promise — and I do promise — that the United Kingdom will not abandon your world to the war you have been fighting alone. Not because we share your theology. Because we share your humanity. Because the enemy you face is an enemy to everything our world believes about the dignity and worth of every human soul. Because the suffering your people have endured is not a necessary cost of some divine plan — it is a tragedy, pure and simple, and the fact that we have the power to help alleviate it creates an obligation to do so that no theological framework can erase and no secular framework can avoid.*

*I am offering you, Your Majesty, an alliance that does not require us to agree about the name of the thing that brought us together. We will share our knowledge: our medicine, our engineering, our methods of fighting a war that your world has been fighting with prayer and pike and the blessings of the saints. We will bring the weight of our fleet to the defense of your cordon — not as conquerors, not as missionaries, but as partners who have no interest in ruling a world we did not know existed two weeks ago and every interest in seeing that world survive. We will open our ports to your ships, our hospitals to your wounded, our universities to your scholars. We will ask in return only what any ally asks: honesty, good faith, and the recognition that the portal binds us together whether we wish it or not, and that the only question now is what we build on that binding.*

*I do not know if God preserved my world. I do not know if God delivered us at the hour of your greatest need. I do not know if there is a God, or if the portal is a miracle or an accident, or if any of the words we use to describe what is happening are adequate to the reality of it. I know only that we are here — your world and mine, joined across a wound in the fabric of reality — and that we have, both of us, a choice to make about what we do with the time we have been given.*

*I choose friendship. I hope, when you have read this letter and consulted your conscience and your counsellors, that you will tell me you choose it too — even if the friendship I am offering does not wear the name you hoped it would wear.*

*God be with you, Your Majesty — or, if you prefer the voice of a man who cannot claim to know whether God exists, then: good fortune, good counsel, and the friendship of a world that is still learning what it means to be asked to stand beside another.*

*Given under my hand aboard the USS Gerald R. Ford, at the portal threshold, on the first day of July in the Year of Our Lord 2026, being the fourteenth day since the opening of the portal and the first day on which I have allowed myself to believe that the task before us is impossible — and worth attempting regardless.*

*Stephen Davenport*

---

He folded the pages carefully — five sheets of A4, the paper of his own world's bureaucracy, thin and white and unremarkable. He did not have a seal. He had a signature, which was the same thing in different clothing, and he had the conviction — strange and unwelcome and not entirely comfortable — that the letter, whatever its flaws, was honest. Not diplomatic. Not strategic. Not clever. But honest. And honesty, he had discovered in the course of the long night, was the only thing he could offer a king who had wept at the thought of a world that had never known war.

He pressed the intercom. Five minutes later, a young lieutenant from the communications division — a woman he had never seen before and would probably never see again, whose name he would not remember and whose role in the history of two worlds would never be acknowledged — took the folded pages from his hand and promised to transmit them to the Faithful cordon on the most secure channel the fleet could manage.

[[Stephen Davenport|Davenport]] watched her go. Then he turned back to the porthole, where the portal was catching the first full light of dawn — silver and gold and the cold grey-blue of the Atlantic — and he thought about the words he had written and the words he had not written and the question he had left unanswered because he did not know the answer: *what if the Faithful king was right, and wrong only about the name of the thing that brought them here?*

He did not know. He was not certain he would ever know. But he had written the letter, and the letter was honest, and the honesty was the only instrument he was prepared to offer — to a king, to a world, to a God he did not believe in but could no longer entirely dismiss.

The carrier hummed. The portal shimmered. The pen lay uncapped on the desk, its work finished, its question not quite answered — but asked, at last, in words that could be sent.

### Scene 2: *[[HMS Resolute]]* — The Wardroom

**[TIME]: Day 14 — 1914-09-27, 16:00 TC / 2026-07-01, 04:00 Zulu**

**[POV]: Third Person — [[Father Beauchamp|Beauchamp]]*

The wardroom of *[[HMS Resolute]]* was wood-panelled and low-ceilinged and smelled of coal smoke and beeswax and the faint, permanent brine of the Atlantic. A long table of dark oak ran its length — scarred by decades of charts and cutlery and the occasional sword laid upon it during councils of war — and at its head, beneath the stern windows that looked out upon the Faithful cordon, [[Alistair Pembroke|Captain Pembroke]] sat with the transcribed letter in his hands.

The transcription had come from the wireless room twenty minutes ago — the modern fleet's transmission received by the *[[HMS Resolute|Resolute]]*'s spark-gap set, the dots and dashes decoded by a young signals officer who had spent the past two weeks learning to recognise the moderns' faster, cleaner pulse patterns. The officer had written the Prime Minister's words in his own hand, on Faithful paper, in Faithful ink — and the result was a document that was simultaneously modern and medieval, a secular letter transcribed by a boy who had never heard of secularism and would not have understood it if he had.

[[Father Beauchamp|Beauchamp]] had read the transcription twice. [[Alistair Pembroke|Pembroke]] had read it three times. Two other Faithful captains — Morgan of the HMS *Herald*, Ashby of the torpedo-boat destroyer squadron — had read it once and were now waiting, their expressions unreadable, for their commander to speak.

"He refuses the Providence," [[Alistair Pembroke|Pembroke]] said. His voice was quiet — the voice of a man who was working through a problem that had no obvious solution. "He does not reject it. He does not mock it. He does not argue against it. But he refuses it. He will not wear the name the King gave him."

"He cannot," [[Father Beauchamp|Beauchamp]] said. The words came before he had fully formed the thought behind them — the habit of a chaplain who had learned to answer before his captain asked. "He is telling us, as honestly as he can, that his world is not what we hoped it was. It is not an ark. It is not a divine instrument. It is a secular democracy — a government that has spent three centuries removing God from public life. He cannot claim to speak for Providence because his office forbids it. And he will not claim to speak for Providence because his conscience forbids it."

"And yet he offers alliance," [[Alistair Pembroke|Pembroke]] said. "Full alliance. Medicine, engineering, the fleet. His words are refusal, but his offer is —" He paused, searching for the word. "His offer is everything we asked for. Minus the name."

"That is the problem," said Morgan — a lean, grey-haired captain whose face was permanently set in the expression of a man who had seen too much and trusted too little. "He gives us the alliance. He refuses the reason for it. The King's letter framed the alliance as Providence — as a gift from God, as the answered prayer of eight centuries. The Prime Minister's reply reframes it as — what? Friendship? Moral obligation? A secular commitment that asks nothing in return but good faith. It is generous. It is also —" He stopped.

"Empty," said Ashby, who was younger and less careful with his words. "It is empty of God. It gives us ships and medicine and engineering. It does not give us a reason to believe that any of it matters — that any of it is more than the charity of a power that could destroy us in a single afternoon and is choosing, for reasons we cannot verify, not to."

[[Father Beauchamp|Beauchamp]] looked at the transcribed letter — at the cramped handwriting of the young signals officer, at the words that had been sent across the portal in pulses of light, at the offer that was everything the Faithful Crown had asked for and nothing they had hoped for. He understood Ashby's suspicion. He shared it, in a way — or rather, he had shared it, until he had spent enough time with the modern world to understand that the absence of God was not the same thing as the absence of good.

"It is not empty," he said. "The Prime Minister — [[Stephen Davenport|Davenport]] — has made a choice. He could have accepted the Providence framing. He could have told us what we wanted to hear — that God sent his world, that the portal is a miracle, that the alliance is blessed — and we would have believed him. He could have lied. He chose not to. He chose to tell us the truth — that he does not know what brought his world to ours, that he cannot speak for God, that he will not pretend to a faith he does not hold. And then — having told us the truth, having risked our rejection by refusing to give us what we wanted to hear — he offered us the alliance anyway. Not because God demanded it. Because he believes it is right."

He paused. The wardroom was silent. The coal smoke curled in the ventilation, the beeswax shone on the dark wood, the Atlantic pressed against the hull — the same hull that had carried the Resolute through eight centuries of war, that had met the Heretic fleet in battle and blessed the Faithful dead and carried the cross that was welded to the bridge. The hull that was, at this moment, holding station beside a portal to a world that had never known war and was offering — for reasons it could not fully explain — to help.

"That is not emptiness," [[Father Beauchamp|Beauchamp]] said. "That is honour. A different kind of honour than the honour we know. But honour nonetheless."

[[Alistair Pembroke|Pembroke]] set the transcription down on the dark oak table. The light from the stern windows — grey, northern, the light of a North Atlantic afternoon in late September — fell across his face, and [[Father Beauchamp|Beauchamp]] saw, for the first time in two weeks, the exhaustion that the captain had been hiding from his crew.

"I do not know how to answer this," [[Alistair Pembroke|Pembroke]] said. "The King will need a recommendation. The Synod will need a theological assessment. The fleet will need orders. And I am looking at a letter that gives us everything we need and nothing we can use — because the framing is wrong, and the framing is what the King's decree depends upon, and the King's decree is what the Faithful people have been told to believe." He looked at [[Father Beauchamp|Beauchamp]]. "You formulated the ark theology. Does this letter break it?"

[[Father Beauchamp|Beauchamp]] had been asking himself the same question. The ark theology — the careful, bold, terrifying argument that the modern world had been preserved by God as a vessel, a refuge, a remnant sealed against the flood — had been the theological foundation of everything the Faithful command had done since the portal opened. It had justified the Royal Decree of Hospitality. It had justified the parley and the exchange and the unprecedented tolerance of a secular fleet at the Faithful cordon. It had justified [[Father Beauchamp|Beauchamp]]'s own crossing — his own decision to hand his Orichalcum crucifix to [[Rebecca Osei|Osei]] and trust that the modern world was not what the Office of the Propagation of Virtue suspected it might be.

The letter did not break the ark theology. But it bent it — bent it past the point where it could support the weight that had been placed upon it.

"The ark theology says that the modern world was preserved," [[Father Beauchamp|Beauchamp]] said. "It does not say that the modern world knows it was preserved. The Prime Minister's refusal — it is not a denial of Providence. It is an admission of ignorance. He does not say *God did not preserve us.* He says *I do not know if God preserved us.* That is not heresy. That is —" He searched for the word. "That is humility. A humility we do not have a category for, because in our world, faith is not a choice. It is a condition. A necessity. The Prime Minister's world has lived without that necessity for eight centuries. Their faith is not absent. It is — waiting. It is waiting for a reason to believe that we cannot give them and they cannot give themselves. The letter does not break the ark theology. It asks the ark theology a question the ark theology was not designed to answer: *what if the ark does not know it is an ark?*"

The silence that followed was long. Morgan was staring at the table. Ashby was staring out the stern windows at the grey Atlantic and the distant shimmer of the portal. [[Alistair Pembroke|Pembroke]] was staring at the transcribed letter as if it might rearrange itself into something he could work with.

And then the door of the wardroom opened — not the knock-and-wait of a junior officer requesting permission to enter, but the direct, unhesitating opening of someone who had authority in this room and knew it.

[[Bishop Aldric|Aldric]] stood in the doorway.

He looked different. That was [[Father Beauchamp|Beauchamp]]'s first thought — not *he is here*, not *he has returned*, but *he looks different*. The Bishop of Southwark, who had crossed the portal twelve days ago in full episcopal regalia, who had been described in the reports as a man of iron conviction, who had called modern London more terrifying than any Heretic trench — that bishop was standing in the doorway of *[[HMS Resolute|HMS Resolute]]*'s wardroom in the same black-and-silver vestments, and his face was the same face, and his bearing was the same bearing. But something behind his eyes had shifted. Something in the way he held his shoulders had loosened. The certainty was still there — the conviction, the devotion, the unshakeable core of a man who had spent his life in the service of a Church that was also a fortress. But it had been joined by something else. Something quieter. Something that looked, [[Father Beauchamp|Beauchamp]] thought, almost like doubt — except that doubt was the wrong word, and wonder was closer, and neither word was quite right.

"Your Grace," [[Alistair Pembroke|Pembroke]] said, rising. "We were not expecting you until —"

"I know," [[Bishop Aldric|Aldric]] said. "I returned early. The moderns — your destroyer, the one that was to bring me across — is holding at the portal. I came ahead by boat. There is something I need to tell you. All of you. Before you answer the Prime Minister's letter."

[[Father Beauchamp|Beauchamp]] looked at the bishop. The bishop looked back — not at him, but at the transcribed letter on the table, at the words of a secular Prime Minister who had refused the name of Providence and offered friendship instead. And what [[Father Beauchamp|Beauchamp]] saw in the bishop's face was not judgment. It was recognition. The recognition of a man who had seen something he had not expected to see — and was still, even now, trying to find the words for it.

"The Prime Minister's reply has arrived," [[Alistair Pembroke|Pembroke]] said. "We were discussing —"

"I know what it says," [[Bishop Aldric|Aldric]] said. "I heard the transmission. The destroyer's communications picked it up. That is — that is part of what I need to tell you." He stepped into the wardroom and closed the door behind him. The coal smoke swirled in the ventilation. The beeswax glinted on the dark wood. The Atlantic pressed against the hull, patient and permanent, the sea that was the same sea on both sides of the portal and the thing that held them all together.

"I have spent the past week in the modern world," [[Bishop Aldric|Aldric]] said. "I have walked through its city. I have spoken to its Archbishop. I have read its Bible and listened to its prayers and watched its people live lives that have never been shaped by the war we have been fighting for eight centuries. And I have come to believe —" He paused. The words were hard. He had not said them aloud before — not to [[Peter Chisholm|Chisholm]], not to himself, not to anyone. "I have come to believe that we have been wrong. Not about God. About them. About what they are."

Morgan leaned forward. Ashby turned from the windows. [[Alistair Pembroke|Pembroke]] was very still. [[Father Beauchamp|Beauchamp]] felt something open in his chest — not fear, but a kind of anticipation, the anticipation of a man who had built a theology and was now about to watch it tested.

"They are not asleep," [[Bishop Aldric|Aldric]] said. "I told [[Monsignor Franco Ricci|Monsignor Ricci]] — I told the reports — that their world is a world that has forgotten God. I was wrong. They have not forgotten God. They have been forced to find Him in the silence. Their churches are not fortresses, but they are not empty either. Their faith does not wear armour, but it does not break. The Archbishop of Canterbury — his world's Archbishop, the shepherd of a Church that I dismissed as a museum — told me that his faith had been tested to breaking and had held. Not because it was strong. Because it was true. Because when a terrified child in a prison in Leeds asked him if God could forgive him, he knew the answer — and the answer was still yes, and it had been waiting for someone to speak it, and he was the one who spoke it. That is what faith is in the modern world, [[Father Beauchamp|Beauchamp]]. Not a fortress. A voice in the dark. A word spoken into a silence that no miracle breaks and no adversary confirms. A word that holds — not because the evidence is visible, but because the word itself is true."

[[Father Beauchamp|Beauchamp]] did not speak. He was thinking of [[Daniel Foster|Foster]] — of the modern chaplain who had first put the question to him, who had asked what it meant that the modern world had survived without God. He was thinking of the ark theology, and the way it had assumed that the modern world was a vessel — a thing preserved, a thing carried, a thing that was passive in its own preservation. And he was thinking that [[Bishop Aldric|Aldric]] was right, and that the ark theology was right too, but that they were right about different things, and the difference might be everything.

"What are they, then?" [[Alistair Pembroke|Pembroke]] asked. The question was not hostile. It was the question of a captain who needed a framework for action and was being offered a framework for contemplation instead.

"They are witnesses," [[Bishop Aldric|Aldric]] said. "Not instruments. Not an ark. Not a test. They are a people who have lived through the hiddenness of God — the *Deus Absconditus*, the God who conceals Himself so that faith can be freely chosen — and they have come out the other side. They are not what we expected. They are not what we hoped for. But they are not our enemies either. They are a mirror. They show us what we might become — what faith might become — if we stopped looking for God in the miracles and started looking for Him in the ordinary. In the food banks and the prison chaplaincies and the quiet cathedrals that still fill on Christmas and Easter with people who come because something in them needs to hear the old story one more time."

He paused. "We must wait. We must watch. We must not embrace them as saviours — they are not saviours. [[Father Beauchamp|Beauchamp]] is right that they were preserved, but he is wrong about what that preservation means. They are not a vessel sent to rescue us. They are a parallel — a people who walked a different path through the same wilderness, and who can show us what the wilderness looks like from the other side. And we must not reject them as heretics — they are not heretics. [[Inquisitor Paulus|Paulus]] is right to be cautious, but he is wrong about what caution demands. The Office's instinct to test, to judge, to categorise — it cannot see what they are, because what they are is not something that can be categorised. They are a people who have been hidden by God. You cannot judge what God has hidden. You can only wait — and watch — and let the meaning reveal itself in its own time."

"And in the meantime?" Morgan's voice was sharp — the voice of a captain who had been asked to absorb theology when he needed tactics.

"In the meantime," [[Bishop Aldric|Aldric]] said, "we answer the Prime Minister's letter as honestly as he wrote it. He has refused the name of Providence. He has offered friendship instead. I believe — having seen his world, having spoken to his Archbishop, having watched his people live — that the friendship is genuine. And I believe that accepting the friendship without the framing is not a compromise. It is —" He searched for the word, and found it, and spoke it carefully, as if it were fragile and might shatter in the speaking. "It is faith. Faith that God is working even when we cannot see the work. Faith that the moderns are what they claim to be, even if they do not claim to be what we need. Faith that waiting is not passivity — it is trust. And trust, in a world at war with Hell, is the hardest thing any of us has ever been asked to give."

The wardroom was silent. The coal smoke curled. The Atlantic pressed against the hull. Through the stern windows, the portal shimmered on the horizon — patient, permanent, waiting.

[[Father Beauchamp|Beauchamp]] looked at the letter on the table — the transcription, the cramped handwriting, the words of a man who had refused Providence and offered friendship — and he looked at [[Bishop Aldric|Aldric]], who had returned from the modern world carrying a theology that neither he nor [[Inquisitor Paulus|Paulus]] had expected. And he understood, with a clarity that was almost painful, that the Faithful command was no longer deciding between two options — acceptance or rejection of the Prime Minister's framing. They were deciding between three. Acceptance, rejection, or waiting. And waiting — the bishop's word, the bishop's theology, the bishop's quiet, unarmed faith in a God who did not always announce Himself — was not the retreat it sounded like. It was the hardest of the three. Because it required them to act — to accept the alliance, to open their ports, to share their knowledge and their woundedness and their fear — without knowing whether what they were acting upon was Providence or chance or something that had no name at all.

And then, before [[Alistair Pembroke|Pembroke]] could respond, before Morgan could object, before Ashby could turn from the window and add his voice to the argument, the door of the wardroom opened again — this time with the knock-and-wait, the formal entry, the white-faced urgency of the young signals officer who had transcribed the letter and who was now holding a second transcription, folded and sealed, his hands trembling slightly as he offered it to his captain.

He did not know the chief's name. He would never know the chief's name. But he would remember the gesture — the small, unthinking kindness of a sailor who had seen a stranger in danger and had tried, in the only way available to him, to help.

The door closed behind him. The ship hummed. The sea waited. And on the grey horizon, the portal shimmered — patient, permanent, indifferent to the wreckage of the morning and the theology of the afternoon and the long, slow work of two worlds learning to see each other clearly.

## Key Decisions

- [[Stephen Davenport|Davenport]] writes his reply to [[King Robert the Longsword|King Robert]] through the night — not as a diplomatic formula but as a surrender to honesty. He refuses the Providence framing while offering concrete alliance, friendship, and a secular commitment that asks nothing theological in return.
- The letter is transmitted to the Faithful cordon, where [[Alistair Pembroke|Pembroke]], [[Father Beauchamp|Beauchamp]], Morgan of the HMS *Herald*, and Ashby of the torpedo-boat destroyers receive it with mixed emotions. Pembroke respects the honesty. Beauchamp finds it honorable. Morgan and Ashby see emptiness — an alliance without a reason, a gift without a giver.
- [[Bishop Aldric]] returns from the modern world carrying a new theology — the *theology of waiting* — born from his conversation with [[Peter Chisholm|Archbishop Chisholm]] and his immersion in the modern world. He argues that the moderns are neither ark (Beauchamp) nor test (Paulus) — they are witnesses, a people who have lived through the hiddenness of God (*Deus Absconditus*) and whose meaning is not yet fully revealed. The Faithful must wait, watch, and learn — not embrace the moderns as saviors nor reject them as heretics.
- Before the Faithful command can formulate a unified response to Davenport's letter, *[[USS Stout (DDG-55)|USS Stout]]* detects a Heretic submarine conducting systematic intelligence surveillance on the portal cordon — intercepting and relaying every transmission from both fleets.
- *[[USS Stout (DDG-55)|USS Stout]]*, which had crossed the portal with permission to transfer [[Bishop Aldric|Aldric]] to *[[HMS Resolute]]*, engages the submarine at flank speed. The submarine uses the *[[HMS Resolute|Resolute]]* as cover; in the close-quarters chase, *[[USS Stout (DDG-55)|Stout]]*'s bow tears into the *[[HMS Resolute|Resolute]]*'s port side below the waterline, leaving the Faithful flagship listing and mission-incapable.
- The Heretic submarine attempts to flee. *[[USS Stout (DDG-55)|Stout]]* reacquires the contact, pursues, and destroys it with a single Mk 54 torpedo — thirty-one seconds from launch to impact. [[Bishop Aldric|Aldric]] watches the entire sequence from the open deck.
- The collision shatters the fragile trust between the fleets — a Faithful dreadnought crippled by a modern destroyer in Faithful waters, in the confusion of a chase against a shared enemy. The aftermath leaves the Faithful command holding three unresolved things: Davenport's reply, Aldric's theology, and the wreckage of the *[[HMS Resolute|Resolute]]*.

## New Concepts Introduced

- [[Davenport's Reply]] — the letter from [[Stephen Davenport|Davenport]] to [[King Robert the Longsword|King Robert]], respectfully refusing the "instrument of Providence" framing while offering full alliance, partnership, and friendship on secular-moral grounds. The letter pivots from theology to shared humanity without pretending the pivot is costless.
- [[The Theology of Waiting]] — [[Bishop Aldric|Aldric]]'s new theological framework, born from his pilgrimage in the modern world and his conversation with [[Peter Chisholm|Archbishop Chisholm]]. The moderns are neither an ark nor a test; they are a people who have survived the hiddenness of God, and their meaning is not yet revealed. The Faithful must wait, watch, and learn rather than rushing to embrace or reject.
- [[The Heretic Submarine]] — a Heretic Legion submarine, constructed sometime after 1866, conducting systematic intelligence surveillance on the portal cordon — intercepting and relaying all fleet transmissions to an unknown Heretic command post. First confirmed Heretic presence at the portal. Destroyed by *[[USS Stout (DDG-55)|USS Stout]]* after the collision with *[[HMS Resolute|HMS Resolute]]*.
