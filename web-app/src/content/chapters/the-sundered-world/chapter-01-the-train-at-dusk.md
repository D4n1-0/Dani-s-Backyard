# Chapter 1 – The Train at Dusk

## Summary

In a coastal border town on the Tethyan shore, a troop train rattles the supermarket windows at dusk. A can falls. A girl catches it with a gesture from childhood — and a boy who has spent six years looking for her face in every crowd says her name for the first time since she left.

---

## Scenes

### Scene 1: The Girl at the Register

The supermarket opened at six in the morning and closed at nine at night, and [[Asahi]] worked the after-school shift — two to eight, weekdays, with Saturdays if the old woman's breathing was bad.

She knew the rhythms of the place the way she'd once known the sound of her grandmother's footsteps in the precinct apartment — the particular creak of the third floorboard from the door, the pause before the knock. Here, the rhythms were different but no less specific. The fluorescent tubes hummed at a frequency that made her temples ache by noon. The refrigeration unit in the back coughed once every forty-seven seconds — she'd counted, once, during a slow Tuesday when the only customer in three hours had been a harbour cat that slipped through the loading door and stole a smoked fish. The front window, barred with iron grilles that the old woman's husband had welded himself after the last Five-Year Tension turned ugly, looked out across the harbour road toward the grey water of the [[Tethyan Ocean]], where the [[The Celestial Tether|Celestial Tether's]] three silver ribbons were just visible on the horizon — three threads of light catching the late-afternoon sun like scratches on the sky.

The [[The Celestial Tether|Tether]] was the first thing every child in [[Kedros Garrison]] learned to ignore. By seventeen, Asahi had mastered the art of not looking at it. She knew it was there — knew it was *always* there, the great silver needle rising from an artificial island at the centre of the ocean, three ribbons ascending beyond the edge of the atmosphere into a geostationary point no one on [[Gaethon]] had ever seen clearly — but she had trained herself to treat it like weather. Like the census. Like the Five-Year Tension that was currently overdue and making the garrison commander twitchy and the harbour-master irritable and the customers in her supermarket sharper with their coins than usual.

She'd learned the rhythms of the Tensions too. Every five years, like clockwork, the border dispute between the [[Federated Crowns of Valdris]] and the [[Iron Concordat]] flared up — a ritual of diplomatic letters, troop rotations, and requisitioned warehouses. The last one had been six years ago, which meant this one was running late, which made everyone nervous in the specific way that Valdrans got nervous about things that didn't adhere to schedule. The garrison had doubled its complement through rotating deployments. The harbour was crowded with warships — two pre-dreadnoughts and a screen of torpedo sloops that had been anchored in the outer basin for three weeks, their crews growing restless and spending too much money in the dockside taverns. The troop trains ran day and night now, carrying soldiers and supplies from the Valdran interior to the border outposts, and every time one passed the supermarket the windows rattled and the cans on Shelf Four — beans, peas, condensed milk in blue tins — began to tremble.

Asahi had learned to compensate for the vibration without thinking. When the cans began their warning tremor, she would reach up and steady them with her left hand while her right continued making change, weighing vegetables, stamping ration cards. It was a skill she hadn't asked for and didn't want, like the ability to recite the census bureau's population thresholds from memory or the knack of knowing which transmigrant families were about to be relocated before the letters went out — the census clerk always shopped on Thursdays, and she bought more tinned food than usual when the relocation queue was being drafted.

Beneath the collar of her uniform, pinned to the inside of her shirt where no one could see, she wore a small enamel pin — the crest of Precinct Four, Valdris Prime. Her grandmother had given it to her the morning the furniture truck came. *You're a Valdran*, she'd said, her voice flat in the way old Valdran women had perfected — not cold, but pressed, like a document filed in triplicate. *Wherever they send you, you're still from here. Don't let them forget it. Don't let yourself forget it.*

The pin was the only thing Asahi had kept from the precinct, besides the stone. And the stone didn't count, because the stone had been given to her by someone else, and thinking about that someone hurt in a way she'd trained herself not to examine.

At seventeen, Asahi had the posture of a woman twice her age. Spine straight, chin up, shoulders back. It was the posture her grandmother had taught her on Sunday afternoons in the precinct's green belt, walking laps around the mandated parkland while the census tower loomed above them like a finger held to the city's lips. *If you walk like you know where you're going, the census-takers assume you do.* The posture had survived transmigration, relocation, three different schools, and the particular indignity of being the new girl in a coastal town where "new" meant "transmigrant" and "transmigrant" meant "temporary" and "temporary" meant "we don't need to learn your name because the Tension will evacuate you in a year anyway."

She'd been here six years. The Tension hadn't evacuated her yet. She still wasn't sure if that meant she was lucky or just forgotten.

Her shift had started at two. By five-thirty, she'd served forty-one customers — harbour workers, garrison soldiers on leave, a fisherman who paid for his bread with coins that still smelled of the Tethyan. The harbour fog was rolling in early, the way it always did in late summer when the warm air met the cold current. Through the barred window, Asahi could see the masts of the fishing boats swaying in the inner basin, the grey shapes of the pre-dreadnoughts farther out, and beyond them all — always beyond — the [[The Celestial Tether|Tether]]'s three silver lines, catching the last clean light before the fog swallowed them.

At five forty-seven, the troop train's whistle blew.

It was the eastern line — Asahi knew the pitch. The western line was higher, thinner, the sound of the passenger service running empty coaches back toward the Valdran interior to pick up the next batch of transmigrant families. The eastern line was lower, heavier, the sound of army transports. She'd learned to distinguish them the way a sailor learned to read tides. The eastern whistle meant the garrison was still receiving. The eastern whistle meant the Tension was still building. The eastern whistle meant the garrison commander would be on the telegraph to [[Valdris Prime]] again tonight, asking for clarification on rules of engagement that never seemed to arrive.

The vibration started in the floor first — a deep, bass hum that travelled up through the concrete and into the soles of Asahi's shoes — before it reached the shelves. The fluorescent tubes flickered. The refrigeration unit skipped one of its forty-seven-second coughs. And the cans on Shelf Four began to rattle.

Asahi reached up to steady them without looking. Her left hand found the shelf's edge; her right hand was in the process of bagging three onions for a woman in a grey transmigrant coat who stood at the register, counting coins from a leather purse worn soft at the edges.

"Sixty-three," Asahi said. "Sixty-four. Sixty-five."

The woman in the grey coat was perhaps fifty, perhaps seventy — transmigrant life aged people in ways the census bureau's actuarial tables couldn't capture. She had the look of someone who'd been relocated more than once: a permanent uncertainty in how she held her purse, a tendency to glance toward the harbour as if expecting to see her name on a ship's manifest.

"Thank you," the woman said — not meeting Asahi's eyes, because transmigrants didn't meet each other's eyes. Eye contact was a luxury for people who knew they'd be in the same town next season.

"Thank *you*," Asahi replied, the Valdran politeness reflex so deeply ingrained it had survived six years of coastal-town salt and garrison-town suspicion.

Then the shelves bucked.

It was a bigger tremor than usual — one of the heavy transport cars, maybe, the ones that carried field guns or ammunition crates or whatever the Concordat's latest border provocation had prompted the Chamber of Magnates to authorise this week. The supermarket's front window shivered in its iron frame. From the back room, where she kept the kettle and the spare ration-card forms, the old woman who owned the store called out — *"Was that the train or the Tension?"* — a joke she'd been making for thirty years, one Asahi had heard a hundred times and still smiled at because the old woman's voice was thin today, the fog getting into her chest again.

And a can of condensed milk — the blue tin, the sweet one, the one Asahi had restocked twice already today — tipped forward off Shelf Four and fell.

Asahi's hand moved.

It moved before her eyes registered the motion, before her brain issued the command, before any conscious part of her was involved. It was a gesture from another life — a riverbank life, a precinct life, a life where the most important thing in the world was whether a skipping stone would touch the water four times or five. Her palm opened. Her fingers curled. The can landed in her grip as if it had been aiming for her hand all along — *thwump*, a soft metallic kiss against her skin that no one in the store noticed except the woman in the grey coat, who blinked once and said nothing, because transmigrants didn't comment on each other's reflexes.

Asahi set the can on the counter as though it were nothing. As though her heart weren't suddenly pounding. As though she weren't suddenly nine years old again, standing at the edge of a river that ran through a green belt in a city she would never see again, catching a skipping stone that a boy had tossed back to her because she'd asked to see it one more time before she threw.

The woman in the grey coat took her onions and left. The fluorescent tubes steadied. The eastern train continued its slow grind past the supermarket, past the harbour road, past the transmigrant district where identical two-storey tenements stood in identical rows on a low rise above the sea, waiting for the next census order to empty them out and fill them up again.

And someone, somewhere in the supermarket, said her name.

### Scene 2: The Boy at the Airfield

Three hours earlier, [[Akahito]] had been lying on his back under a biplane engine, trying to determine where the oil leak was coming from.

The airfield sat on the headland east of town — a grass strip that turned to mud in the spring and dust in the summer, three iron hangars that had been built in Year 210 PC and patched so many times the original metal was more weld than wall, and a wooden control tower that leaned three degrees to the left and had been doing so for so long that the mechanics considered it straight. From the hangar doors, on clear days, you could see the whole sweep of the [[Tethyan Ocean|Tethyan]] coast — the harbour, the breakwater, the grey shapes of the pre-dreadnoughts at anchor, and on the horizon, where the sea met the sky, the [[The Celestial Tether|Celestial Tether's]] three silver threads catching the light like scratches on glass.

School had let out at two. By three, Akahito was already under the *Skylark*'s oil pan, his school shirt wrapped in a rag and stuffed in his bag.

Akahito had been watching [[The Celestial Tether|the Tether]] since he arrived in Kedros two years ago. Not out of fear — fear was a luxury his family couldn't afford, the census had made sure of that — but out of a quiet, methodical fascination. He'd mapped its visibility patterns by season and weather: clearest in winter when the air was cold and dry, faintest in summer when the harbour fog rolled in, and every evening at dusk it caught the sunset at a slightly different angle, as if the Island at the ocean's centre was slowly, imperceptibly rotating. He'd written none of this down. Some observations were too dangerous to record, even in the margins of an engine maintenance log.

The biplane he was currently buried under was a Valdran *Skylark*-class scout — a fabric-covered, twin-winged thing with an inline six-cylinder engine and a habit of leaking oil from a gasket that was supposed to be replaced every hundred flight hours but had been in service for a hundred and forty. Akahito had been working on the leak for two afternoons. He liked problems like this — mechanical, solvable, indifferent. An engine didn't care where you were born or whether your family was on a census relocation list. An engine leaked or it didn't. If it leaked, you fixed it. If you fixed it, it ran. There was a dignity in that simplicity that the rest of Kedros didn't offer.

"You're going to miss dinner again," said Tasos, appearing under the wing with a grease-stained rag and a cup of coffee that had probably been fresh three hours ago.

Tasos was the head mechanic — a grizzled Valdran veteran who'd lost two fingers to a howitzer breech during the Tension of '98 and his faith in the census bureau shortly thereafter. He was the kind of man who didn't care much about regulations but cared enormously about whether you showed up on time and kept your tools clean. He'd hired Akahito at fifteen — younger than the official age — because Akahito had walked into the hangar one afternoon, asked if they needed help, and then spent four hours silently organising a parts shelf that hadn't been touched since the Tension of '89. Tasos had watched him work for ten minutes, then handed him a wrench and said, "Don't make me regret this."

Akahito hadn't.

"I'll eat when it's fixed," Akahito said.

"It's been 'almost fixed' since Tuesday."

"The gasket's got a hairline crack. I can see it now — right there, see? — the seal's worn through on the inner edge. If I replace it with the spare from Hangar Two, it'll hold for maybe fifty hours, but the spare's not rated for this engine — "

"You're stalling."

Akahito's hands stopped moving. He didn't look at Tasos. He stared up at the biplane's undercarriage, at the oil drip that was still, despite everything, leaking onto the hangar floor.

"I'm not stalling."

"You've been under that plane for two hours. The gasket was replaced at noon. You're stalling because the troop train goes past the supermarket at quarter to six, and you're trying to decide whether to be on it or off it when it does."

Akahito said nothing. Tasos, who had been seventeen once and had spent his own adolescence watching a girl from Precinct Nine get on a relocation truck he couldn't follow, didn't push. He just set the coffee cup on the wing — carefully, where Akahito could reach it — and walked back toward the hangar door, where [[The Celestial Tether|the Tether]] was just visible through the gathering fog.

"There's a transmigrant family arriving on the evening passenger service," Tasos said over his shoulder. "Tenement 4C. They've got three children and more boxes than they can carry. Thought you might want to know."

Akahito slid out from under the biplane. The grease on his hands was permanent now — embedded in the creases of his knuckles, dark crescents under his fingernails that no amount of scrubbing could remove. At seventeen, his hands looked ten years older than the rest of him.

"I'll be at the depot," he said.

"Thought so," said Tasos, and didn't smile, because Tasos never smiled when it mattered.

### Scene 3: Flashback — The Riverbank (Year ~305 PC)

There was a river at the edge of the precinct.

It was not a large river. It was not a famous river. It appeared on none of the civic maps distributed by the Valdran Chamber of Magnates, because the civic maps were concerned with populations and precincts and the precise locations of census towers, and a river that flowed through a mandated green belt was, by definition, not demographically significant. But it mattered to the children of Precinct Four. To them, it was the only place in the city that didn't feel counted.

The green belt that surrounded it was eleven metres wide — the legal minimum for a precinct separation zone, a strip of parkland and scrub oak whose sole purpose was to ensure that Precinct Four and Precinct Five were "not continuously built." The children didn't know this. They knew only that the green belt was where you went when you had coins for sweets or stones for skipping or nothing at all except the need to be somewhere the census-takers didn't patrol.

[[Akahito]] was nine years old and built like a question mark.

His shoulders curved inward — not from any physical defect, but from a learned instinct, the habit of a boy who had been told since birth that taking up too much space was a census violation in spirit if not in letter. His mother, a railway clerk's wife with the weary precision of someone who had filled out more census forms than she had birthday cards, had raised him on a single principle: *Don't draw attention.* Not to the apartment, not to the neighbours, not to the census tower whose spire was visible from every window in Precinct Four. Attention was a currency, and Akahito's family had none to spend.

So he learned to be small. He walked with his hands in his pockets and his eyes on the ground, which had the unintended benefit of making him an excellent finder of skipping stones.

"The key," he told [[Asahi]] one afternoon, crouching at the edge of the riverbank with a handful of stones spread out before him like a merchant's wares, "is the weight ratio. Too heavy, it sinks on the first skip. Too light, it catches the wind and spins. You want it flat, round, and heavy enough to cut the surface but not so heavy that the surface cuts back."

Asahi, who was also nine and who considered herself the superior stone-skipper by virtue of having once achieved five skips to his four, snatched a stone from his palm and threw it without looking.

It sank immediately.

"You're flicking your wrist too early," Akahito said.

"I'm not."

"You are."

He didn't argue when he said it. He never argued. Akahito had a way of stating things — quietly, patiently, with the absolute certainty of someone who had watched the world carefully and drawn his conclusions before opening his mouth — that made arguing feel beside the point. He was right, and he knew he was right, and he wasn't going to make you feel bad about being wrong. He would just wait for you to catch up.

"Watch me," he said, and he threw.

Six skips. The stone touched the water six times — *tick-tick-tick-tick-tick-plunk* — and vanished into the green depths with a sound like a door clicking shut.

Asahi punched him in the arm.

"I hate you."

"No you don't."

She did not, and they both knew it. They had known each other since before they could remember knowing anyone — their families lived three doors apart in the same precinct block, their mothers traded recipes across the hallway, their fathers shared a bench at the tram stop every morning at seven-fifteen. Akahito had been present at Asahi's sixth birthday party and her seventh and her eighth, had walked her to school every morning since the first grade, had sat beside her through every census compliance lesson the precinct school required, memorising the population thresholds and the violation penalties and the precise wording of the [[Kaelen Mandate|Mandate's]] most terrifying article — *No continuously built urban area may house more than one million souls* — not because he was a diligent student but because Asahi was, and sitting beside her meant doing what she did.

"My dad says we might get selected," Asahi said, picking up another stone — a bad one, round and heavy and probably useless. She threw it. It sank.

The river went quiet. Not the water — the water kept moving, indifferent. But the air between them changed, the way air changes when a door opens somewhere in the house and you don't know who's coming through it.

Akahito didn't say anything. He was the kind of boy who noticed when someone was hurting, and the kind of boy who knew that sometimes the best response to news like that wasn't words. His mother, after a census compliance inspection that had found their apartment's occupancy ratio "technically compliant but warranting ongoing monitoring," had cried at the kitchen table for an hour without making a sound, and Akahito had sat across from her and not said a word, because what words were there? The census didn't care about tears. The Mandate didn't care about fairness.

"Which precinct would they send you to?" he finally asked.

"They don't send you to precincts," Asahi said. Her voice was flat — she was testing it, trying on the flatness like a coat she'd have to grow into. "They send you to the border towns. The coastal garrisons. The places where they need people to keep the population above the minimum threshold but below the maximum."

"How do you know that?"

"I read the relocation protocols. In the library."

Akahito didn't ask why a nine-year-old was reading census relocation protocols. He already knew. Asahi read everything — she was the kind of girl who treated information as a weapon, who believed the system could be outmanoeuvred if you just understood it well enough. It was part of why he liked her, and part of why he worried about her. The system didn't like being understood.

He reached into his pocket and pulled out the flattest, smoothest stone she had ever seen — almost black, almost shiny, worn down by the river to the exact thickness of a census form.

"Here," he said. "This one. Keep it."

She looked at the stone. She looked at him. She was nine years old and she already knew that the world was arranged in such a way that people like her — old precinct families, people who walked with their chins up — were the first to be selected for relocation, because the census bureau liked to make examples of the proud. She also knew that Akahito had spent the entire afternoon finding this stone, had probably rejected fifty others before settling on this one, had held it in his pocket for hours waiting for the moment when giving it to her would mean something.

She put it in her pocket. She wrapped it in a handkerchief that night. She still had it, six years later, at the bottom of her bag, because she'd never been able to bring herself to throw it — and because throwing it would mean admitting the boy who'd given it to her was gone, and she wasn't ready.

Not yet.

Maybe not ever.

### Scene 4: Flashback — The Letter (Year ~306 PC)

The transmigration letter arrived on a Tuesday.

Asahi's mother, [[Asahi|Rei]], opened it at the kitchen table at seven in the morning, before her children were awake. She read it twice. She set it down next to her coffee cup. She stared at the wall above the stove for eleven minutes — Asahi, watching from the hallway, counted — and then she folded the letter into precise thirds and slid it into her apron pocket, where it would stay for the next three days while she decided how to tell her family.

Rei was a Valdran woman of the old school — not old in years, though she had aged faster than the calendar suggested, but old in values. She believed in order. She believed in compliance. She believed that the census system, for all its cruelties, was the reason Valdris Prime still stood while the [[The Sunken Kingdom of Myrr|Sunken Kingdom of Myrr]] was a vitrified bay and [[Keth]] was a glass crater. She had never questioned the Mandate's authority. She had also, until this morning, never been personally selected for relocation.

The two things were not unrelated.

Asahi's father, [[Asahi|Davik]], argued with the census clerk for four hours. Asahi heard him through the walls — not shouting, because Davik never shouted, but using the precise, clipped tone he reserved for railway schedules and people who had disappointed him. He cited their generational tenure — five generations in Precinct Four, a record that appeared in the civic archives if anyone bothered to look. He cited his mother's grave in the precinct cemetery, two hundred years old and still visited every spring. He cited the statistical weaknesses in the projection model: the variability of migration patterns, the margin of error in the annual growth rate, the fact that Precinct Seven was demonstrably closer to the cap and had higher density and why weren't *they* being relocated first?

Asahi pressed her ear to the wall and listened to every word. Her younger brother, Ryo — then four years old, too young to understand what was happening, too young to remember this day when he grew up — sat on the floor beside her, drawing stick figures on a scrap of census paper. He drew a house. He drew a sun. He drew a train. Asahi looked at his drawing and felt something crack open inside her chest.

The census clerk — Asahi never learned her name, would never forgive her for it — listened to all of Davik's arguments and said, in a voice that had clearly delivered this speech a thousand times before, "The projection model is not negotiable, sir. The relocation order is not negotiable. Your family's destination assignment has been processed. Your apartment in [[Kedros Garrison]] has been reserved. Would you like the extended deadline or the standard deadline?"

The extended deadline was ninety days. The standard was fourteen.

Davik took the standard. He said later — much later, when Kedros was home and the precinct was memory and the census had become just another fact of life, like the harbour fog and the troop-train schedules and the way the Tethyan wind smelled of salt and coal — that he'd taken the standard deadline because he couldn't bear to spend ninety days watching his family live in a house that had already stopped being theirs.

Asahi never asked if he meant it.

The furniture truck came three mornings later. It was grey, military-surplus, a vehicle that had been manufactured by the same Valdran foundry that produced the garrison's anti-airship batteries and its census-relocation fleet — the two pillars of Mandate compliance made manifest in diesel and steel. The movers were transmigrants themselves — men from Precinct Eleven, relocated five years earlier, now working for the bureau's logistics division at a wage that was technically above the minimum threshold and practically below the cost of survival. They packed the apartment in two hours. They didn't make eye contact.

Akahito came to the door at four in the afternoon, but Asahi wouldn't see him.

She sat on her bed — the bed that would be dismantled in an hour — with the flat black stone in her hand, running her thumb over its surface, memorising its weight. She'd already packed everything else: her clothes, her school books, the enamel precinct pin her grandmother had pressed into her palm that morning with the instruction *don't let them forget*. The stone was the last thing. She couldn't decide whether to put it in her bag or leave it on the windowsill, where it had lived for the past year, catching the morning light.

In the end, she put it in her pocket.

Through the wall, she heard her mother offer Akahito tea. She heard the clink of the kettle, the scrape of a chair, the long silence that followed. She imagined him sitting at the kitchen table, in the chair where she usually sat, drinking tea from her mother's second-best cup because the first-best had already been packed. She imagined him looking at the empty hook where the family calendar used to hang. She imagined him not crying, because Akahito never cried — not where anyone could see.

She didn't go out to meet him. She was afraid that if she saw his face, she wouldn't get on the truck.

She got on the truck anyway — of course she did. There was no alternative. The census didn't offer alternatives, and the Mandate didn't negotiate, and as the truck pulled away from the curb, Asahi stood at the back window and watched Precinct Four shrink behind her. The riverbank. The green belt. The apartment block with its six legal storeys. The census tower rising at the city centre like a finger held to the lips of the city. And at the corner where the tram line turned — a small figure with his hands in his pockets, not waving, just standing there, getting smaller and smaller until the truck rounded the bend and the precinct was gone.

She cried for the first time since the letter arrived.

She cried because she'd left something behind, and she didn't know what it was, and she didn't know if she'd ever get it back.

### Scene 5: Flashback — The Empty House (Year ~308 PC)

Akahito walked past her house every day for two years.

The bureau had assigned a new family three weeks after Asahi's left — a young couple from Precinct Nine, a husband who worked in the municipal water department and a wife who was pregnant with their first child. They painted the door green. They planted geraniums in the window boxes. They hung a small wind chime on the balcony — a gift from the husband's mother, Akahito assumed, because it was the kind of gift you gave someone when they moved into a new apartment and you wanted to pretend it felt like home.

They didn't know that the previous family had lived there for five generations. They didn't know that the scratch on the kitchen table's left leg was from a butter knife, wielded by a seven-year-old girl who'd wanted to carve her initials into something permanent and had chosen the wrong surface. They didn't know that the windowsill in the back bedroom had held a flat black stone for a year, that the stone had rested in exactly the spot where the morning light hit first, that the girl who'd kept it there had stood at that window every evening watching the tram line for a boy who always walked her home.

Akahito knew all of this. He knew it with the precision of someone who had spent his childhood memorising the details of a person he hadn't understood he might lose. He walked past the green door every afternoon on his way home from school, his hands in his pockets, his shoulders curved inward, his eyes tracking the geraniums and the wind chime and the new mailbox with the new family's name painted on it in careful Valdran script. The new family's infant grew into a toddler who sometimes waved at him from the window. He waved back.

He never stopped walking.

The day the green door appeared, he'd stood on the corner for twenty minutes, staring. The blue door — the old door, Asahi's door — had been the colour of the precinct flag, a particular shade of blue that appeared in every civic document and every census form and every school uniform. Akahito had walked through that door approximately three thousand times. He'd never thought about its colour before. Now he couldn't stop thinking about it.

Green was wrong. Green was the colour of the precinct's mandated green belts, the colour of *separation*, the colour of the buffer zones the census bureau maintained between districts to keep the population count legally distinct. Green did not belong on a door.

His mother, who had noticed the change earlier and spent three days deciding whether to tell him, found him on the corner that evening. She didn't say anything. She just stood beside him, her hand resting on his shoulder with the weight of someone who understood that some losses couldn't be spoken.

"It's just paint," she said eventually.

"It's not," he said.

She didn't argue. His mother never argued either. It was one of the things they shared — a recognition that some truths didn't need to be defended, just acknowledged.

Two weeks before his own transmigration letter arrived — drawn in the same lottery system that had taken her, with the same indifferent arithmetic, the same mechanical spin of numbers that never asked who you were or how long you'd lived there — Akahito went to the riverbank one last time.

It was late autumn. The water was low, exposing the flat stones that had been submerged all summer. He didn't pick any up. He just stood at the edge, where the green belt ended and the railway began, and watched the light die on the far bank. The census tower was visible through the bare trees, its spire catching the last of the sunset — the same sunset that was falling on Valdris Prime, on the Chamber of Magnates, on the diarchy's endless debates. [[The Celestial Tether|The Tether]] — not yet a part of his daily landscape, not yet the first thing he'd see every morning from the airfield hangar — was still a distant rumour, a silver scratch on the horizon that the newspapers mentioned but no one in Precinct Four had ever seen clearly.

Akahito thought about Asahi. He thought about the skipping stone. He thought about whether she'd kept it, whether she still had it, whether six years in a coastal border town had worn her smooth the way the river wore down its stones — or whether she was still the girl who flicked her wrist too early.

He didn't know. But he was about to find out.

His father's letter arrived two days later. Destination: Kedros Garrison. Reason: population redistribution, standard tier. The incentives were a formality. The compliance was real. The destination was random — a name pulled from the pool of coastal garrison towns with available housing in the transmigrant district. That the name happened to be Kedros was nothing more or less than a coincidence, the kind of coincidence the census lottery produced every day without noticing and without caring.

Akahito didn't ask how the lottery had landed him here, of all places. He didn't ask whether the universe was capable of coincidence on this scale. He packed the stone, and his tools, and boarded the train.

### Scene 6: The Supermarket – Reprise

The can of condensed milk rolled under the register.

Asahi didn't pick it up. She was looking at him — at the boy standing three metres away in the produce aisle, holding a bag of potatoes in one hand and a slip of paper in the other, his shoulders still curved inward, his hands still stained with grease, his eyes exactly the same as they had been on the corner of the tram line six years ago.

Through the barred window behind him, the harbour fog was rolling in. The fishing boats had lit their running lamps — small orange pinpricks in the grey — and the pre-dreadnoughts were dark shapes against the water. Beyond them, on the horizon, [[The Celestial Tether|the Tether]]'s three silver ribbons were still visible, barely, catching the last of the sunset. The troop train was still rattling past, car after car, soldiers and supplies heading east toward a border that had been in dispute for three hundred years and would be in dispute for three hundred more, a ritual of posturing and preparedness that had never yet become a war but had never quite stopped threatening to.

None of this mattered.

The bag of potatoes slipped from Akahito's grip. It hit the floor — *thump* — and rolled to a stop against a display of canned tomatoes. He didn't notice. He was still looking at her, and she was still looking at him, and the space between them — three metres of cracked linoleum and flickering fluorescent light — felt like the width of the Tethyan and the length of six years and the distance between a precinct apartment with a blue door and a coastal town with bars on its windows.

"I didn't think you'd be sent too," she said.

Her voice was flat. She was proud of how flat it was. She had spent six years learning to sound like nothing could touch her — not the census, not the Tension, not the relocation order folded in her pocket, not the boy who had given her the best skipping stone she had ever held and who was now standing in her supermarket looking at her like she was a ghost he'd been chasing across two continents and three transmigration zones.

"*Sent*," Akahito said. It wasn't a question. It was the recognition of a word he understood — a word his father had used on the telephone, in hushed conversations that stopped when Akahito entered the room. *Sent. Relocated. Redistributed.* Valdran had a dozen words for the same thing, and they were all euphemisms for loss.

He took a step forward. Just one. He had always been careful about taking up space.

"The airfield," he said. "I work at the airfield. The mechanical workshop, on the headland. Tasos took me on two years ago — I fix the *Skylarks*, mostly. Engine overhauls. Oil leaks."

She nodded. She didn't know what to do with that information, but nodding was easier than speaking and speaking was harder than she'd expected. Her throat had closed around something she didn't want to name.

"I help the new families unpack," he said. "On weekends. When the transmigrant trains come in — the passenger service, the one with the empty coaches. I meet them at the depot. I carry the boxes. I show them where the water pump is, and the census office, and the supermarket." He paused. "I didn't know you worked here. I mean — I hoped. But I didn't know."

She nodded again.

"I looked for you," he said. "Every day. On every street. In every face."

The fluorescent tubes flickered. The train was still passing — she could feel the vibration in her teeth now, the deep bass rumble of cars carrying soldiers toward a border that had been quiet for six years and was overdue for trouble. The Tension was building. Everyone in town knew it. The garrison knew it. The census clerk knew it — she'd been drafting relocation orders all week, bumping transmigrant families up the queue, because if the Tension tipped into something real, the transmigrant district was the first place the Concordat would shell and the first place Valdris would evacuate.

"The Tension's overdue," Asahi said. "The border dispute — it was supposed to flare up last year. The garrison's been at double complement for three weeks. My father says the telegraph office hasn't been this busy since the Tension of '06."

"I know."

"My family's been flagged for secondary relocation. I have the order in my pocket."

The words landed between them like a stone dropped into still water. Akahito's expression didn't change — his face wasn't the kind of face that changed easily — but something shifted behind his eyes. A door closing. Or opening. Asahi couldn't tell which.

He was quiet for a long moment. Then he reached into his coat — a mechanic's coat, stained with grease and salt, the left pocket torn and badly stitched — and pulled out a small, flat object wrapped in a handkerchief.

A children's handkerchief. Faded blue. The colour of a precinct flag, the colour of a door that used to be open.

He unwrapped it.

"I kept it," he said.

The stone was black and smooth and slightly warm from his body. It was thinner than she remembered, worn down by the river and by six years in a boy's pocket and by everything that time does to objects carried close to the skin. It caught the fluorescent light the way it had caught the riverbank sun when she was nine and the world was still small enough to fit inside her hands.

Asahi looked at the stone. She looked at him — at the grease on his knuckles, the salt stains on his collar, the way he was standing with his shoulders still curved inward, still taking up less space than he needed, still waiting for her to catch up. She thought about the riverbank. She thought about the blue door. She thought about six years of not learning names, six years of wearing her grandmother's precinct pin under her collar, six years of watching [[The Celestial Tether|the Tether]] on the horizon and pretending it didn't make her feel like a child standing at the edge of something she couldn't cross.

"If you get relocated again," Akahito said, "I'll find you."

"How?"

"I don't know yet."

He said it the way he said everything — quietly, with the absolute certainty of someone who had decided something and would now spend the rest of his life following through. The way he'd decided to walk past her house every day for two years. The way he'd decided to learn every train schedule by heart. The way he'd decided — or the world had decided — to draw his number from the same lottery that had drawn hers, to send him to the same coastal garrison town at the edge of a border dispute that was older than either of them, because she was there, and that was enough.

The supermarket door chimed. A new customer — a garrison soldier in a harbour-patrol coat, still damp from the fog, his face carrying the particular exhaustion of a man who had been on elevated readiness for three weeks and hadn't slept well for any of them. He walked to the counter and set down a tin of meat and a packet of cigarettes and didn't look at either of them.

Asahi picked up the can of condensed milk from under the register. She set it on the counter. She looked at the stone in Akahito's hand, still wrapped in the blue handkerchief, still warm from his pocket, still hers.

"My shift ends at eight," she said.

He wrapped the stone again — carefully, the way you wrap something you've been keeping for six years and don't intend to lose now — and put it back in his pocket.

"I'll be outside," he said.

The soldier paid for his cigarettes and left. Outside, the harbour fog thickened around the breakwater, swallowing the fishing boats' running lamps one by one. The troop train's last cars rattled past. [[The Celestial Tether|The Tether]]'s three silver ribbons shimmered briefly before the fog obscured them completely — but they'd be back in the morning, Asahi knew. They always were. That was the thing about [[The Celestial Tether|the Tether]]: it never went anywhere. It just waited, patient and silver and utterly indifferent, the same way the census waited, the same way the Mandate waited, the same way the Five-Year Tensions came and went like weather that never quite broke into storm.

And in a supermarket in a coastal garrison town at the edge of a border dispute that had been going on for three centuries, a girl who had spent six years not planting roots watched the boy who had spent six years looking for her walk to the door, pause in the doorway with the harbour fog at his back, and look over his shoulder once — just once — before stepping outside to wait.

The train passed. The shelves settled. The refrigeration unit coughed its forty-seven-second cough.

From outside, through the barred window, came the low, mournful sound of a ship's horn — one of the pre-dreadnoughts, signalling its position in the fog. A sound Asahi had heard ten thousand times since she arrived in Kedros. A sound so familiar she'd stopped noticing it years ago.

She noticed it now.

She looked at the clock above the register.

Ninety-three minutes until eight.

## Key Decisions

- [[Asahi]] and [[Akahito]] reunite under the most ordinary and extraordinary circumstances — a troop train rattling the supermarket, a can falling, a gesture that bridges six years.
- Neither says "I missed you." Both say everything else.
- [[Akahito]] reveals he kept her skipping stone for six years, wrapped in his childhood handkerchief.
- [[Asahi]] reveals she's flagged for secondary relocation — the Five-Year Tension is overdue, and the census bureau is clearing the transmigrant district as a precaution.
- [[Akahito]]'s transmigration to [[Kedros Garrison]] was pure coincidence — the random lottery drew his family's number, and the destination pool assigned them to the same coastal town [[Asahi]] had been sent to six years earlier. No conspiracy. No corruption. The census doesn't play favourites, even in the dark.
- The backdrop — the [[The Celestial Tether|Celestial Tether]] on the horizon, the Five-Year Tension building, the garrison doubling, the harbour crowded with warships — is sensed entirely through the characters' eyes. The tension is atmospheric, not active.
- The [[Kaelen Mandate|Mandate's]] surveillance is implied by the world — the census, the relocation orders, the Tether — but no [[Silent Chorus|drones]] or [[The Observers|Observers]] appear. The Island is felt, not seen.
- [[Asahi]] wears her grandmother's precinct pin under her collar — a detail that will return.
- The chapter ends on a clock. Ninety-three minutes. The countdown has begun.
