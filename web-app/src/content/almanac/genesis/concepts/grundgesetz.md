# Grundgesetz

## Summary
The German constitution, drafted after the Second World War as a carefully constructed framework for liberal democracy. Its Articles 87a and 4(3) create a legal barrier that prevents Germany from committing troops to any operation related to the Anomaly without a lengthy and uncertain parliamentary process.

## Relevant Articles

**Article 87a(2)** — The Bundestag must approve every military deployment with a mandate specifying scope, duration, rules of engagement, and troop cap. No existing legal framework covers "contact with a parallel theocratic civilisation" or "defence against a non-territorial supernatural threat." A new mandate would require weeks of committee hearings, floor debate, and a majority vote. A constitutional amendment (requiring a two-thirds supermajority in both chambers) would be needed to create a new deployment category — a process that could take months and may fail.

**Article 4(3)** — Conscientious objection is absolute and non-waivable. Any soldier may refuse military service on grounds of conscience, and the state cannot compel, punish, or override that refusal. If the Bundeswehr were deployed to the portal zone, individual soldiers could refuse to participate — because they object to "holy war," because they doubt the threat is real, or because their church advises against it — and the constitution protects that refusal absolutely.

## Context

The Grundgesetz was written in reaction to the catastrophes of the Nazi era — the militarisation of society, the suspension of individual conscience, the subordination of law to executive decree. Every safeguard that makes German democracy resilient also makes it slow. The portal crisis, which demands speed and flexibility, encounters a system designed to prevent speed and flexibility in military matters. This is not a bug. It is the intended feature of a constitution built by people who had seen what happens when constitutions fail to constrain power.
