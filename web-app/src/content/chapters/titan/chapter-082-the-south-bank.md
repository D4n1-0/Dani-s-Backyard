# Chapter 08.2 – The South Bank

## Summary

The capital's telegraph arrives on Day 124: Magath is endorsed ex-post-facto. Not promoted. Not celebrated. But granted formal authority to negotiate on behalf of the Marleyan Empire. Ishihara returns to Odiha with a mandate from the Quadripartite Committee — a permanent military facility on Marleyan soil, a forward operating base on the AOT mainland. What follows is not a war council and not a surrender. It is an exchange — two powers measuring what they want against what they are willing to give. The negotiation takes two sessions across five days. By the end, CATF has a 99-year lease on the Odiha river mouth's south bank. Marley has technology access, a trade lane through the Gate, and CATF's recognition of its sovereignty. And the Paradis problem — acknowledged, not resolved — has been driven into the terms like a splinter that neither side can remove and neither side will name.

## Scenes

### Scene 1: The Telegram — Day 124

The telegraph machine in the Port Authority Building's ground-floor communications room was a Marleyan model, cast iron and brass, built thirty years earlier by a manufacturer that had since been absorbed into the naval supply directorate. It had never received a message that mattered — shipping manifests, weather reports, the routine traffic of a southern port that the empire had built and then forgotten. On the morning of Day 124, it received a message that mattered.

The operator was a young naval clerk named Keller — nineteen years old, three months into his posting at Odiha, his principal qualification being that he could type faster than the other clerks and did not ask questions about the messages he processed. He had been processing messages about the foreign ships at Pier 3 for four days now, and he had learned, in that time, to recognise the difference between a routine signal and a signal that needed to be carried upstairs at a run. This signal needed to be carried upstairs at a run.

[[Theo Magath|Magath]] was in [[Konrad Verner|Verner]]'s office when the clerk arrived — the same wood-panelled room with the same view of the harbour and the same three ships at Pier 3 that had not moved since they arrived. The clerk's face was flushed. His uniform was damp under the arms. He handed Magath the transcribed message with a hand that trembled slightly, and then he stood at attention against the doorframe, waiting to be dismissed, and Magath read the message while Verner watched from behind his desk.

The message was from [[Hoffmann|General [[Hoffmann]]]]. It was addressed to Commander Magath, personal and immediate. It ran to three paragraphs.

*Paragraph one:* The General Staff had reviewed Commander Magath's report from Odiha. The report's contents had been assessed against the available intelligence, including the crew debriefings and Admiral Verner's initial contact summary. The assessment was complete. The findings were accepted.

*Paragraph two:* Commander Magath's decision to answer the frequency was not authorised in advance. The General Staff noted that the commander had been given a brief to *assess and decide*, and that the decision to answer the frequency fell within the interpretive range of that brief. The General Staff endorsed the decision ex-post-facto. Commander Magath was hereby confirmed as the Marleyan Empire's authorised negotiating representative for all further contact with the Combined Anomaly Task Force.

*Paragraph three:* The General Staff expected that Commander Magath would exercise his authority with the same discretion he had demonstrated in his report. The empire was watching. The capital was watching. The general staff trusted that Commander Magath understood the weight of the responsibility he had accepted.

Magath read the message twice. The language was [[Hoffmann]]'s — formal, precise, constructed to give the General Staff maximum distance from whatever Magath agreed to while claiming credit if the agreement succeeded. The endorsement was real. The trust was conditional. Magath had been right about the empire's decision-making patterns for years, and the telegram confirmed every prediction he had made on the train south: the General Staff would endorse him because they needed someone to carry the burden, and the moment the burden grew too heavy, they would drop him.

"Admiral," Magath said. He handed the message to Verner. "The capital has responded."

Verner read it. Read it twice. The same three paragraphs, the same carefully distanced language, the same institutional impulse to delegate the risk while retaining the authority. He had been a flag officer for twenty years. He recognised the shape of the message. He had written messages like it himself.

"They endorsed you," Verner said.

"They endorsed the decision," Magath said. "Not me. The distinction is the difference between a promotion and a permission slip. I have been given permission to continue what I have already started. If it succeeds, I am a patriot. If it fails, I am a renegade. The general staff has arranged it so that either outcome reflects on me, not on them."

"And if it succeeds?"

"Then I have made the empire something it was not before I arrived. Something that can survive what is coming." Magath looked out the window. The white ship was still at Pier 3, her four-ring flag still limp in the harbour air, her gangway still lowered. "The diplomat will return. The Quadripartite Committee — I am learning their terminology — will want a formal presence on this side of the Gate. The channel we opened is a conversation. They will want to turn the conversation into a base."

"A base on our territory."

"Yes. The question is what we ask for in return." Magath turned from the window. "Admiral. I need to know everything you learned about this diplomat during the four days she was here. Not what you wrote in the report. What you observed. What you inferred. What she did not say. Everything."

Verner leaned back in his chair. His knee ached — the same dull ache that had been with him since the storm off Lakua, the same ache that reminded him, every time he stood, that he was older than the empire he served and wiser than the men who commanded it. He thought about the woman in the dark blue coat. The northern-province accent. The way she had walked the length of the pier alone. The way she had placed a document on his desk and asked for nothing.

"She is exactly what she appears to be," Verner said. "That is the most dangerous thing about her. Diplomats lie as a profession. She did not lie. She told me the truth — the Gate, the coalition, the desire to talk rather than fight — and she told it with the conviction of someone who knows the truth is more powerful than any lie she could construct. She is not afraid of us. She is afraid of what happens if this channel closes. That fear is real. It is the only thing she asked for — not our resources, not our allegiance, not our submission. Our conversation."

"The coalition has already contacted Paradis," Magath said. It was not a question. He had been assembling the evidence since he read Haas's account of the captured Titan — the six-metre Pure Titan, captured by CATF Marines on Paradis Island, fifteen thousand kilometres north of Grande Terre. A power that had captured a Titan on Paradis had already made contact with Paradis. The inference was unavoidable.

Verner was silent for a moment. Then he nodded. "I suspected it. The Titan in Haas's report — they did not capture that by accident. They went to Paradis, took what they wanted, and left. I did not raise it because I did not want to force the diplomat into a lie. If she had lied, the trust would have broken. If she had told the truth, I would have been obliged to report it. I chose not to force the question."

"You bought time."

"I bought trust. She will tell us when she is ready, or we will ask when we are positioned to receive the answer. That moment — " Verner looked at the white ship, " — is approaching."

"Then we need to be ready for the answer," Magath said. "What do we ask for?"

Verner thought about it. The ache in his knee. The ships at Pier 3. The empire that had sent him south thirty years ago and forgotten he was here, and was now, for the first time in thirty years, paying attention.

"We ask for what the empire cannot build itself," he said. "Medicine. Radio. Advice. The things that will let us fight our own wars without depending on children in armbands. We ask for enough to survive without asking for so much that we become dependent. We ask for a relationship that does not end with us as the junior partner in someone else's empire."

"And the trade," Magath said. "If the coalition is trading with Paradis — and they are — then the empire needs a share of the exchange. Not protection money. Not tribute. Commerce. A lane through the Gate. Goods from their world to ours. Including weapons. If we do not have weapons that can match theirs, we cannot enforce our sovereignty. And sovereignty is the only thing we have to offer them in return."

Verner looked up at him. "You are suggesting we trade land for weapons."

"I am suggesting we trade land for the things that make land defensible. The coalition wants a base on our southern coast. We give them a base. In return, we get medicine so our soldiers stop dying of infections. Radio so our fleets can communicate beyond line of sight. Weapons so our infantry can fight without waiting for a Shifter to arrive. And a trade compact that guarantees us a share of whatever passes through the Gate — not as charity, but as commerce. Marley is not a beggar. Marley is a partner. The terms reflect that, or there are no terms."

"And Paradis?"

"Paradis is a separate conversation. We ask the question directly. If she answers honestly — " Magath paused, " — we accept the answer, whatever it is, and we do not let it close the door. The door is too valuable to close over a question whose answer we already know."

Verner was silent for a long moment. The fire in the grate had burned down. Outside the window, the harbour was grey and still. The signal tower's beacon had stopped its rotation — the harbour master had ordered it dimmed at night, a security measure that had been instituted since the foreign ships arrived, a measure that no one in the city understood but everyone accepted.

"You are planning a negotiation," Verner said, "that will define the empire's relationship with another world for the next century. And you are doing it from my office, with a telegraph that took four days to deliver a response, without a legal advisor, without a trade attaché, without any of the infrastructure that a negotiation of this magnitude would require in the capital."

"Yes."

"Then let us prepare. The diplomat will be back within the week. We have until then to decide what we are willing to give and what we are not." Verner stood — the cane taking his weight, the knee protesting, the same knee, the same protest, the same empire. "Commander. I have been sitting in this office for four years waiting for something that mattered. It has arrived. Let us not waste it."

### Scene 2: The Return — Day ~128

The [[CATF Envoy|Envoy]] tied up at Pier 3 for the second time under a grey autumn sky, and the gate was open.

[[Satomi Ishihara|Ishihara]] stood on the bridge wing and watched the harbour resolve. The signal tower. The Port Authority Building. The mountains to the north. The river mouth to the south — broad and grey, the water moving slow and deep toward the sea. The pier where she had walked alone a week ago, the iron gate that Verner had opened for her, the stone buildings of Odiha climbing the lower slopes of the mountains. It was all the same harbour. It was a different harbour entirely.

The gate was open. Two guards stood at the posts — not eight, not rifles at rest, not the posture of soldiers expecting a threat. The containment was over. The Pelikan crew had been moved ashore — debriefed, reunited with their families, processed back into the empire's administrative machinery. The wooden bollards where the white ship had tied up a week ago were empty, waiting. The harbour master had been notified of their arrival two hours in advance by radio — the same frequency that Magath had accepted, the same channel that was now an active, functioning conduit between two worlds.

[[Theo Magath|Magath]] was waiting at the pier gate. Not Verner this time — the admiral was in his office, watching from the window, a glass of something amber at his elbow and the telegraph transcript on his desk. Magath was the face of the empire's response. He stood alone, hands at his sides, no cane, no limp, the posture of a soldier who was not trying to intimidate and was not trying to placate. He was just — waiting.

Ishihara walked down the gangway. She had chosen to walk alone again — [[Takeda Kaito|Takeda]] would follow with the documents, [[Seo Min-ah|Seo]] would attend the negotiation sessions, but the first impression would be made in the first thirty seconds, and she wanted Magath to see her the way Verner had seen her: a woman in a dark blue coat approaching without protection, trusting the host to behave. The calculation was the same. The counterpart was different.

She stopped at the gate. Magath looked at her. She looked at Magath. The eye contact was longer than it had been with Verner — not hostile, not warm, but measuring, the way two professionals measured each other across a table that had not yet been set.

"Commander Magath," she said in Marleyan. "We spoke on the telephone. I am glad to meet you in person."

"Dr. Ishihara. I have read your document. I have spoken with your deputy. I have watched your demonstration. And I have spent the past four days preparing for this conversation." Magath stepped aside — not retreating, but yielding the space beside the gate. "Welcome back to Odiha. The gate is open. The harbour is yours. Shall we begin?"

"I would like to see the admiral first. He contained the situation when protocol did not exist. I owe him a personal greeting."

Magath nodded. The request was not unexpected. It was, he thought, exactly what a diplomat who understood the weight of gratitude would ask. "The admiral is in his office. He has been expecting you. I will walk with you."

They walked together — the commander and the diplomat, past the gate, past the guards, up the cobbled street toward the Port Authority Building. The city was quieter than it had been a week ago — not the quiet of a town under siege, but the quiet of a town that had grown accustomed to the presence of something unprecedented and had stopped staring. The dock workers went about their business. The fish sellers argued about prices. The children chased chickens through the narrow streets. The white ship at Pier 3 was becoming — not normal, not ordinary, but *present*, a fact that had been absorbed into the daily texture of the city the way a new building was absorbed, the way a new ship in the harbour was absorbed, the way anything unprecedented became, over time, just another part of the world.

Verner was waiting in the doorway of the Port Authority Building. His cane was in his hand. His uniform was pressed. His expression was the expression of a man who had been managing an impossible situation and was now watching it evolve into something manageable — and was not sure whether to be relieved or alarmed.

"Dr. Ishihara. Welcome back."

"Admiral. Thank you. For the containment. For the conversation. For the fact that when I returned, the gate was open."

"The gate is open because you did not try to force it," Verner said. "You waited. The waiting was the message. I received it."

Ishihara looked at him — the man who had signed the Pelikan's departure orders and its condolence letters, who had confined her to a stone pier and then opened the gate, who had written a report that had split the General Staff and sent a commander south on a train. He was not a friend. He was not an ally. He was something rarer: a counterpart who had done his job, in the absence of protocol, and had done it well.

"Then let us continue," she said. "I have a mandate from my coalition. Commander Magath has his endorsement from your capital. We have a channel. We have trust — enough to begin. What we do not have is time. The longer the Gate is open without a formal presence on this side, the more unstable our position becomes. My coalition wants a base on Marleyan soil. I am here to negotiate the terms."

She had said it in the doorway of the Port Authority Building, in the hearing of the guards at the gate and the clerks at the windows, and the words landed in the harbour air like a stone dropped into still water.

Magath looked at her. The diplomat who had walked into Verner's office a week ago and offered a radio frequency was now, a week later, standing in the same doorway and asking for territory.

"You have been planning this," he said.

"Since the Pelikan was intercepted," Ishihara said. "The long-term mission was always to establish a forward presence on the AOT mainland. The Odiha contact was the first step. This — " she gestured at the harbour, the city, the open gate, " — is the second."

"Then let us not waste the open gate," Magath said. He gestured toward the Port Authority Building. "The admiral's office is this way. We have terms to discuss."

### Scene 3: The Negotiation — Days 129–133

The negotiation took two sessions across five days. The room was Verner's office — the same wood-panelled corner room, the same desk, the same photograph of the woman who had been dead for twelve years. But the arrangement was different. A second desk had been brought in — a folding table, set against the far wall, for the documents, the maps, the reference materials that would accumulate across the sessions. Chairs had been placed in a semicircle facing the admiral's desk — not a conference table, not a war council, something closer to a discussion among equals who were still working out whether they were equals.

The first session convened on Day 129. The participants were four: Ishihara and [[Takeda Kaito|Takeda]] for CATF; Magath and Verner for Marley. [[Seo Min-ah|Seo]] sat behind Ishihara, notebook open, her presence insurance against linguistic failure that was never needed — Ishihara's Marleyan had improved noticeably since her return, the vocabulary richer, the accent more fluid, the result of days spent in conversation with Admiral Verner during her first visit and the long debrief at Grande Terre with the linguists and cultural advisors who had been preparing for this moment since the channel opened. The tea was the same Marleyan brew. The fire in the grate was the same fire.

The terms were established in the first hour. CATF wanted a base — a permanent, defensible facility on Marleyan soil, with deep-water access for logistics vessels, a landing area for aircraft, and enough land to accommodate a compound of approximately two hundred personnel with room to expand. Ishihara placed a map on the admiral's desk. The map was a satellite image — printed on glossy paper, the resolution too fine for any Marleyan camera to produce, the topography rendered in false colour with elevation contours that showed every metre of the terrain.

"The south bank of the Odiha river mouth," she said. "Approximately twenty kilometres of coastline, extending two kilometres inland to the foothills. The land is undeveloped. No villages. No infrastructure. The river provides fresh water and inland access. The mountains to the north provide a natural defensive barrier. The deep-water channel allows logistics vessels to dock without dredging. The location is twenty kilometres from Odiha's city centre — close enough for personnel to access the port, far enough that a compound does not impose on civilian life."

Magath studied the map. He had studied maps his entire career — terrain maps for artillery positioning, naval charts for coastal defence, tactical maps for infantry deployment. He had never studied a map that had been produced by a machine in another world. The precision of it — the contours, the elevation markers, the river depth measured in metres to the decimal point — was a statement. The coalition had surveyed this coastline before the Pelikan had ever returned to port. The coalition had known what it wanted before the first conversation had begun.

"You are not asking for land," he said. "You are asking for a lease. Terms?"

"Ninety-nine years, renewable. A single compound with a defined perimeter, not a territorial exclave. Marleyan law applies within the compound perimeter unless specifically waived by mutual agreement. The compound is sovereign Marleyan territory — the coalition does not claim sovereignty, does not plant a flag, does not treat the site as an embassy or diplomatic mission. It is a leased facility on Marleyan soil, under Marleyan jurisdiction, operated by CATF personnel for logistics, military operations, science, and diplomacy."

"Military operations," Magath repeated. "In our territory. What kind of military operations."

"A forward operating base," Ishihara said. "Not an invasion force. The compound will host a security detachment, a military advisory cell, and command-and-control infrastructure for CATF regional operations. The personnel numbers and equipment levels are negotiable — we will define them in an annex to the lease. But to be clear: the compound is a military installation. It will fly the CATF flag. It will be armed. It will support military activities in Marleyan and international waters. We are not disguising it as a research station. Marley knows what it is granting, and we know what we are receiving."

Magath looked at Verner. Verner looked at the map. The terms were generous — more generous than the empire would have offered to a foreign power arriving in its port without precedent. The coalition was not asking for sovereignty. It was asking for a lease — a commercial arrangement, the kind of arrangement that Marley had granted to Hizuru for the Azumabito hangar, the kind of arrangement that nations made when they wanted something from each other and trusted the other side to honour the terms.

"The terms are acceptable in principle," Magath said. "Now. What are you offering in return?"

Ishihara opened a second folder. The document inside was a list — typed, single-spaced, the CATF insignia at the top of the page. She had prepared this on the voyage south, consulting with the Quadripartite Committee via encrypted radio, balancing the committee's strategic priorities against the political reality of what Marley would accept. The list was not a demand. It was an opening position — the first draft of an exchange.

"The coalition offers three categories of return," she said. "One: technology access. Medical supplies and equipment — morphine, antibiotics, antiseptics, surgical tools, basic diagnostic equipment. Radio communications — a relay station at the compound, capable of linking your naval command to your southern fleet in real time. A military advisory cell — liaison officers from the CATF nations, stationed at the compound, available to consult on conventional military modernisation. Not Titans. Not Shifters. The tools that win wars when Titans are not available."

"Two: trade. A standing compact that grants the Marleyan Empire a share of Gate traffic — goods and resources passing between our worlds. Marleyan merchants will have access to Earth markets under CATF oversight. Earth goods will be available to Marleyan purchasers at negotiated rates. The compact includes a weapons procurement agreement — Marley may purchase small arms, ammunition, and light military equipment from CATF nations at values to be negotiated. No Titans. No weapons of mass destruction. No chemical or biological agents. Conventional arms only."

"Three: recognition. The Combined Anomaly Task Force formally recognises the Marleyan Empire's sovereignty over its claimed territory and waters. The Gate is not a territorial claim. The south bank facility is not a territorial claim. Our presence on this coast is by your permission, and we acknowledge that. The coalition does not contest Marleyan borders. The coalition does not support insurgencies within Marleyan territory. The coalition respects the legal and political authority of the Marleyan government and its designated representatives."

The words sat in the room. The fire crackled. The adjutant's pen — Drexler, still standing by the door, still recording everything — had stopped moving. He had heard the diplomat offer the empire medicine, radio, trade, weapons, and recognition — a package that would take the Marleyan General Staff a generation to digest and that Commander Magath was being asked to accept in an afternoon.

Magath looked at the list. Then he looked at Ishihara. The diplomat in the dark blue coat. The woman who had walked into Verner's office a week ago and offered a radio frequency and nothing more. She was now offering the empire the tools to rebuild its military doctrine from the foundation up.

"You are offering us weapons," he said.

"I am offering you the ability to defend your sovereignty without relying on children in armbands," Ishihara said. "The Titan programme is a wasting asset. You know this. I know this. Your General Staff knows this and has been refusing to act on it for a decade. The weapons are not a gift. They are a purchase — Marley buys them through the trade compact, at negotiated rates, under CATF oversight. But they are available. And the availability of conventional weapons at scale means the empire can begin to transition away from Titan dependency. That transition is the single most important strategic shift your nation will make in the next fifty years. We are offering to accelerate it."

Verner looked at Magath. The commander's face was unreadable — the face of a man who was processing an offer that was too generous and too calculated to be naive. The coalition was not being generous out of altruism. The coalition was being generous because a Marley that was strong conventionally was a Marley that did not need to start wars with Titans. A Marley that did not start wars with Titans was a Marley that did not threaten to collapse the political order of the AOT world. And a stable AOT world was the coalition's principal strategic objective.

"There is one more thing," Ishihara said. "It is not in the document. It is a question. I believe Commander Magath already knows the answer. But I will state it directly so that there is no ambiguity between us." She paused. "The Combined Anomaly Task Force has contacted Paradis."

The silence that followed was the longest silence of the session.

"We have established a diplomatic channel with the Eldian government. We have exchanged liaison officers. We have provided medical and material aid. We have not provided weapons. We have not formed a military alliance. The relationship is independent of the relationship we are establishing with Marley. The two channels do not intersect. They are not conditional on each other. They are separate negotiations with separate objectives."

Magath waited. Verner waited. The adjutant's pen hovered above the page — not writing, not moving, suspended in the moment of revelation.

"I am telling you this now," Ishihara continued, "because you would have discovered it eventually. I prefer the trust of disclosure over the suspicion of discovery. But I am also telling you that the details of our Paradisian engagement are not open to negotiation. We will not disclose our plans with Paradis. We will not restrict our Paradisian engagement as a condition of this agreement. The terms we have offered — technology, trade, recognition — are the terms. They are not contingent on Marleyan approval of our other relationships. We are negotiating a bilateral compact. Paradis is not a party to it."

The adjutant's pen resumed writing. The words were careful — *Dr. Ishihara: CATF has contacted Paradis. Will not disclose plans. Will not accept Paradis-related conditions in bilateral negotiations.* The adjutant was a naval officer. He understood the significance of what he was writing. He kept writing anyway.

Magath did not look at Verner. He had been expecting this — not the precise moment, not the precise phrasing, but the fact itself. Since Haas's account of the Titan. Since the GPR survey of the Walls. Since the dawning realisation that a coalition that could capture a Titan on Paradis had already made contact with Paradis. The question was not whether CATF was talking to Paradis. The question was what Marley would do about it.

"If Paradis is not a condition," Magath said, "and our compact is not conditioned on Paradisian engagement, then we accept your disclosure as a statement of fact. We record it. We note that the empire regards the Paradisian government as a hostile entity and that any material support CATF provides to Paradis is recorded as a concern — not a breach, but a concern. We will raise the concern through this channel when the concern warrants it. Is that acceptable?"

"Acceptable."

"There is one adjustment to the trade terms," Magath said. "You offered a weapons procurement agreement. We accept it. But the share of Gate trade — the percentage Marley receives — will be higher than the initial proposal. If the coalition is trading with Paradis and trading with us, and we are the nation that has granted you territory on our soil, then the empire's share reflects that asymmetry. We are not asking for a veto over your Paradisian relationship. We are asking for acknowledgement that our cooperation comes with a price — and the price is a larger cut of the exchange." He named a figure. Ishihara considered it. The figure was higher than her opening position but lower than the maximum her committee had authorised. She had room to accept.

"Agreed," she said.

The first session ended at 14:00. The second session convened on Day 133 to finalise the details — the compound's perimeter, the security complement, the number of CATF personnel authorised to reside on the south bank, the timeline for construction, the terms of the technology transfer (medical first, radio second, advisory cell third). The weapons procurement schedule was drafted in an annex — small arms, ammunition, communications equipment, transport vehicles, nothing heavier than a crew-served weapon, nothing that could be turned against civilian populations without violating the compact's non-aggression clause. The Marleyan General Staff would review the annex. The Quadripartite Committee would review the whole document. The signatures would come next.

But the terms were established. The compact was real. And as the second session adjourned and the participants filed out of Verner's office, Ishihara stopped at the window and looked south — past the signal tower, past the breakwater, past the river mouth, toward the flat grassy stretch of coastline where the first CATF compound on AOT mainland soil would be built.

She had asked for a door, and the door had opened. Now she was asking for a house, and the house was being built. The transition from contact to presence was the hardest part of diplomacy. She was halfway through it.

### Scene 4: The South Bank — Day ~134

The survey boat was a Marleyan naval launch — a flat-bottomed steam tender, twelve metres, the kind of vessel that ferried supplies between the port and the warships at anchor. It was crewed by two sailors who had been told they were transporting a survey party and had not been told what the survey was for. The river mouth opened before them at 10:00 — a broad channel of grey water where the Odiha River emptied into the harbour, the current slow and deep and navigable for vessels with a draft of five metres or less.

Ishihara stood at the bow. [[Takeda Kaito|Takeda]] stood beside her, a tablet in his hand, the satellite imagery open alongside a live feed from a small drone that a CATF technician was piloting from the launch's stern. Magath stood behind them, his arms crossed, his posture the posture of a man who was observing a process he understood and was reserving final judgement until the process was complete. Verner had remained at the port — his knee would not tolerate a boat journey, and his presence was not needed. The survey was a verification exercise. The deal had already been struck.

The south bank came into view. It was a flat, grassy plain extending inland from the river's edge to the foothills of the mountains — approximately two kilometres of level terrain, well-drained, the soil dark and fertile but never farmed, the river providing a natural boundary on three sides and the foothills forming a natural defensive arc to the south. The deep-water channel ran close to the bank — a logistics vessel could anchor within fifty metres of shore and unload via ramp. The mountains to the north provided shelter from the prevailing winds. The distance from Odiha's city centre was approximately twenty kilometres by road — a distance that could be covered by a vehicle in thirty minutes, by a helicopter in five.

Ishihara had studied this site on satellite imagery for weeks before the contact mission had launched. She had reviewed the bathymetric data, the soil surveys, the climate models, the line-of-sight calculations for radio and radar coverage. She had briefed the Quadripartite Committee on the south bank's suitability as a CATF forward base — deep-water access, flat terrain, freshwater supply, defensible perimeter, proximity to a friendly port with existing infrastructure. Everything she had told Magath in the negotiation was true. And now she was standing on a boat, looking at the physical reality of the site she had described, and the physical reality was better than the imagery.

The boat nosed into the bank. The sailors extended a gangplank. Ishihara stepped onto the grass — the first CATF diplomat to set foot on the land that would become the coalition's permanent foothold on the AOT mainland. The grass was damp with morning dew. The air smelled of river water and mountain pine and the faint salt of the harbour a short distance north. The ground was solid under her boots — good drainage, stable foundation for prefab construction.

[[Takeda Kaito|Takeda]] followed, his tablet updating the site map in real time. "The survey data confirms the satellite imagery. The soil bearing capacity is adequate for foundation loads. The deep-water channel is within fifty metres of the bank as predicted. The drone is mapping the perimeter now. We'll have a full site plan by the end of the day."

Ishihara walked twenty paces inland. The grass was waist-high — wild, uncultivated, the kind of grass that grew on land that had never been farmed because no one had needed to farm here. The river curved gently to the south, forming a natural peninsula that would serve as the compound's eastern boundary. The mountains rose to the north — grey peaks, snow-capped, the same mountains that Verner had looked at from his office window every day for four years.

She turned back toward the boat. Magath had not left the launch. He was standing at the stern, watching her the way he had watched the helicopter — stationary, hands at his sides, processing evidence that no briefing could convey. She walked back across the gangplank and stood before him.

"Commander. This is the site. The compound will be built here — a pier at the riverbank, a helipad on the flat ground, prefab buildings for personnel and operations, a radio tower for communications relay, a medical clinic for CATF personnel and, by arrangement, for Marleyan civilians in the Odiha district. The perimeter will be fenced but not walled. The gate will face the internal road that connects to Odiha's port. The flagpole will fly the CATF insignia alongside the Marleyan ensign — two flags on one pole, a visual statement that this land is Marleyan territory and the facility on it is operated by the coalition with Marleyan consent."

Magath looked at her. The diplomat in the dark blue coat. The woman who had walked into Verner's office a week ago and offered a radio frequency, and was now standing on the riverbank describing a future that the empire had not imagined. She had planned all of this — the compound, the pier, the clinic, the radio tower, the flags. She had planned it before the Pelikan had ever returned to port, before Verner had ever opened the gate, before Magath had ever boarded a train south. She had planned it because she knew that the first contact was not the end of the conversation. It was the beginning. And the beginning required infrastructure.

"How long until construction begins?" he asked.

"The Quadripartite Committee will authorise the project within two weeks of my return. The first construction vessel will arrive within four weeks. The pier and helipad will be operational within six weeks. Full compound occupancy within twelve weeks. Two hundred personnel — logistics, engineering, medical, diplomatic, and a military operations element. The force cap is in the annex. The terms we negotiated."

"Twelve weeks," Magath said. It was not a question. It was a measurement — the length of time between the signing of a memorandum and the appearance of a foreign base on the southern coast of the empire. Twelve weeks was not a long time in the lifespan of a nation. It was an eternity in the lifespan of a decision that the General Staff had not yet fully accepted.

"Yes," Ishihara said. "Commander Magath. You have endorsed the decision to talk. The capital has endorsed you. The terms have been agreed. The site has been surveyed. What remains is the signature. And then — " she looked across the river, at the flat grassland that would become the first CATF compound on AOT soil, " — then we build."

### Scene 5: The Memorandum — Day ~138

The signing took place in Verner's office. Four people present: Ishihara and Magath at the desk, Verner and Takeda as witnesses, Drexler recording. The document — the Memorandum of Understanding between the Marleyan Empire and the Combined Anomaly Task Force — was five pages. It had been drafted over three days, revised twice by Ishihara on the Envoy, transmitted to Grande Terre for committee review, transmitted back with annotations, and printed on the Envoy's single office printer on paper that was whiter than any paper produced in the empire.

The articles were numbered. The language was clinical. The weight of the document was not in its prose but in its existence — the first bilateral agreement between a nation of the AOT world and the coalition that had arrived through the Gate.

Article One: The south bank lease. Ninety-nine years, renewable. A perimeter of approximately forty square kilometres — twenty kilometres of coastline extending two kilometres inland. Marleyan sovereignty maintained; CATF operational authority within the perimeter. The compound is not a diplomatic mission. It is a leased facility under Marleyan jurisdiction.

Article Two: Technology transfer. Medical supplies and equipment first — morphine, antibiotics, antiseptics, surgical tools, basic diagnostic devices. Radio communications second — a relay station at the compound, capable of linking the Marleyan naval command network. Military advisory cell third — CATF liaison officers available for consultation on conventional military modernisation. No weapons in the technology transfer package. The weapons come through the trade compact, not the lease.

Article Three: Trade compact. Marleyan merchants authorised to access Gate trade lanes under CATF oversight. CATF goods available to Marleyan purchasers at negotiated rates. A standing weapons procurement agreement — small arms, ammunition, communications equipment, transport vehicles. CATF nations reserve the right to decline specific sales on grounds of strategic sensitivity. Marley reserves the right to purchase from any CATF nation subject to that nation's export laws.

Article Four: Recognition. CATF formally recognises Marleyan sovereignty over its claimed territory and waters. The south bank facility is not a territorial claim. The Gate is not a territorial claim. CATF does not contest Marleyan borders or support insurgencies within Marleyan territory.

Article Five: The Paradis annex — verbal, unwritten, recorded in the session minutes but not in the signed text. CATF's engagement with Paradis is independent, ongoing, and not subject to Marleyan condition. Marley records the engagement as a concern and reserves the right to raise the concern through the channel established by this memorandum.

Ishihara signed first. She used a pen from her own coat — a fountain pen, Japanese manufacture, the same pen she had used to take notes during her first conversation with Verner a week and a half ago. Her signature was small and precise. The characters were Latin script — S. Ishihara, Chief of Mission, Combined Anomaly Task Force.

She slid the document across the desk to Magath.

He picked up Verner's pen — the same pen that had signed the departure orders for the Pelikan and the condolence letters for its crew, the same pen that had written the report to the capital, the same pen that had drafted the first line of the assessment that had claimed personal responsibility. He signed his name. T. Magath — Commander, Imperial Marleyan Army, Authorised Representative of the General Staff.

The pen touched the paper. The ink dried. The document was signed.

Verner poured four glasses of something amber — the same bottle that had been at his elbow for a week, the same liquor that he rationed because the southern command's supplies were limited and the capital rarely replenished them. He raised his glass.

"To the door," he said. "Which is now open from both sides."

They drank. The liquor burned — good Marleyan whiskey, aged in oak casks that had been imported from the interior, the kind of whiskey that naval officers drank when they signed things that mattered. Ishihara drank hers without flinching. Magath drank his without ceremony. Takeda took a sip and set the glass down — the deputy protocol officer, whose job had been to coordinate and document and not interfere, had done his job.

Ishihara folded her copy of the memorandum. She placed it in her coat — the same dark blue coat, the same pocket that had carried the first document she had offered Verner a week and a half ago. The first document had been a single page. It had contained a radio frequency and a statement of intent and nothing more. The second document was five pages. It contained a lease, a technology package, a trade compact, a sovereignty clause, and an unspoken agreement about Paradis that both sides had chosen not to write down.

"Commander," she said. "Admiral. My delegation will depart Odiha within forty-eight hours. The south bank compound will be operational within twelve weeks of the Quadripartite Committee's authorisation. Until then — the channel is open. The gate is open. The door you opened a week ago remains open. And the woman who walked through it first — " she paused, giving the words their full weight, " — thanks you both for letting her in."

She walked out of the office. Takeda followed — his tablet under his arm, his expression the expression of a man who had witnessed history and was still processing it. The door closed behind them. The room was quiet. The fire in the grate had burned down. The harbour outside the window was grey and still.

Magath looked at the document on the desk. Five pages. His signature on the last one — the third time his name had appeared on a piece of paper that would define his career, after the warning memo about Paradis nine months ago, after the telegram assessment six days ago. The first had been ignored. The second had been accepted. The third was his.

"Admiral. What happens now."

Verner looked out the window. The white ship was still at Pier 3 — but not for much longer. The diplomat would board it within two days. The ship would steam south toward the Gate, toward Grande Terre, toward the world that had sent it. Behind it, on the south bank of the Odiha river mouth, the first construction vessel would arrive within four weeks. The pier would go in. The helipad would follow. The prefab buildings would rise on the grassland. The flagpole would go up — the CATF insignia and the Marleyan ensign, two flags on one pole, the visual statement of a compact that had been signed in an admiral's office by a commander and a diplomat who had never expected to be in the same room.

"Now," Verner said, "we build a relationship on top of a memorandum. The memorandum is five pages. The relationship will be a thousand. And every page will be harder than the ones we just signed."

"The Paradis question will not stay buried."

"No. It will surface the first time a Marleyan patrol encounters a CATF ship on its way to Paradisian waters. It will surface the first time a Marleyan intelligence report mentions that the coalition has provided Paradis with medicine, or food, or infrastructure. It will surface the first time the General Staff demands to know why we accepted a compact that does not prevent the coalition from arming our enemy." Verner turned from the window. "And when it surfaces, you and I will be the ones who answer for it. The memorandum is a door. The door is open. What walks through it next — that is the part we have not yet decided."

Magath looked at the document. The signature. His name. The date — Day 138, Year 850 of the Marleyan calendar, approximately four months since a spatial anomaly had opened in the southern ocean and changed the shape of the world.

"Then let us be ready," he said, "for what walks through it next."

## Key Decisions

- The General Staff endorses Magath ex-post-facto — formal authority to negotiate, but responsibility stays on him
- Ishihara returns to Odiha with a Quad Committee mandate for a permanent CATF presence on the AOT mainland
- The south bank of the Odiha river mouth is designated as the site — twenty kilometres of flat, undeveloped coast, deep-water access, defensible perimeter
- Two negotiation sessions across five days establish the terms:
  - CATF gets a 99-year renewable lease on the south bank
  - Marley gets technology transfer (medicine → radio → advisory cell), a trade compact including weapons procurement, and CATF recognition of Marleyan sovereignty
- The Paradis question is addressed directly: Ishihara discloses the contact, refuses to discuss details, and establishes that Paradis engagement is independent and not subject to Marleyan conditions
- Magath accepts the disclosure and adjusts Marley's position: a larger trade share compensates for the Paradis asymmetry
- The MOU is signed in Verner's office on Day ~138 — five pages, the first bilateral agreement between an AOT power and CATF
- The south bank compound is scheduled for construction: pier and helipad operational within six weeks, full occupancy within twelve weeks
