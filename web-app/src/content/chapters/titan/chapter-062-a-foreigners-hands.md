# Chapter 06.2 – A Foreigner's Hands

## Summary

Connie Springer boards a flying machine on a dark Paradisian field and does not come down again until the ocean has changed continents beneath him. He has never been on a ship that hums. He has never pushed a button that made water appear from a wall. He has never met an Eldian who grew up outside the Walls. Over the next week — four days at sea, three days on a subantarctic base built from shipping containers — Connie learns what happens when a soldier from a nineteenth-century island walks through a door into the twenty-first century. He is given a thermal jacket and a wrist-worn device that counts his heartbeat. He is asked questions by an intelligence officer who studies him like a map. He stands in front of a chained Titan and asks the only question that matters. And at night, in the common room, he drinks tea with a woman from Liberio who tells him he has never been free — but neither had she, until someone handed her a wrench and told her to work.

## Scenes

### Scene 1: The Helicopter Deck — Night of Day 104

The machine lifted off the grassland with a noise that [[Connie Springer|Connie]] felt in his ribs before he heard it in his ears.

He was strapped into a canvas seat in a metal cabin, his knees pressed against a bulkhead that vibrated like a living thing. Across from him, [[Elias Vogler|Dr. Vogler]] was saying something — his mouth was moving, the shapes of Eldian words visible on his lips — but the rotor noise swallowed everything. [[Connie Springer|Connie]] nodded as though he understood. He did not understand. He had never been inside a machine that flew, and his body had not yet decided whether this was a miracle or a fall.

The cabin had windows. He looked out of one. The grassland was gone. The dark ocean had replaced it — a sheet of black water stretching to a horizon that was indistinguishable from the sky. Somewhere below, the ten soldiers he had ridden with were already turning their horses west toward the Walls. He was the only Paradisian in the air.

The helicopter banked. [[Connie Springer|Connie]]'s stomach banked half a second later. He gripped the edge of his seat and decided — with the specific clarity of a soldier who had made worse decisions in worse places — that he would not be sick. Not in front of the men from another world.

The ship appeared below them after what felt like an hour and was probably twelve minutes.

It was a grey shape on the grey water, studded with lights — red, green, white, a constellation of running lamps that marked its outline against the dark. The helicopter descended toward it, and [[Connie Springer|Connie]] watched the ship grow from a toy into a vessel the size of a Marleyan cruiser, and then into something larger than any ship he had ever seen. The deck was metal. The superstructure was metal. The people standing on the deck, waiting for the helicopter to land, were dressed in uniforms that did not belong to any nation on his Earth.

The helicopter touched down. The rotors slowed. The noise dropped from a roar to a whine to the ticking of cooling metal. [[Elias Vogler|Vogler]] unstrapped himself and gestured toward the door.

"Private Springer," he said, in the careful Eldian that had become the common language of the rendezvous. "Welcome to the R-V Isabu. It is a Korean ship. A research vessel. You are the first Paradisian to set foot on it."

[[Connie Springer|Connie]] unbuckled his harness. His legs were unsteady. He stepped out of the helicopter onto the metal deck of a ship that hummed, and the first thing he noticed was not the size or the lights or the people in strange uniforms. It was the air. It was cold — colder than Paradis, colder than the grassland, a dry Antarctic cold that bit at his cheeks and made his breath visible in the deck lights. He had not known the world could be this cold.

A young woman in a dark uniform was walking toward him. She had dark hair pulled back, and she was carrying a jacket — a thick, insulated thing with a lining that reflected the deck lights. She held it out.

"Connie, right?" she said. Her Eldian was functional, accented but clear, the accent of someone who had learned the language recently and was still surprised to be speaking it. "I'm Ensign DeSilva. Put this on. You'll freeze otherwise."

[[Connie Springer|Connie]] took the jacket. The fabric was lighter than wool but warmer than anything he had ever worn. He pulled it on, and the cold retreated.

"Thank you," he said.

"Don't thank me yet. You haven't seen the shower."

### Scene 2: The Room on the Ship — Day 104–105

The dorm room was small — two cots, two metal lockers, a porthole that showed only darkness — but it was the most alien space [[Connie Springer|Connie]] had ever occupied. The walls were painted grey. The lights were not flame — they were bars of white glow set into the ceiling, activated by a switch on the wall that DeSilva pressed with her thumb. A switch. No oil. No wick. Just a plastic rectangle that made light appear.

"The lights," DeSilva said. She pressed the switch again. The lights went out. She pressed it again. The lights came back. "On. Off. You try."

[[Connie Springer|Connie]] pressed the switch. The lights went out. He pressed it again. They came back. He did it three more times before he realized he was smiling.

The bathroom was through a door at the back of the room. It was smaller than any room he had ever been in — a closet with a toilet, a sink, and a nozzle set into the wall above a drain. There was no bucket. No basin. No pump.

"This is the shower," DeSilva said. She pointed at a knob on the wall. "Turn this to the left. Water comes out of the ceiling. Hot water. From a tank. You don't need to heat it first."

[[Connie Springer|Connie]] stared at the nozzle. "Water comes out of the ceiling."

"Yes."

"Hot water."

"Yes."

"From a tank I cannot see."

"It's in the hull. You don't need to see it. You just need to turn the knob."

[[Connie Springer|Connie]] did not turn the knob. He was not ready to turn the knob. He was still processing the light switch. He looked at the toilet — a white porcelain bowl with water already in it, a lever on the side. "And this?"

"Toilet. You sit. You do what you need to do. Then you push the lever. The water carries it away. Pipes in the walls. You don't need to think about it."

He pushed the lever. The water swirled and vanished and refilled itself. He watched it for perhaps ten seconds. Then he looked at DeSilva.

"Your world has solved waste disposal."

DeSilva laughed. It was the first time he had heard her laugh — a short, surprised sound, the laugh of someone who had not expected a Paradisian soldier's first observation about her civilisation to be about toilets. "We've solved a few things. Showers. Lights. Waste disposal. We're still working on the rest."

She showed him the bed — a mattress on a metal frame, sheets that were not wool, a pillow that was not straw. She showed him the locker where he could store his clothes. She showed him the call button — a red button on the wall that would summon help if he needed it. She demonstrated it by pressing it, and a voice came out of a grille in the ceiling — *"Bridge. Everything okay?"* — and [[Connie Springer|Connie]] nearly fell off the cot.

"Voices," he said. "In the ceiling."

"Intercom. It's like a telephone. Do you know what a telephone is?"

"No."

"I'll show you tomorrow. Sleep first. You look like you've been awake for three days."

"I have been awake for three days."

She nodded, the nod of someone who understood what exhaustion did to a soldier. "Then sleep. I'll be back at six hundred. That's morning. You'll hear a bell. Or a buzzer. Or someone shouting in Korean. You get used to it."

She left. The lights stayed on — warm, steady, electric. [[Connie Springer|Connie]] lay on the cot in his strange jacket, in a room that hummed, on a ship that was carrying him toward a continent he had not known existed four hours earlier. He reached up and pressed the light switch. Darkness. He pressed it again. Light. Darkness. Light.

He left the lights on.

He was not afraid of the dark. He was a soldier. He had fought Titans in the dark. But the dark on this ship was different — it was a dark full of machines he could not name, and he wanted to be able to see them if they moved.

They did not move. He slept.

### Scene 3: Vogler's Debrief — Day 105–106

The ship's mess was a long room with fixed tables and a serving counter where the crew lined up with trays. The food was strange — rice that had been cooked in a machine, meat that had been sealed in a package and heated, vegetables that were too uniform to have been grown in soil — but it was hot and it was free and [[Connie Springer|Connie]] ate two portions before he remembered that he was supposed to be representing Paradis.

[[Elias Vogler|Vogler]] sat across from him on the second day of transit. The scientist looked tired — he had been awake through the rendezvous, the helicopter flight, the shipboard debriefings with Tanaka and Ahn, and the quiet diplomatic labour of keeping four nations from second-guessing the agreement they had signed in a dark field. But his voice was calm. His Marleyan/Eldian was fluid. And he had brought coffee — real coffee, from a machine that ground beans and forced steam through them — which he pushed across the table toward [[Connie Springer|Connie]].

"This is coffee. From my world. It is better than the coffee on yours."

[[Connie Springer|Connie]] drank it. It was bitter and hot and it made his heartbeat feel like it was happening in his temples. 

He leaned back. His tone shifted — not formal, but focused. The debriefing, [[Connie Springer|Connie]] realized, had been happening since the coffee arrived. It was just dressed as a conversation.

"How much do you know about what my coalition wants from Paradis?"

"What you told Commander Hange. Stability. Security. Research. Trade." [[Connie Springer|Connie]] recited the words carefully. He had been paying attention at the rendezvous, even when it looked like he was just standing behind [[Levi Ackerman|Levi]] and trying not to fall off his horse.

"That is the public version. The version we gave in the field. I am going to give you the version we give ourselves. The version that the committee in Seoul and Beijing and Washington and Tokyo is reading this week." [[Elias Vogler|Vogler]] set down his coffee. "We are afraid of the Rumbling. We are more afraid of it than we are of Marley, of the Mid-East war, of anything else on your world. The Colossal Titans in your Walls could destroy the planet. We have counted them. We have mapped them. And we know the activation mechanism is a fifteen-year-old boy who has just been told that everyone outside his island wants his people dead."

[[Connie Springer|Connie]] said nothing. He was thinking about Eren — about the look on Eren's face when they reached the basement, about the silence that had followed, about the weeks since when Eren had spoken less and stared more. He was thinking about the way Hange had said *Eren stays at headquarters* during the council, and the way no one had argued.

"Our first priority is to prevent that activation," [[Elias Vogler|Vogler]] continued. "Not by killing the Founder. Not by invading Paradis. By building a relationship that makes the Rumbling unnecessary. If Paradis has allies — real allies, with technology and resources and a voice in our world's counsels — then Eren Yeager never needs to use the Founding Titan as a weapon. He can use it as a deterrent, and a deterrent is a weapon that never fires."

"That's the logic," [[Connie Springer|Connie]] said. "What's the fear?"

"The fear is that logic collapses. That the Founder destabilises. That Marley invades before the relationship solidifies. That one of our nations decides that a pre-emptive strike is better than a diplomatic gamble. That something we cannot predict happens, and the Rumbling activates, and half a million Colossal Titans march across the planet, and the extinction of the human species is on the hands of everyone who had the power to stop it and didn't."

[[Connie Springer|Connie]] looked at his coffee. It had gone cold. He drank it anyway.

"You know Eren," [[Elias Vogler|Vogler]] said. It was not a question.

"For five years. Since training. He's — " [[Connie Springer|Connie]] stopped. He did not know how to describe Eren to an alien scientist. Angry? Devoted? Broken? A person who had watched his mother die and then spent five years killing and then learned that the world wanted to finish the job? "He's been through a lot. We all have. He's still here."

"That is not the same as stable."

"No," [[Connie Springer|Connie]] said. He met [[Elias Vogler|Vogler]]'s eyes. "It's not."

The conversation moved on — to Marleyan troop dispositions, to the Volunteers, to the railway and the coastal batteries and the state of Paradisian agriculture. [[Connie Springer|Connie]] answered what he could. He deferred what he could not. He did not mention his mother. That question was still forming — a shape in his chest that had not yet become words — and he was not ready to give it to a man he had met four hours ago.

When the debriefing ended, [[Elias Vogler|Vogler]] stood. "Thank you, Private Springer. You have been more helpful than you know."

"Dr. Vogler," [[Connie Springer|Connie]] said. "The thing you're afraid of. The Rumbling. The extinction. If you're wrong — if the logic works, if Eren stays stable, if the relationship holds — what does Paradis get?"

[[Elias Vogler|Vogler]] considered this. "Everything," he said. "Everything our world can give you. And everything you were never supposed to have."

### Scene 4: The Engineers — Day 106–107

The Eldian engineers worked in the Isabu's machinery spaces — six men and two women in CATF-issued coveralls, their hands marked with grease and their speech marked with the flat, careful accent of Liberio's internment zone. [[Connie Springer|Connie]] found them on the third day, following the sound of voices that were speaking his language with a different cadence.

[[Frieda]] was the one who noticed him first. She was crouched beside a turbine housing, a wrench in one hand and a diagnostic tablet in the other — a CATF device that she had learned to read in three weeks, because she had never been allowed to learn anything that fast in Liberio and she was making up for lost time. She looked up at [[Connie Springer|Connie]] with the expression of a mechanic who had been interrupted and was calculating whether the interruption was worth the delay.

"You're the Paradisian," she said. Her voice was flat. Not hostile. Just... flat. The voice of someone who had learned to give nothing away.

"You're the Liberian," [[Connie Springer|Connie]] said. "I mean — Liberio. From the internment zone."

"Liberio. The zone. The cage on the mainland." She stood, wiping her hands on a rag. "What do you want to know? Whether we have tails? Whether we eat humans? Whether the armbands hurt?"

"No. I want to know what it's like. To grow up outside the Walls."

[[Frieda]] looked at him for a long moment. Then she laughed — a short, sharp sound with no humour in it. "You grew up inside Walls. Three Walls. Concentric. Fifty metres high. And you want to know what it's like to grow up in a zone."

"Different kind of cage," [[Connie Springer|Connie]] said.

"Different kind of cage." She repeated the words as though weighing them. "In Liberio, we had no Walls. We had guards. Checkpoints. Curfews. Quarterly loyalty tests. If you failed a loyalty test, you were transferred to the Titan Security Division. Nobody ever came back from the Titan Security Division. But we had the sky." She looked up at the ceiling of the machinery space, as though she could see through it. "You had Walls. We had the sky. I don't know which is worse."

"The Walls kept Titans out."

"The guards kept us in. Same function. Different architecture." She returned her attention to the turbine. "You're the first Paradisian I've met. I thought you'd be different."

"Different how?"

"I don't know. Taller. More monstrous. The Marleyan propaganda said you were devils — the true Eldians, the ones who escaped the empire's justice and fled behind indestructible Walls to plot humanity's destruction. I didn't believe it. But when you've been told something since childhood, you still expect it. In your bones. In the part of you that doesn't listen to reason."

"And now?"

[[Frieda]] looked at him — a short Paradisian soldier with a shaved head and a face that had not yet learned to hide anything. "Now you look like a person. Which is disappointing. I was hoping for a devil. A devil would be easier to understand."

[[Connie Springer|Connie]] sat on an empty crate. The turbine hummed. The other engineers worked around them, pretending not to listen. "What do you do here?"

"I fix things. They have machines that do the work of fifty men, and the machines break, and I fix them. They pay me. They give me quarters. They let me study gas turbines at night. They don't call me a devil. They don't make me wear an armband. I don't know if I trust them. I don't know if I trust anyone. But I trust the work. The work is real."

"That's how I feel about the Survey Corps," [[Connie Springer|Connie]] said. "I don't trust the government. I don't trust the world. But I trust the soldiers next to me. They're real."

[[Frieda]] was silent for a moment. Then she handed him the rag. "You want to help? The turbine needs a filter change. It's not complicated. You just need hands."

[[Connie Springer|Connie]] took the rag. He did not know what a turbine filter was. He did not know what a gas turbine was. But he had hands, and he had spent five years learning to do things he didn't understand because they needed doing.

"Show me," he said.

### Scene 5: Arrival at Grande Terre — Day 108

The Isabu rounded the volcanic headland at dawn, and [[Connie Springer|Connie]] saw the base.

It was not a city. It was not a port. It was a grid of shipping containers and prefab modules and fuel bladders spread across a plateau of grey volcanic rock, studded with lights that burned against the grey sky, connected by roads of crushed gravel that wound down to a deep-water harbour where four warships rode at anchor. The ships were grey. Their lines were sharp — sharper than any Marleyan cruiser, sharper than any vessel [[Connie Springer|Connie]] had ever seen. Their guns were not gun turrets. Their bridges were not open to the wind. They looked like weapons that had been designed by people who had stopped thinking of war as noble and started thinking of it as efficient.

"That's the fleet," DeSilva said. She had joined him at the rail an hour earlier, wrapped in a cold-weather coat, her breath steaming. "Four destroyers. One from each CATF nation. Japan, Korea, China, America. And the big one at the deep-water berth — that's the Tripoli. Amphibious assault ship. It carried the Marines who captured the Titan."

[[Connie Springer|Connie]] looked at the Tripoli. It was the largest ship in the harbour, a grey slab of a vessel with a flat deck and a superstructure like a fortress. It had captured a Titan. It had sailed to Paradis, taken one of the monsters that had killed his people for a century, and sailed back. And now it sat at anchor, quiet, as though this were normal.

"How many people live here?" he asked.

"About two thousand. More rotating through. The base is still growing. Commander Rivera — he's the engineer in charge — says they'll have permanent barracks by the end of the year. Right now it's mostly shipping containers and prefabs. But it works."

The Isabu docked at a modular pier that jutted into the harbour on pontoons. The gangway was metal. The air was cold — subantarctic autumn, the wind off the grey water cutting through the thermal jacket DeSilva had given him. [[Connie Springer|Connie]] walked down the gangway onto the volcanic rock of Grande Terre, and for the first time since the helicopter had lifted off the Paradisian grassland, he was standing on solid ground that was not his world.

### Scene 6: The Room — Day 108, Afternoon

The room was his alone.

It was larger than the dorm on the Isabu — a single bed, a desk, a wardrobe, an attached bathroom with a shower and a toilet and a sink with running water. A staff member — a Korean petty officer with careful Eldian and the patient manner of someone who had given this tour before — walked him through every feature.

"Light switch. On. Off. You remember from the ship."

"Shower. Hot water from this knob. Cold water from this one. The temperature is adjustable. Try not to burn yourself."

"Heater. The base is cold. This dial controls the temperature in your room. Turn it to the right for warmer. The staff recommend twenty-two degrees."

"Toilet. Same as the ship. Push the lever. The water carries everything through pipes to a treatment facility. Do not put anything except toilet paper in the toilet."

"Window blinds. The sun is bright in the mornings. Pull the cord to close them."

[[Connie Springer|Connie]] listened. He nodded. He asked questions — where does the water come from? (The desalination plant.) Where does the waste go? (The treatment facility.) How does the heater work without a fire? (Electricity from the base generators.) Each answer was a door opening onto another room of questions, and the petty officer answered them all with the patience of someone who had been trained to expect exactly this.

The petty officer left. [[Connie Springer|Connie]] stood alone in his room — the first Paradisian to have a private room on a CATF base — and touched the light switch. On. Off. On. Off. He turned the shower knob. Hot water came out. He flushed the toilet and watched the water swirl away. He opened and closed the blinds. He adjusted the heater.

Then he sat on the bed and did nothing for a long time.

The reading material was on the desk — a slim booklet translated to Eldian. It was titled *The Combined Anomaly Task Force: A Briefing for Liaison Personnel*. It described the four nations. The Gate. The mission. The command structure. The science division. The rules of engagement. It was written in clear, careful prose, as though the authors had understood that their reader was from a pre-industrial civilisation and had decided not to condescend.

[[Connie Springer|Connie]] read the first three pages. Then he closed the book. There was too much. He would read it later. For now, he just wanted to sit in a room that was his, with a bed that was his, on a base that belonged to people who had decided — for reasons he did not fully understand — not to kill him.

### Scene 7: The First Meal — Day 108, Evening

The meal arrived on a tray.

It was delivered by a CATF orderly who knocked twice, entered at [[Connie Springer|Connie]]'s invitation, placed the tray on the desk, and left without comment. The tray was brown plastic divided into compartments. In one compartment: rice, white and steaming. In another: chicken in a brown sauce, with vegetables that were too brightly coloured to be natural. In a third: something that might have been a bread roll, if bread were soft and sweet. A plastic fork. A paper napkin. A cup of water.

[[Connie Springer|Connie]] stared at the tray. He picked up the fork — lighter than any fork he had ever held, perfectly smooth, with no trace of the blacksmith's hammer. He ate the rice. It was the best rice he had ever tasted. He ate the chicken. It was tender and hot and tasted of spices he could not name. He ate the bread roll. It was sweet, inexplicably sweet, and he ate it in three bites before his brain caught up with the sugar.

He was sitting on the bed, eating a meal from another world, wearing clothes from another world, in a room that was heated by electricity from a generator he could not see. Outside the window, the four destroyers rode at anchor in the grey harbour. Somewhere on this base was a Titan in a cage. Somewhere south of this island was a Gate that led to a world he had never seen. And he was eating rice.

He finished the meal. He stacked the tray neatly on the desk — the way he had learned to stack his gear in the Survey Corps, each item aligned — and sat on the bed and waited for whatever happened next.

What happened next was sleep. He collapsed onto the mattress at nine in the evening, the heater humming, the blinds drawn against the subantarctic darkness, and slept the way a soldier sleeps when the body finally trusts the ground.

### Scene 8: Night Wander — Night of Day 108, 3 AM

He woke at three in the morning.

Not gradually. Not with the slow surfacing of a mind leaving dreams. He woke all at once — eyes open, body still, brain alert — the way a soldier woke when something had changed. Nothing had changed. The heater was humming. The blinds were drawn. The room was warm and quiet and safe. But his body did not know this. His body knew that he was eight thousand kilometres from Paradis in a building full of strangers on a world that was not his world, and his body had decided that three in the morning was the appropriate time to remind him of this.

He lay still for ten minutes. Then he stood.

The hallway outside his room was lit by dim panels set into the ceiling — emergency lighting, a permanent twilight that cast no shadows. The doors along the corridor were numbered. Twelve rooms. Most of them closed. One of them, at the far end, was open — a faint glow spilling onto the corridor floor.

[[Connie Springer|Connie]] walked toward it.

The common room was a rectangle of soft furniture and low tables, lit by a single lamp on a side table. Two sofas faced each other across a coffee table. A bookshelf held paperbacks in languages he could not read. A vending machine glowed in the corner — red and white, its glass front revealing rows of packaged snacks and aluminium cans. It hummed. Everything on this base hummed.

He sat on one of the sofas. The fabric was soft — thinner than the wool blankets on Paradis, smoother than cotton. He leaned back. The sofa accepted him. He had never sat on furniture that was designed to be sat on. The chairs in the Survey Corps mess were wood. The benches were wood. The beds were cots. This was a machine for sitting, disguised as a cushion.

He explored. The bookshelf — he pulled out a volume at random, opened it to a page of English text, understood nothing, put it back. The vending machine — he pressed a button through the glass, and nothing happened, because the machine required money and he had none. The window — he parted the blinds and looked out at the harbour, where the destroyers floated in their own lights, silent and watchful, their hulls blurred by the cold mist rising from the water.

He stood at the window for a long time. The base was asleep. The world was asleep. He was the only person awake on an island that belonged to another Earth, and the silence was the deepest he had ever heard.

At four in the morning, he walked back to his room. He closed the door. He climbed onto the bed and lay staring at the ceiling until the heater's hum lulled him under.

### Scene 9: The Jog — Day 109, 6 AM

The knock came at six.

[[Connie Springer|Connie]] opened the door. DeSilva was standing in the hallway, dressed in a thin, close-fitting garment that covered her from neck to ankle — black fabric with a high collar and a texture like the thermal jacket she had given him on the ship. She was holding a second outfit, identical to the first, folded over her arm.

"Morning. You run on Paradis, right?"

"Sometimes. When there's time. When there's ground."

"There's ground here. And it's cold, but I've got something for that." She handed him the folded outfit. "Thermal exercise gear. It traps heat. You wear it under your clothes, or instead of your clothes. Today, instead of. Put it on. I'll wait."

She waited in the hallway. [[Connie Springer|Connie]] examined the garment. It was light — impossibly light, lighter than any fabric he had ever touched — and it stretched when he pulled it, and when he put it on it fit against his skin like a second layer of warmth. He had never worn anything like it.

He stepped into the hallway. DeSilva looked him up and down. "Good. Now come here." She led him back into the common room, where a small box sat on the coffee table. She opened it. Inside was a black band with a glass face — a watch, but not a watch. The face was blank, dark, waiting.

"Wrist," she said.

He held out his wrist. She strapped the band onto it — snug, not tight, the material gripping his skin without pinching. The face lit up. Numbers appeared. A heartbeat symbol. A number: 72.

"This is a Garmin. It's a device — a machine — that tracks your body while you run. It counts your heartbeats. It measures how far you go. How fast you run. How many steps you take. It tells you everything your body is doing while you're doing it."

[[Connie Springer|Connie]] stared at the Garmin. The number 72 pulsed. His heart. The machine could see his heart. "Why do you need to know all that?"

"Because if you know what your body is doing, you can make it do more." She tapped her own wrist, where an identical band glowed. "It's not for combat. It's for improvement. To be better than you were yesterday. It's a game, kind of. A private game against yourself."

"It's a loan, by the way," she added. "Not a gift. You give it back before you get on the helicopter. Standard inventory." She said it lightly — the lightness of someone who was saying it aloud for the record, not because she expected him to try to keep it.

[[Connie Springer|Connie]] nodded. He understood loan. He understood that the device on his wrist was a piece of equipment, not a possession. But he also understood that he was going to ask to keep it, before the helicopter came, and that asking was the kind of thing that happened when a soldier met a tool that fit.

"Your world has games for exercise."

"Our world has games for everything. It's exhausting." She smiled — the second time he had heard her laugh, and this time it was genuine. "Come on. Let's run."

The path followed the harbour perimeter — crushed volcanic gravel underfoot, the grey water on the left, the fuel farms and the command complex on the right. The wind was sharp, subantarctic, cutting through the thermal gear in a way that made [[Connie Springer|Connie]] grateful for every thread. They ran at a steady pace — not fast, not slow, the pace of soldiers who were maintaining rather than training.

The Garmin beeped after ten minutes. A number appeared: 1.6 kilometres. Another number: 148 heartbeats per minute. [[Connie Springer|Connie]] looked at the band, at the numbers, at the machine on his wrist that knew more about his body than he did. He had never known his heart rate before. He had never needed to.

Ahead, the path curved around the fuel farm toward the helipad, where two MH-60S Seahawks sat under canvas covers, their rotors folded, their fuselages dark. DeSilva pointed. "Those are the workhorses. Different from the H125 — bigger, heavier, for cargo and transport. They're what we use to move supplies between the base and the ships. The H125s that brought you in are light utility — smaller, faster, for personnel and recon."

"What are the H125s doing now?"

"Maintenance. Refuelling. They'll be ready for the second rendezvous." She glanced at him. "You're going back for that, right? To meet the scouts?"

"Yes," [[Connie Springer|Connie]] said. He had not thought about the second rendezvous. He had been too busy processing the lights and the shower and the chicken in brown sauce. "In — " he counted the days, " — three more days. Day 111."

"Same grassland?"

"Same grassland."

She nodded. They ran in silence for another kilometre. The Garmin beeped again. 3.2 kilometres. Heart rate: 152.

When they returned to the barracks, DeSilva pointed him toward his room. "Shower. Hot water. Take as long as you want. The tank doesn't run out."

[[Connie Springer|Connie]] stood under the shower for ten minutes, letting the hot water beat against his shoulders, watching the steam rise and cloud the mirror. The Garmin was still on his wrist. He had forgotten to return it — DeSilva had said it was a loan, and he had meant to give it back, but somewhere between the run and the shower the intention had dissolved into the fog of a morning that had already overflowed with new things. The face was dark now — resting, waiting for him to move. He looked at it for a long time. Then he took it off, dried it carefully, and placed it on the desk beside the reading booklet, where it waited for the moment he would either return it or ask to keep it.

He would return it later. For now, he just wanted to look at it — a machine from another world that had counted his heart, and counted it well.

### Scene 10: The Briefing — Day 109, Late Morning

The conference room was in the command complex — a converted shipping container with a table, six chairs, a water jug, and a screen that was currently dark. Three people sat across from [[Connie Springer|Connie]]: [[Tanaka Kenji|Lieutenant Colonel Tanaka]] at the centre, his notepad open and his pen ready; Dr. [[Ahn Jae-hyun]] to his right, his glasses pushed up on his forehead and his expression the careful blankness of a scientist who was not yet sure whether this was an interview or an examination; and DeSilva to [[Connie Springer|Connie]]'s left, not as an interrogator but as a presence — someone on his side of the table.

[[Tanaka Kenji|Tanaka]] spoke first. His Eldian was functional — less fluid than Vogler's, more deliberate, the words chosen and placed like pieces on a board. "Private Springer. Thank you for agreeing to this. This is not an interrogation. It is an information exchange. We will ask you questions. You will answer what you can. You will ask us questions. We will answer what we can. Is that acceptable?"

"Yes."

"Good." [[Tanaka Kenji|Tanaka]] opened his notepad. "I will begin with structure. The Survey Corps — how many active soldiers?"

"About two hundred. Maybe more with the recent recruits. We've been building up since Shiganshina. Everyone's training. Even the Garrison is training."

"Command structure. Commander Hange. Below her?"

"Captain Levi. Squad leaders — I'm one. Jean Kirstein. A few others. Mikasa Ackerman is — " he paused, searching for the word, " — she's not a squad leader. She's more than that. Less than Levi. She fights."

[[Tanaka Kenji|Tanaka]] made a note. "The Volunteers. Yelena. Onyankopon. How integrated are they with your operations?"

"They've been with us for a couple of months. They brought ships, weapons, intelligence about Marley. They eat with us. They train with us. But they're not Survey Corps. They're — " he paused. "They're themselves. Their own thing. Hange trusts them. I don't know if Levi does."

Another note. "Eldian population centres. What is the largest city inside the Walls?"

"Mitras. Wall Sina. The capital. That's where the Queen is. And the Premier — Zachary. The government."

"Population?"

"I don't know. Hundreds of thousands. I've never counted." He paused. "I'm a soldier. I don't do population counts. I fight."

[[Tanaka Kenji|Tanaka]] nodded. The questions continued — supply lines, agricultural output, iceburst stone extraction rates, the railway completion timeline, the status of the captured Marleyan cruiser, the Thunder Spear production capacity. [[Connie Springer|Connie]] answered what he could. He was not a strategist. He was not a logistics officer. He was a soldier who had spent five years killing Titans and one week learning about a world that had solved toilets. But he answered honestly — more honestly than a strategist might have — and [[Tanaka Kenji|Tanaka]] wrote everything down.

Then Dr. [[Ahn Jae-hyun|Ahn]] leaned forward. "Private Springer. You have fought Titans. Many Titans. Can you describe your experience with them? Close-range. What you saw. What you felt."

[[Connie Springer|Connie]] looked at the biologist. The question was simple. The answer was not.

"They're big," he said. "They're stupid. They move like they don't know where their limbs are. But they're fast — faster than they look. And they don't stop. You cut off an arm, it grows back. You cut off a leg, they crawl. You think you've killed one, and another one comes from behind a building. For five years, that was my life. Cutting things that grew back."

"And the nape. The killing blow. You learned to target the nape."

"We all learned. First thing they teach you in training. The nape is the only thing that kills them. Everything else is just delay."

Dr. [[Ahn Jae-hyun|Ahn]] made a note. "The Titans you fought. Were they — " he paused, selecting his words, " — did they appear to have any awareness? Any recognition? Any sign that they understood what they were doing or who they had been?"

[[Connie Springer|Connie]] was silent for a long moment. He was thinking about his mother. The Titan in Ragako. The face frozen mid-transformation in the ruins of his childhood home. The question he had been holding since the basement — since they learned that Titans were people — and had never asked aloud.

"No," he said. His voice was quieter than before. "They don't remember. They don't know. They just eat. That's all they do. They eat until you kill them, or they kill you."

Dr. [[Ahn Jae-hyun|Ahn]] wrote this down. He did not ask another question.

[[Connie Springer|Connie]] spoke into the silence. "Can you turn them back?"

[[Ahn Jae-hyun|Dr. Ahn]] looked at [[Tanaka Kenji|Tanaka]]. [[Tanaka Kenji|Tanaka]] looked at [[Ahn Jae-hyun|Dr. Ahn]]. The silence stretched.

"We don't know," [[Ahn Jae-hyun|Dr. Ahn]] said. "The transformation mechanism — the Ex Nihilo mass creation, the spinal fluid cascade, the Path connection — is beyond our current physics. We are studying it. We have a live Titan in containment. We are trying to understand the process. Whether it can be reversed — " he paused. "We hope so. We do not know."

"You have a Titan in a cage."

"Yes."

"Was it a person? Before?"

"Yes."

[[Connie Springer|Connie]] nodded. He had expected this answer. He had not expected it to feel like a door closing.

"Keep trying," he said.

"We will."

The briefing ended. [[Tanaka Kenji|Tanaka]] closed his notepad. "Thank you, Private Springer. You have been more helpful than you know."

[[Connie Springer|Connie]] stood. He did not ask what the lieutenant colonel meant by that. He was not sure he wanted to know.

### Scene 11: Tour of the Facility — Day 109, Afternoon

[[Elias Vogler|Vogler]] led the tour.

The command complex — a grid of shipping containers linked by covered walkways, the walls lined with screens and maps and the quiet hum of electronics. The operations centre — a darkened room where analysts tracked drone feeds and acoustic signatures and the slow, steady pulse of the AOT world's maritime traffic. The communication suite — a room of consoles and headsets where operators maintained contact with the four destroyers and the Isabu and the distant, unseen Gate.

[[Connie Springer|Connie]] followed. He listened. He asked questions — what does this screen show? (Maritime traffic. Ships. Their courses and speeds.) How far can you see? (From drones? Two thousand kilometres. From hydrophones? Two hundred.) Is Paradis on the screen? (Not now. But it was, before the rendezvous.)

The tour wound through the fuel farm, the pontoon causeway, the helipad where the H125s sat under maintenance tarps, the medical bay where a Korean doctor was reviewing charts and a Chinese nurse was organising supplies. Everything was clean. Everything was organised. Everything hummed.

And then the containment pen.

It was at the edge of the base — a converted shipping container with the roof removed, a steel cage around the opening, a light-control system rigged to blackout curtains, and a remote camera array that fed data to the biological analysis division. The Titan inside was six metres tall, chained at the shoulders and pelvis, its limbs bound by industrial cargo straps. It was dormant — the shroud had been retracted to allow the tour, but the creature had not yet stirred.

[[Connie Springer|Connie]] stopped at the edge of the containment zone. He had seen Titans before. He had killed fifteen of them across five years of service. He knew the proportions — the too-large head, the too-small limbs, the blank face that was almost human and entirely wrong. But he had never seen one in a cage. He had never seen one that was being *studied*.

"It was a person," he said. "Before. You said it was a person."

"A Subject of Ymir," [[Elias Vogler|Vogler]] said. "From Paradis. Transformed by Marleyan spinal fluid injection, then abandoned in the northern wilderness during the Warrior Unit's initial operation. We identified the genetic markers. Eldian. Female. Approximately thirty years old at transformation."

"She's been like this for — "

"Five years. Possibly longer. The transformation arrests biological ageing. The Titan state is metabolically stable. As long as sunlight reaches the specimen, it remains in its current form."

[[Connie Springer|Connie]] stared at the face. It was slack. Empty. The mouth was slightly open, the jaw working faintly even in dormancy. The hands — massive, disproportionate — were curled against the chains. He could see the stumps of regenerated fingers, the steam still rising from wounds that had closed weeks ago and were still healing, the body's endless, mindless drive to repair.

"Can you hear me?" he said. He said it in Eldian. The Titan did not respond. It did not know his language. It did not know anything.

"This is what happens," [[Connie Springer|Connie]] said. He was not asking a question. "To anyone. To everyone. One injection. This."

"Yes."

"My mother is a Titan." The words came out before he could stop them. They were quiet — the quiet of a soldier admitting something he had never said aloud to anyone except the walls of his own skull. "In Ragako. Wall Rose. The Beast Titan turned the whole village. My whole family. They're Titans. Still. In the ruins."

[[Elias Vogler|Vogler]] said nothing. There was nothing to say. The silence was full.

"Dr. Ahn said you don't know if you can turn them back."

"We don't know. The mechanism — the Ex Nihilo creation, the Path connection — we are trying to understand it. But we are months from a theory and years from a solution. If a solution exists at all."

[[Connie Springer|Connie]] looked at the chained Titan. At the face that had been a face. At the hands that had been hands. At the body that had been a woman, and was now a specimen.

"Keep trying," he said. He had said it twice now — once in the briefing room, once at the cage — and he would keep saying it until someone told him the answer was yes or the answer was never. "Just keep trying."

### Scene 12: The Report — Day 110

The desk was metal. The paper was white. The pen was a ballpoint — the same model that [[Hange Zoë|Hange]] had touched in the mess on Paradis, the same tiny machine that had fascinated the Commander of the Survey Corps and started a writing lesson on a wooden tabletop.

[[Connie Springer|Connie]] wrote in Eldian. He wrote slowly — he was not a writer, had never been a writer, had spent his life fighting things that did not read — but he wrote clearly. The letter was for [[Hange Zoë|Hange]]. He did not know how to send it. He would give it to [[Elias Vogler|Vogler]] before the helicopters returned for the second rendezvous. If they returned.

The letter said:

Commander Hange.

I am on the base. It is called Grande Terre. It is an island south of Paradis, near the Gate. The people here are from a world called Earth. There are four nations — Korea, China, Japan, America. They have a fleet. They have flying machines. They have a Titan in a cage.

The Titan was a person. A woman from Paradis. She was turned five years ago by Marleyan soldiers. She has been a Titan for five years. They are studying her. They do not know if they can turn her back.

The people here are not hostile. They ask a lot of questions. They want to understand Paradis. They want to prevent the Rumbling. They want iceburst stone in exchange for technology and medicine. Dr. Vogler says the deal is real. I believe him.

I met an Eldian from Liberio. Her name is Frieda. She grew up in the internment zone. She is not a devil. She is a mechanic. She fixed a turbine while I watched. She told me the Marleyan guards made her people wear armbands. She told me she had never been free until she came here. I told her I had never been free until the Walls came down. We did not know what to do with that. So she handed me a wrench and I helped her change a filter.

I went jogging this morning. They have a machine that counts your heartbeats. They have showers that make hot water from the ceiling. They have toilets that carry waste through pipes in the walls. The food is in plastic trays and tastes better than anything I have eaten in a year.

I asked the biologist if they could turn a Titan back. He said he did not know. He said they were trying.

I am coming back for the second rendezvous. I will tell you everything. But I want you to know, before I come back: I think they are telling the truth. About the Rumbling. About the trade. About wanting to help. I am not a strategist. I am not Armin. But I have been here for a week, and I have watched them, and I think they are telling the truth.

Tell Jean I said hello. Tell Mikasa I said nothing, so she doesn't have to respond.

Connie

He folded the letter. He placed it on the desk. Then he sat back and looked out the window at the harbour, where the four destroyers rode at anchor, and the grey water stretched to a horizon that was not Paradis but was, for now, enough.

### Scene 13: The Common Room — Night, Day 110

The base was quiet. An evening hush, the generators humming, the wind off the harbour moving through the covered walkways with a low, steady sound like breathing.

[[Connie Springer|Connie]] found [[Frieda]] in the common room, sitting on the same sofa he had discovered at three in the morning, a metal cup of tea in her hands and her boots up on the coffee table. The vending machine glowed in the corner. The lamp on the side table cast a warm circle of light. The bookshelf stood undisturbed.

"You're up late," he said.

"I am always up late. The turbines run at all hours. The engineers who maintain them do too." She gestured at the sofa opposite. "Sit. You look like you need to not be standing."

He sat. The sofa accepted him the way it had accepted him the night before — completely, without resistance, a machine for sitting that required nothing in return.

"Tea?" She tilted her cup. "There's a kettle in the kitchenette. Hot water from a switch. Another machine. You push a button and water boils."

"I know. DeSilva showed me."

"DeSilva shows everyone everything. She is the most helpful person on this base. It is exhausting." But [[Frieda]] said it without rancour — the fond exasperation of someone who had been helped and resented needing it.

[[Connie Springer|Connie]] went to the kitchenette. He found the kettle — a plastic jug on a base, a switch on the side. He filled it with water from the sink, pressed the switch, and watched water boil in thirty seconds. He made tea from a paper packet labelled in English and Korean. He carried it back to the common room and sat on the opposite sofa.

"How long have you been here?" he asked.

"Since the Pelikan was captured. Two months. I was an engineer on a Marleyan polar expedition. The ship got lost. The navigation failed. The stars were wrong — we had drifted into waters no Marleyan chart had ever touched, waters where the constellations belonged to a different sky entirely." She paused. "And then we found out about the other Earth. The inverted sky. That explained a lot — and nothing at all."

"I haven't seen it. The sky. It was dark when we flew. And Grande Terre is — "

"Cloudy. Always cloudy. The southern ocean is a grey blanket for nine months of the year. You'll see the stars eventually. They look wrong. Everything looks wrong. You get used to it. You get used to everything, eventually."

[[Connie Springer|Connie]] drank his tea. It was weak — the paper packet had not steeped long enough — but it was hot, and the cup was warm in his hands, and the common room was quiet, and for the first time since the helicopter had lifted off the Paradisian grassland, he was not thinking about what he was supposed to be doing.

"In Liberio," [[Frieda]] said, "we were told that the Eldians on Paradis were devils. The true devils. The original sin. The ones who escaped punishment and hid behind Walls to plot the destruction of the world. Every child learned this. Every adult believed it. And then I met you, and you asked me what it was like to grow up outside the Walls, and I realized the propaganda worked both ways."

"Paradis didn't have propaganda," [[Connie Springer|Connie]] said. "We didn't know the outside world existed. We thought humanity was extinct. Everyone else was dead. We were the last."

"That's worse," [[Frieda]] said. "At least we knew we were hated. You just thought you were alone."

The silence that followed was the silence of two people who had been told, their whole lives, that they were monsters — and had just met someone else who had been told the same thing.

"My mother is a Titan," [[Connie Springer|Connie]] said. "In Ragako. Wall Rose. The Beast Titan turned the whole village. I saw her face. It was frozen. Mid-transformation. She's still there. In the ruins of our house."

[[Frieda]] did not say she was sorry. She was from Liberio. Sorry was a word that meant nothing. She just looked at him, and her expression shifted — a small shift, almost imperceptible, the kind of shift that meant she had just recalculated something about the person in front of her.

"In Liberio," she said, "when someone failed a loyalty test, they were transferred to the Titan Security Division. No one came back. But everyone knew what happened. They were injected. They were turned. They were deployed. The thing that killed your village — the Titan weapon — was invented in Liberio. It was perfected on Liberian bodies. My people built the weapon. Your people took the hit."

She set down her cup. "We have both been ammunition, Private Springer. The difference is that you are still fighting. I am just fixing engines."

"That's still fighting. Just different."

"Maybe." She looked at him. "You asked the biologist if they could turn a Titan back."

"Yes."

"What did he say?"

"He said he didn't know. He said they were trying."

[[Frieda]] nodded slowly. "The CATF scientists are the first people I have ever met who did not look at an Eldian and see a weapon. They see a problem. A physics problem. Something to understand. That is better than being a weapon. It is not the same as being a person. But it is better."

"My mother is a physics problem," [[Connie Springer|Connie]] said. "That's — " he stopped. He did not know what that was. Devastating. Hopeful. Neither. Both.

"It is a start," [[Frieda]] said. "Physics problems have solutions. Weapons have targets. Which would you rather your mother be?"

[[Connie Springer|Connie]] looked at his tea. The cup was half empty. The liquid was cold. He drank it anyway.

"A problem," he said. "I'd rather she be a problem."

"Then stay here. Let them study her. Let them try. You are the first Paradisian they have ever met — the first friendly face from the island they have been afraid of for months. Your presence here is worth more than any report. You are proof. Proof that Paradisians are people. Proof that the Walls held humans, not devils. Proof that the Rumbling is not inevitable."

"I'm not proof," [[Connie Springer|Connie]] said. "I'm just a soldier."

"All proof is just a soldier," [[Frieda]] said. "Someone who was there. Someone who saw. Someone who lived. You lived. That is enough."

The wind moved through the covered walkways. The generators hummed. The vending machine glowed in the corner — red, white, patient. [[Connie Springer|Connie]] sat on the sofa, his tea cold in his hands, and thought about his mother — a Titan in the ruins of Ragako, frozen and waiting — and about the biologist who had said *we do not know* and the mechanic who had said *physics problems have solutions* and the helicopter that would come back in three days to carry him home.

He would tell Hange everything. He would tell her about the food and the showers and the machine that counted heartbeats and the Titan in the cage and the woman from Liberio who had handed him a wrench. He would tell her that the people from Earth were afraid, and the fear was real, and the fear was not of Paradis. It was of what Paradis was carrying inside its Walls.

And he would tell her that he wanted to come back. Not as a hostage. Not as a liaison. As a person who had seen the common room at midnight, and drunk tea with a stranger who understood, and decided that this place — cold, alien, humming with machines — was worth returning to.

He finished his tea. He stood. [[Frieda]] was still on her sofa, her boots on the coffee table, her eyes half-closed.

"Goodnight," he said.

"Goodnight, Private Springer."

"Connie."

She opened her eyes. Looked at him. Nodded. "Goodnight, Connie."

He walked back to his room through the quiet hallway, past the numbered doors and the emergency lights and the heater humming in the wall. The room was warm. The blinds were drawn. The bed was soft. He climbed onto it, lay on his back, and looked at the ceiling.

Four more days until the second rendezvous. Four more days until he saw the scouts again — [[Hange Zoë|Hange]] and [[Jean Kirstein|Jean]] and [[Armin Arlert|Armin]] and the others, the faces he had known since training, the soldiers he had fought beside and nearly died beside. He had been gone for a week. It felt like a year. It felt like he had lived an entire second life in seven days, and he was coming back with hands that had touched a light switch and a Garmin and a turbine filter and the cold metal railing of a ship from another world.

His mother was a problem. The scientist had said *we do not know* and the mechanic had said *problems have solutions*. He held onto that. It was not hope. It was something smaller than hope — a door that had not been closed, a question that had not been answered, a thread that had not been cut. It was, for now, enough.

He closed his eyes. The heater hummed. The base slept. The destroyers rode at anchor in the grey harbour, and the Titan in its cage waited for sunlight, and somewhere eight thousand kilometres north, the scouts were riding through the dark toward a second rendezvous that would decide whether the door stayed open.

[[Connie Springer|Connie]] slept.
